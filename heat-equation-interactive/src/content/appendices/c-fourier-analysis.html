Appendix C · Fourier analysis and distributions

When to use this appendix. Use this appendix when a boundary function is extended harmonically, especially when the boundary power is not an ordinary integrable Fourier transform.

This appendix supplies the precise conventions and elementary derivations used in Chapter 6. All transforms here act in \(d\) Euclidean variables; normal height is a separate parameter.

C.1 Schwartz space and tempered distributions

A multi-index \(\beta=(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_d)\) has nonnegative integer entries, \(|\beta|=\sum_j\beta_j\), and \(\partial^\beta=\partial_1^{\beta_1}\cdots\partial_d^{\beta_d}\). Define

\[\mathcal S(\mathbb R^d)=\{f\in C^\infty:\ p_{m,\beta}(f)=\sup_x(1+|x|)^m|\partial^\beta f(x)|<\infty\text{ for all }m\ge0,\beta\}.\]

Convergence means convergence in every displayed seminorm. A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional on this space; equivalently its absolute value on a test is bounded by a constant times a finite sum of these seminorms. We use bilinear distribution pairing \(\langle T,\psi\rangle\) (no complex conjugation). If a locally integrable function has integrable pairing against rapidly decreasing tests with a seminorm bound, it defines such a functional. In particular \(|x|^{-\alpha}\), \(0<\alpha<d\), is locally integrable by polar integration; outside the unit ball it is bounded, so a seminorm with \(m>d\) bounds the tail.

Distributional derivatives obey \(\langle\partial_j T,\psi\rangle=-\langle T,\partial_j\psi\rangle\). For instance, the derivative of the indicator of the positive half-line is \(\delta_0\): integration of the test derivative on the half-line gives its value at zero. Weak convergence in \(\mathcal S'\) means convergence of every such pairing. It is the convergence used for the Gaussian mixture approximants in Lemma 6.3.

C.2 Fourier transform, inversion and Plancherel

Use the unitary convention of (6.3). Differentiation under the integral and integration by parts show that derivatives in frequency correspond to multiplication by coordinate powers and multiplication by frequency powers corresponds to derivatives in space. The resulting bounds by integrals of Schwartz functions prove continuity of \(\mathcal F:\mathcal S\to\mathcal S\).

Derivation of Fourier inversion

For \(f\in\mathcal S\), multiply the inverse integral by \(e^{-\delta|\zeta|^2}\) with \(\delta>0\). Absolute integrability permits Fubini. Gaussian integration gives

\[(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int e^{ix\cdot\zeta}e^{-\delta|\zeta|^2}\widehat f(\zeta)d\zeta=(g^{(d)}_\delta*f)(x),\quad g^{(d)}_\delta(x)=(4\pi\delta)^{-d/2}e^{-|x|^2/(4\delta)}.\]

The right side tends to \(f(x)\) by the approximate-identity argument in §2.3. On the left the Schwartz decay of \(\widehat f\) allows dominated convergence. Thus the inverse is the same unitary integral with the positive sign. The Gaussian integral follows in one variable by squaring the integral and passing to polar coordinates; differentiation in frequency gives its Fourier transform, and products give the d-dimensional formula.

Derivation of Parseval and extension to L²

Insert the inverse transform of \(g\in\mathcal S\) into \(\int f\overline g\). Fubini is justified by \(f,\widehat g\in L^1\); the inner integral is the transform of \(f\). This gives

\[\int f\overline g\,dx=\int \widehat f\,\overline{\widehat g}\,d\zeta.\]

Taking \(g=f\) gives an isometry on Schwartz functions. Density of smooth compactly supported functions in \(L^2\) (truncate and mollify) extends it uniquely by completion; the inverse extends by the same argument, making it unitary. For weak derivatives in \(L^2\), testing the distributional derivative identity gives the multiplier \(i\zeta_j\widehat f\).

C.3 Convolution and distributional transform

For integrable functions the convolution \((k*f)(x)=\int k(x-y)f(y)dy\) is integrable. Fubini and translation give the unitary convolution formula

\[\widehat{k*f}=(2\pi)^{d/2}\widehat k\,\widehat f.\]

The extra factor is essential: the Poisson multiplier is \((2\pi)^{d/2}\widehat{\mathcal P_z}=e^{-z|\zeta|}\). Define \(\langle\mathcal FT,\psi\rangle=\langle T,\mathcal F\psi\rangle\) for tempered distributions; this agrees with the usual transform on Schwartz functions by Fubini with bilinear pairing. Continuity on Schwartz tests ensures that distributional convergence passes through the transform. In this convention \(\mathcal F\delta_0=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\) and \(\mathcal F^{-1}1=(2\pi)^{d/2}\delta_0\).

Multiplying an arbitrary distribution by \(|\zeta|\) is not automatically defined, because this function is not smooth at zero. In §6.4 the density \(a(d,\alpha)|\zeta|^{\alpha-d}\) is locally integrable and of polynomial growth; multiplication by \(|\zeta|\) is ordinary multiplication of functions. At \(\alpha=d-1\) the resulting constant density gives a Dirac mass, explaining the excluded endpoint in the power-to-power formula.

C.4 Gamma and Beta integrals

\[\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}dt,\qquad B(a,b)=\int_0^1 t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt\quad(a,b>0).\]

Integration by parts gives \(\Gamma(a+1)=a\Gamma(a)\), and substitution gives \(\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-ct}dt=c^{-a}\Gamma(a)\) for \(c>0\). With \(t=r^2\) and the Gaussian integral, \(\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi\). In the product \(\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\), change variables from \((s,t)\in(0,\infty)^2\) to \(R=s+t\), \(v=s/R\); the Jacobian is \(R\), so the product is \(\Gamma(a+b)B(a,b)\). The same polar-coordinate Gaussian integral gives \(|S^{d-1}|=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)\). These identities give the normalization of the Poisson kernel and the constants in (6.7) and (6.9).

Why the beta integral is strictly log-convex in its first argument

For \(0<\theta<1\), Hölder's inequality bounds \(B(\theta a+(1-\theta)c,b)\) by \(B(a,b)^\theta B(c,b)^{1-\theta}\). Equality for distinct \(a,c\) would require the two positive integrands to be proportional almost everywhere, impossible since their ratio is a nonconstant power of the integration variable. This proves the strict log-convexity used in §6.5.
