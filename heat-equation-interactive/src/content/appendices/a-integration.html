Appendix A · Integration and analytical estimates

Measure, kernel and compactness estimates for the integral formulation in Chapter 2 and its limiting arguments in Chapter 7.

A.1 Nonnegative integration and limiting operations

Tonelli and Fubini

Let \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\) and \((Y,\mathcal B,\nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. For a nonnegative product-measurable function \(F\), Tonelli's theorem gives

\[\begin{aligned} \int_{X\times Y}F\,d(\mu\otimes\nu) &=\int_X\!\left(\int_Y F(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)\\ &=\int_Y\!\left(\int_X F(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y). \end{aligned}\]

The common value may be infinite. Fubini's theorem gives the same identities for signed or complex \(F\) if \(\int_{X\times Y}|F|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty\); then the sections are integrable almost everywhere. Counting measure gives the interchange of nonnegative sums and integrals in Proposition 2.2.

Convergence theorems

Let \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\) be a measure space. All functions below are measurable and the pointwise hypotheses hold almost everywhere.

Monotone convergence theorem
If \(f_n:X\to[0,\infty]\) and \(f_n\uparrow f\), then
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_X f_n\,d\mu=\int_X f\,d\mu.\]
Proof of monotone convergence

Since \(f_n\le f\), the limit of the integrals is at most \(\int_Xf\,d\mu\). For a nonnegative simple function \(s\le f\) and \(0<c<1\), the sets \(E_n=\{f_n\ge cs\}\) increase to \(X\) up to a null set. Each level set of \(s\) has increasing intersection with \(E_n\). Continuity from below of the measure gives

\[\lim_n\int_X f_n\,d\mu\ge c\lim_n\int_{E_n}s\,d\mu=c\int_X s\,d\mu.\]

Let \(c\uparrow1\) and take the supremum over \(s\) for the reverse bound. ∎

Fatou's lemma
If \(f_n:X\to[0,\infty]\), then
\[\int_X\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n\,d\mu\le\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_X f_n\,d\mu.\]
Proof of Fatou's lemma

Set \(g_m=\inf_{n\ge m}f_n\). Then \(g_m\uparrow\liminf_n f_n\). By monotone convergence,

\[\begin{aligned} \int_X\liminf_n f_n\,d\mu &=\lim_m\int_X g_m\,d\mu\\ &\le\lim_m\inf_{n\ge m}\int_X f_n\,d\mu =\liminf_n\int_X f_n\,d\mu. \end{aligned}\]
∎
Dominated convergence theorem
If \(f_n\to f\) and \(|f_n|\le g\) for a single nonnegative \(g\in L^1(X,\mu)\), then \(f\in L^1(X,\mu)\) and
\[\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_n-f\|_{L^1}=0.\]
Proof of dominated convergence

The limiting bound \(|f|\le g\) gives integrability. Apply Fatou's lemma to \(2g-|f_n-f|\ge0\).

\[\begin{aligned} 2\int_Xg\,d\mu &\le\liminf_n\int_X(2g-|f_n-f|)\,d\mu\\ &=2\int_Xg\,d\mu-\limsup_n\|f_n-f\|_{L^1}. \end{aligned}\]

Subtract the finite integral to obtain \(\limsup_n\|f_n-f\|_{L^1}\le0\). ∎

In §7.8, monotone convergence passes from increasing regularizations to the boundary integral. Fatou gives lower bounds at boundary and terminal observations even for nonmonotone approximations. In §9.5, the recent-time contribution is estimated separately so that dominated convergence can be used on earlier times.

Uniform integrability

On a probability space, a family \(\mathcal Z\subset L^1\) is uniformly integrable if \(\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{Z\in\mathcal Z}\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>R\}}]=0\). A uniform \(L^p\) bound with \(p>1\) suffices, since

\[\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>R\}}]\le R^{1-p}\mathbb E|Z|^p.\]

If \(Z_n\to Z\) in probability and the \(Z_n\) are uniformly integrable, then \(Z_n\to Z\) in \(L^1\).

Proof of the uniform integrability criterion

Fatou along an almost surely convergent subsequence bounds \(\mathbb E[(|Z|-R)_+]\) by \(\sup_n\mathbb E[(|Z_n|-R)_+]\), which tends to zero. For each fixed \(R\), truncations at height \(R\) converge in probability and, being uniformly bounded, converge in \(L^1\). The triangle inequality combines this limit with the two truncation errors. A uniform \(L^1\) bound alone does not imply the uniform tail condition. ∎

A.2 Gaussian estimates and approximate identities

Let \(g_h(x)=(4\pi h)^{-N/2}e^{-|x|^2/(4h)}\) on \(\mathbb R^N\) for \(h>0\). Completing the square gives \(g_s*g_t=g_{s+t}\). The substitution \(x=\sqrt h\,y\) gives

\[g_h(x)=h^{-N/2}g_1(x/\sqrt h),\qquad \int_{|x|>d}g_h(x)\,dx=\int_{|y|>d/\sqrt h}g_1(y)\,dy\longrightarrow0\quad(d>0).\]

For bounded \(f\), split \(g_h*f(x)-f(x)\) at radius \(d\). The inner contribution is bounded by \(\sup_{|y|\le d}|f(x-y)-f(x)|\); the outer contribution is bounded by \(2\|f\|_\infty\int_{|y|>d}g_h(y)\,dy\). This proves convergence at continuity points and locally uniform convergence for continuous data by uniform continuity on enlarged compact sets.

For \(f\in L^2(\mathbb R^N)\), Jensen's inequality for the probability density \(g_h\) gives

\[\begin{aligned} \|g_h*f\|_2^2 &\le \int_{\mathbb R^N} g_h(y)\|f(\cdot-y)\|_2^2\,dy=\|f\|_2^2,\\ \|g_h*f-f\|_2^2 &\le \int_{\mathbb R^N}g_h(y)\|f(\cdot-y)-f\|_2^2\,dy\longrightarrow0. \end{aligned}\]

For the limit, use continuity of translations in \(L^2\), first for smooth compactly supported functions and then by density, and split at radius \(d\) as above. Even reflection gives the half-space version for the Neumann kernel.

For \(C,d>0\) and \(a\in\mathbb R\), the recent-time estimates are

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^\eta h^{-a}e^{-d^2/(Ch)}\,dh &=(d^2/C)^{1-a}\int_{d^2/(C\eta)}^\infty v^{a-2}e^{-v}\,dv\longrightarrow0,\\ \int_0^\eta h^{-1/2}\,dh&=2\sqrt\eta\longrightarrow0 \qquad(\eta\downarrow0). \end{aligned}\]

The first estimate uses positive spatial separation to suppress any fixed power of \(h^{-1}\). The second controls the time singularity after spatial integration of the boundary kernel, even without separation. See Chapter 7 and Chapter 9.

A.3 Polar integrability and homogeneous weights

In \(m\ge1\) dimensions, let \(\beta\in\mathbb R\) and \(0<a<R\). After factoring out the surface measure of the unit sphere,

\[\int_a^R r^{m-1-\beta}dr=\begin{cases}(R^{m-\beta}-a^{m-\beta})/(m-\beta),&\beta\ne m,\\\log(R/a),&\beta=m.\end{cases}\tag{A.1}\]

Integrability at zero is equivalent to \(\beta<m\), and integrability at infinity to \(\beta>m\). At \(\beta=m\) both divergences are logarithmic. The boundary dimension is \(N-1\), whereas the bulk dimension is \(N\). The moving graph has boundary space-time measure zero by Tonelli, since each time section is a singleton. These facts govern the endpoint exclusions in the harmonic representation and the dimension-two obstruction.

A.4 Young, Hölder, Minkowski and Grönwall inequalities

Young's inequality

Let \(a,b\ge0\) and \(p,q>1\) with \(1/p+1/q=1\). Then

\[ab\le \frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}.\]

In particular, for \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[ab\le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}a^2+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}b^2.\]
Proof of Young's inequality

For positive \(a,b\), apply concavity of \(\log\) to \(a^p,b^q\) with weights \(1/p,1/q\), then exponentiate. If either factor vanishes, use nonnegativity. For the adjustable estimate take \(p=q=2\) and apply the first inequality to \(\sqrt\varepsilon\,a\) and \(b/\sqrt\varepsilon\). ∎

In the trace estimate, take \(a=\|\partial_N w\|_2\), \(b=\|w\|_2\) and multiply by two. In the localized energy estimates in §7.4 and Chapter 13, choose the coefficient of the cutoff-weighted gradient square below the coercive margin. The other square contributes the cutoff error.

Hölder's inequality

Let \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\) be a measure space and \(p,q>1\) satisfy \(1/p+1/q=1\). If \(f\in L^p(X,\mu)\) and \(g\in L^q(X,\mu)\), then

\[\int_X |fg|\,d\mu\le\|f\|_{L^p}\|g\|_{L^q}.\]
Proof of Hölder's inequality

If either norm vanishes, \(fg=0\) almost everywhere. Otherwise put \(F=|f|/\|f\|_{L^p}\) and \(G=|g|/\|g\|_{L^q}\). Young's inequality gives

\[\begin{aligned} \int_X FG\,d\mu &\le \frac1p\int_X F^p\,d\mu+\frac1q\int_X G^q\,d\mu\\ &=\frac1p+\frac1q=1. \end{aligned}\]

Multiply by the two norms. ∎

Minkowski's inequality

For \(1\le p\le\infty\) and \(f,g\in L^p(X,\mu)\),

\[\|f+g\|_{L^p}\le\|f\|_{L^p}+\|g\|_{L^p}.\]
Proof of Minkowski's inequality

At the endpoints, integrate the pointwise triangle inequality or take the essential supremum. For \(1<p<\infty\), convexity gives \((a+b)^p\le2^{p-1}(a^p+b^p)\), so \(f+g\in L^p\). Hölder gives

\[\begin{aligned} \|f+g\|_{L^p}^p &\le\int_X(|f|+|g|)|f+g|^{p-1}\,d\mu\\ &\le(\|f\|_{L^p}+\|g\|_{L^p})\|f+g\|_{L^p}^{p-1}. \end{aligned}\]

Divide by the last factor if it is nonzero. ∎

Grönwall's inequality

Differential form.
Let \(y\ge0\) be absolutely continuous on \([0,T]\), with \(g\in L^1(0,T)\) and \(C\in\mathbb R\). If \(y'\le Cy+g\) almost everywhere, then for every \(t\in[0,T]\),
\[y(t)\le e^{Ct}y(0)+\int_0^t e^{C(t-s)}g(s)\,ds.\]
Proof of differential Grönwall

The product \(e^{-Ct}y(t)\) is absolutely continuous and its derivative is bounded above almost everywhere by \(e^{-Ct}g(t)\). Integration on \((0,t)\) gives

\[e^{-Ct}y(t)-y(0)\le\int_0^t e^{-Cs}g(s)\,ds.\]

Multiply by \(e^{Ct}\). ∎

Integral form.
Let \(y,g\ge0\) be integrable on \((0,T)\) and \(C,y_0\ge0\). Suppose \(y(t)\le y_0+\int_0^t(Cy(s)+g(s))\,ds\) almost everywhere. At every time where this inequality holds,
\[y(t)\le e^{Ct}y_0+\int_0^t e^{C(t-s)}g(s)\,ds.\]
Proof of integral Grönwall

The right-hand side of the hypothesis defines an absolutely continuous majorant \(Y\). Since \(C\ge0\) and \(y\le Y\) almost everywhere, \(Y'\le CY+g\) and \(Y(0)=y_0\). Apply the differential form to \(Y\), then use the original pointwise bound wherever it holds. ∎

See the form evolution estimate.

Discrete form.
Let \(C\ge0\), \(\Delta t>0\), and let \(y_j,g_j\ge0\) satisfy \(y_{j+1}\le(1+C\Delta t)y_j+\Delta t\,g_j\) for \(j\ge0\). Then
\[\begin{aligned} y_n&\le (1+C\Delta t)^ny_0+\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(1+C\Delta t)^{n-1-j}g_j\\ &\le e^{Cn\Delta t}y_0+\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{C(n-1-j)\Delta t}g_j. \end{aligned}\]
Proof of discrete Grönwall

Substitute the bound for \(y_n\) into the recurrence. Multiplication by \(1+C\Delta t\) increases every exponent by one and the new term has exponent zero. This proves the first inequality by induction from \(n=0\). The second uses \(1+C\Delta t\le e^{C\Delta t}\) and nonnegativity of the terms. ∎

A.5 Compactness and uniform convergence

Dini's theorem. Let \(K\) be compact. A monotone sequence in \(C(K;\mathbb R)\) that converges pointwise to \(u\in C(K;\mathbb R)\) converges uniformly.

Proof of Dini's theorem

For an increasing sequence \(u_n\), the sets \(\{x\in K:u(x)-u_n(x)<\eta\}\), for fixed \(\eta>0\), form an increasing open cover. Take a finite subcover, then its largest index. All subsequent sets also cover \(K\), so \(\|u-u_n\|_\infty\le\eta\) for all sufficiently large \(n\). For a decreasing sequence apply this to \(-u_n\). ∎

Arzelà–Ascoli. On a compact metric space \(K\), a uniformly bounded, equicontinuous sequence of real continuous functions has a uniformly convergent subsequence.

Proof of Arzelà–Ascoli

Choose finite \(1/j\)-nets for \(j\ge1\) and extract a diagonal subsequence converging on their countable union. For a given error tolerance, choose a net fine enough that equicontinuity bounds the differences between values at a point and its nearest net point. Convergence at the finitely many net points then makes the subsequence uniformly Cauchy. Its uniform limit is continuous. ∎

If a relatively compact sequence has only one subsequential limit, the full sequence converges: otherwise a subsequence staying a positive distance from that limit would have a convergent subsequence. Diagonal extraction over a compact exhaustion yields local compactness. In the moving-pole arguments the compact sets must stay a positive distance from the excluded graph.

For weak compactness and lower semicontinuity see Appendix B.4. Identify weak gradients against smooth tests, take the lower bound on each compact region, and exhaust the domain to bound the global nonnegative integral without assuming global unweighted gradient control.
