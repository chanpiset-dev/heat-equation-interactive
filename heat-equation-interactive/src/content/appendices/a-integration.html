Appendix A · Integration and analytical estimates

When to use this appendix. Use when a proof exchanges integrals or takes a limit; A.1 and A.2 support the kernel and positive-series arguments in Chapters 2 and 7.

A reference for the limiting operations in the kernel, variational and probability arguments. The fundamental measure and compactness theorems stated here are standard prerequisites; the consequences used in the exposition are derived explicitly.

A.1 Nonnegative integration and limiting operations

Work with σ-finite measures and measurable functions. Tonelli's theorem permits integration in either order for nonnegative functions, even when the integral is infinite. Fubini's theorem permits signed or complex integration in either order when the absolute integral is finite. These are invoked as standard measure theory results. On counting measure, integration is summation; this is the justification for exchanging series and nonnegative integrals in Proposition 2.2.

Monotone convergence. If \(0\le f_n\uparrow f\) almost everywhere, then \(\int f_n\uparrow\int f\). This follows from the definition of the Lebesgue integral by nonnegative simple functions (for each simple function below f, approximate its integral from below using the increasing level sets of f_n). The theorem applies to all regularization limits of nonnegative flux densities.

Fatou's lemma. For \(f_n\ge0\), \(\int\liminf_n f_n\le\liminf_n\int f_n\). Apply monotone convergence to \(g_m=\inf_{n\ge m} f_n\) and use \(\int g_m\le\inf_{n\ge m}\int f_n\). This gives the supersolution extension at boundary and terminal observations and the lower comparison for nonmonotone approximations.

Dominated convergence. If \(f_n\to f\) almost everywhere and \(|f_n|\le g\in L^1\), apply Fatou to \(2g-|f_n-f|\); it gives \(\int |f_n-f|\to0\). The pointwise majorant must be independent of n. For instance, the weighted heat-potential proof separates the recent-time interval before applying dominated convergence to earlier times.

Uniform integrability on a probability space. A family bounded in \(L^p\), p>1, is uniformly integrable: \(\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{|Z|>R}]\le R^{1-p}\mathbb E|Z|^p\). If a uniformly integrable sequence converges in probability, it converges in L¹ (truncate at height R, use convergence in probability for the bounded truncations, and use Fatou along a subsequence for the limit's tails). This is the relevant probability version, not an automatic consequence of bounded L¹ norms.

A.2 Gaussian estimates and approximate identities

Completing the square yields \(g_s*g_t=g_{s+t}\), and rescaling gives \(g_h(x)=h^{-N/2}g_1(x/\sqrt h)\). In particular \(\int_{|x|>d}g_h(x)dx\to0\) for every d>0. Splitting the convolution into the ball of radius d and its complement proves recovery at every continuity point of a bounded datum; compact uniform continuity gives locally uniform convergence. For L² data, Jensen gives contraction, and translation continuity in L² (first for smooth compact functions, then by density) gives \(\|g_h*f-f\|_2\to0\). Even reflection produces the half-space results in Chapter 2.

For d>0 and any real a, \(\int_0^\eta h^{-a}e^{-d^2/(C h)}dh\to0\) as η↓0: substitute \(v=d^2/(Ch)\) and use the exponential tail of a power times \(e^{-v}\). Also \(\int_0^\eta h^{-1/2}dh=2\sqrt\eta\). These are the two independent mechanisms for recent-time smallness in Chapters 7 and 9.

A.3 Polar integrability and homogeneous weights

In m spatial dimensions, for real β, the radial integral of a power over an annulus is the angular constant times

\[\int_a^R r^{m-1-\beta}dr=\begin{cases}(R^{m-\beta}-a^{m-\beta})/(m-\beta),&\beta\ne m,\\\log(R/a),&\beta=m.\end{cases}\tag{A.1}\]

Thus integrability at zero is equivalent to β<m, while at infinity it requires β>m. The boundary has dimension N−1, not N. The graph has boundary space-time measure zero because every time section is a singleton (Tonelli). These facts justify the endpoint exclusions in the harmonic heat representation and the dimension-two obstruction.

A.4 Hölder, Young and Grönwall inequalities

For conjugate p,q>1, convexity gives \(ab\le a^p/p+b^q/q\) for nonnegative a,b. Normalize the Lᵖ and Lᑫ norms and integrate to obtain Hölder. In particular \(ab\le\epsilon a^2/2+b^2/(2\epsilon)\) for ε>0, the adjustable absorption estimate used in Chapters 7 and 13. Minkowski and the triangle inequality then give the standard norm estimates used throughout.

If an absolutely continuous \(y\ge0\) satisfies \(y'\le Cy+g\) with g integrable and C constant, multiply by \(e^{-Ct}\) and integrate: \(y(t)\le e^{Ct}y(0)+\int_0^t e^{C(t-s)}g(s)ds\). If \(y'\) is replaced by an integrated inequality, apply the differential argument to the absolutely continuous majorant \(y(0)+\int_0^t(Cy+g)\) for C≥0 and g≥0. This is the version used in the energy comparison. A nonnegative sequence with \(y_{n+1}\le(1+C\Delta t)y_n+\Delta t g_n\) obeys the corresponding geometric-sum bound because \(1+C\Delta t\le e^{C\Delta t}\).

A.5 Compactness and uniform convergence

Dini. For a monotonically increasing sequence of continuous real functions on a compact set K converging to a continuous finite function u, the open sets \(\{u-u_n<\eta\}\) form an increasing cover of K. Compactness makes one set cover K, proving uniform convergence. The same proof works for decreasing sequences by changing sign.

Arzelà–Ascoli. Uniform boundedness and equicontinuity on compact K imply that every sequence has a uniformly convergent subsequence. For compact metric K, choose finite 1/j nets, take a diagonal subsequence converging at their countable union, then use equicontinuity and a finite net to prove uniform Cauchy convergence. Conversely, if every convergent subsequence has the same possible limit and the family is relatively compact, the full sequence converges. On an increasing compact exhaustion the diagonal argument gives local compactness. For the moving graph, compact subsets must stay a positive distance from the excluded graph; no claim is made about uniform convergence across it.

Weak compactness and lower semicontinuity of norms in Hilbert spaces are recalled in Appendix B.4. The identity of a weak gradient is checked against smooth test functions, and local lower semicontinuity followed by exhaustion gives global nonnegative estimates without assuming global unweighted gradient control.
