Appendix A · Integration and analytical estimates

Measure, kernel and compactness estimates for the integral formulation in Chapter 2 and its limiting arguments in Chapter 7.

A.1 Nonnegative integration and limiting operations

Tonelli and Fubini

Let \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\) and \((Y,\mathcal B,\nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. For a nonnegative product-measurable function \(F\), Tonelli's theorem gives

\[\begin{aligned} \int_{X\times Y}F\,d(\mu\otimes\nu) &=\int_X\!\left(\int_Y F(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)\\ &=\int_Y\!\left(\int_X F(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y). \end{aligned}\]

The common value may be infinite. Fubini's theorem gives the same identities for signed or complex \(F\) if \(\int_{X\times Y}|F|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty\); then the sections are integrable almost everywhere. Counting measure gives the interchange of nonnegative sums and integrals in Proposition 2.2.

Monotone convergence, Fatou's lemma and dominated convergence

All functions below are measurable on \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\), and all pointwise hypotheses hold almost everywhere.

Monotone convergence.
If \(0\le f_n\uparrow f\), then \(\int_X f_n\,d\mu\uparrow\int_X f\,d\mu\). For a nonnegative simple function \(s\le f\) and \(0<c<1\), the sets \(\{f_n\ge cs\}\) increase to \(X\) up to null sets. Thus \(\lim_n\int_X f_n\,d\mu\ge c\int_X s\,d\mu\). Let \(c\uparrow 1\) and take the supremum over \(s\). This is the limit used for increasing regularizations of nonnegative flux densities.
Fatou's lemma.
If \(f_n\ge0\), then \(\int_X\liminf_n f_n\,d\mu\le\liminf_n\int_X f_n\,d\mu\). Indeed, \(g_m=\inf_{n\ge m}f_n\) increases to \(\liminf_n f_n\), and \(\int_X g_m\,d\mu\le\inf_{n\ge m}\int_X f_n\,d\mu\). This gives lower bounds for supersolutions at boundary and terminal observations and for nonmonotone approximations.
Dominated convergence.
If \(f_n\to f\) and \(|f_n|\le g\) for a single \(g\in L^1(X,\mu)\), then \(\|f_n-f\|_{L^1}\to0\). Apply Fatou's lemma to \(2g-|f_n-f|\). In Chapter 9 the recent-time interval is estimated separately so that an integrable majorant can be used on the earlier-time interval.

Uniform integrability

On a probability space, a family \(\mathcal Z\subset L^1\) is uniformly integrable if \(\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{Z\in\mathcal Z}\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>R\}}]=0\). A uniform \(L^p\) bound with \(p>1\) suffices, since

\[\mathbb E[|Z|\mathbf1_{\{|Z|>R\}}]\le R^{1-p}\mathbb E|Z|^p.\]

If \(Z_n\to Z\) in probability and the \(Z_n\) are uniformly integrable, then \(Z_n\to Z\) in \(L^1\). Fatou along an almost surely convergent subsequence bounds \(\mathbb E[(|Z|-R)_+]\) by \(\sup_n\mathbb E[(|Z_n|-R)_+]\). Truncation at height \(R\) gives bounded variables that converge in probability and hence in \(L^1\); the two truncation errors tend uniformly to zero as \(R\to\infty\). A mere uniform \(L^1\) bound does not imply the tail condition.

A.2 Gaussian estimates and approximate identities

Let \(g_h(x)=(4\pi h)^{-N/2}e^{-|x|^2/(4h)}\) on \(\mathbb R^N\) for \(h>0\). Completing the square gives \(g_s*g_t=g_{s+t}\). The substitution \(x=\sqrt h\,y\) gives

\[g_h(x)=h^{-N/2}g_1(x/\sqrt h),\qquad \int_{|x|>d}g_h(x)\,dx=\int_{|y|>d/\sqrt h}g_1(y)\,dy\longrightarrow0\quad(d>0).\]

For bounded \(f\), split \(g_h*f(x)-f(x)\) at radius \(d\). The inner contribution is bounded by \(\sup_{|y|\le d}|f(x-y)-f(x)|\); the outer contribution is bounded by \(2\|f\|_\infty\int_{|y|>d}g_h(y)\,dy\). This proves convergence at continuity points and locally uniform convergence for continuous data by uniform continuity on enlarged compact sets.

For \(f\in L^2(\mathbb R^N)\), Jensen's inequality for the probability density \(g_h\) gives

\[\begin{aligned} \|g_h*f\|_2^2 &\le \int_{\mathbb R^N} g_h(y)\|f(\cdot-y)\|_2^2\,dy=\|f\|_2^2,\\ \|g_h*f-f\|_2^2 &\le \int_{\mathbb R^N}g_h(y)\|f(\cdot-y)-f\|_2^2\,dy\longrightarrow0. \end{aligned}\]

For the limit, use continuity of translations in \(L^2\), first for smooth compactly supported functions and then by density, and split at radius \(d\) as above. Even reflection gives the half-space version for the Neumann kernel.

For \(C,d>0\) and \(a\in\mathbb R\), the recent-time estimates are

\[\begin{aligned} \int_0^\eta h^{-a}e^{-d^2/(Ch)}\,dh &=(d^2/C)^{1-a}\int_{d^2/(C\eta)}^\infty v^{a-2}e^{-v}\,dv\longrightarrow0,\\ \int_0^\eta h^{-1/2}\,dh&=2\sqrt\eta\longrightarrow0 \qquad(\eta\downarrow0). \end{aligned}\]

The first estimate uses positive spatial separation to suppress any fixed power of \(h^{-1}\). The second controls the time singularity after spatial integration of the boundary kernel, even without separation. See Chapter 7 and Chapter 9.

A.3 Polar integrability and homogeneous weights

In \(m\ge1\) dimensions, let \(\beta\in\mathbb R\) and \(0<a<R\). After factoring out the surface measure of the unit sphere,

\[\int_a^R r^{m-1-\beta}dr=\begin{cases}(R^{m-\beta}-a^{m-\beta})/(m-\beta),&\beta\ne m,\\\log(R/a),&\beta=m.\end{cases}\tag{A.1}\]

Integrability at zero is equivalent to \(\beta<m\), and integrability at infinity to \(\beta>m\). At \(\beta=m\) both divergences are logarithmic. The boundary dimension is \(N-1\), whereas the bulk dimension is \(N\). The moving graph has boundary space-time measure zero by Tonelli, since each time section is a singleton. These facts govern the endpoint exclusions in the harmonic representation and the dimension-two obstruction.

A.4 Young, Hölder, Minkowski and Grönwall inequalities

Young's inequality

Let \(a,b\ge0\), \(p,q>1\), and \(p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1\). Then

\[ab\le \frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}.\]

For positive \(a,b\), apply concavity of \(\log\) to \(a^p,b^q\) with weights \(1/p,1/q\), then exponentiate. If either factor is zero, the inequality follows from nonnegativity. Taking \(p=q=2\) and rescaling the factors by \(\sqrt\varepsilon\) and \(1/\sqrt\varepsilon\) gives

\[ab\le \frac{\varepsilon}{2}a^2+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}b^2,\qquad\varepsilon>0.\]

In the trace estimate take \(a=\|\partial_N w\|_2\), \(b=\|w\|_2\) and multiply by two. In the localized energy estimates in Chapter 7 and Chapter 13, the same inequality splits mixed products of the cutoff-weighted gradient and a cutoff derivative into squares. The coefficient of the gradient square is chosen below the available coercive margin; the other term is bounded by the corresponding cutoff error.

Hölder's inequality

Let \((X,\mathcal A,\mu)\) be a measure space. Assume

  1. \(p,q>1\) and \(p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1\);
  2. \(f\in L^p(X,\mu)\);
  3. \(g\in L^q(X,\mu)\).
\[\int_X |fg|\,d\mu\le\|f\|_{L^p}\|g\|_{L^q}.\]

Proof. If either norm vanishes, \(fg=0\) almost everywhere. Otherwise put \(F=|f|/\|f\|_{L^p}\) and \(G=|g|/\|g\|_{L^q}\). Young's inequality gives

\[\begin{aligned} \int_X FG\,d\mu &\le \frac{1}{p}\int_X F^p\,d\mu+\frac{1}{q}\int_X G^q\,d\mu\\ &=\frac1p+\frac1q=1. \end{aligned}\]

Multiply by the two norms. ∎

Minkowski's inequality

For \(1\le p\le\infty\) and \(f,g\in L^p(X,\mu)\), one has \(\|f+g\|_{L^p}\le\|f\|_{L^p}+\|g\|_{L^p}\). At the endpoints, apply the pointwise triangle inequality and take the integral or essential supremum. For \(1<p<\infty\), \(f+g\) has finite norm by \((a+b)^p\le2^{p-1}(a^p+b^p)\), a consequence of convexity of \(t^p\). Hölder gives

\[\|f+g\|_{L^p}^p\le\int_X(|f|+|g|)|f+g|^{p-1}\,d\mu \le(\|f\|_{L^p}+\|g\|_{L^p})\|f+g\|_{L^p}^{p-1}.\]

Divide by the final factor if it is nonzero.

Grönwall's inequality

Differential form.
Let \(y\ge0\) be absolutely continuous on \([0,T]\), \(g\in L^1(0,T)\), \(C\in\mathbb R\), and \(y'\le Cy+g\) almost everywhere. Integrating \((e^{-Ct}y(t))'\le e^{-Ct}g(t)\) gives
\[y(t)\le e^{Ct}y(0)+\int_0^t e^{C(t-s)}g(s)\,ds.\]
Integral form.
If \(y\ge0\) is integrable, \(C\ge0\), \(g\ge0\) is integrable, and \(y(t)\le y_0+\int_0^t(Cy(s)+g(s))\,ds\) with \(y_0\ge0\), apply the differential form to the right-hand side \(Y(t)\). It satisfies \(Y'\le CY+g\) and \(Y(0)=y_0\); thus the same bound holds at each time for which \(y\le Y\). See the form evolution estimate.
Discrete form.
For \(C\ge0\), \(\Delta t>0\), \(g_j\ge0\), and \(0\le y_{j+1}\le(1+C\Delta t)y_j+\Delta t\,g_j\), induction gives
\[\begin{aligned} y_n&\le (1+C\Delta t)^ny_0+\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(1+C\Delta t)^{n-1-j}g_j\\ &\le e^{Cn\Delta t}y_0+\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}e^{C(n-1-j)\Delta t}g_j. \end{aligned}\]

A.5 Compactness and uniform convergence

Dini's theorem. Let \(u_n\) be an increasing sequence in \(C(K;\mathbb R)\), where \(K\) is compact. If \(u_n\to u\) pointwise and \(u\) is continuous and finite, then the convergence is uniform. For each \(\eta>0\) the sets \(\{x\in K:u(x)-u_n(x)<\eta\}\) form an increasing open cover. A finite subcover exists, so the largest index in it already covers \(K\). For a decreasing sequence apply the argument to \(-u_n\).

Arzelà–Ascoli. On a compact metric space \(K\), a uniformly bounded, equicontinuous sequence of real continuous functions has a uniformly convergent subsequence. Choose finite \(1/j\)-nets for \(j\ge1\) and extract a diagonal subsequence converging on their countable union. For a given error tolerance, choose a net fine enough that equicontinuity bounds the differences between values at a point and its nearest net point. Convergence at the finitely many net points then makes the subsequence uniformly Cauchy.

If a relatively compact sequence has only one subsequential limit, the full sequence converges: otherwise a subsequence staying a positive distance from that limit would have a convergent subsequence. Diagonal extraction over a compact exhaustion yields local compactness. In the moving-pole arguments the compact sets must stay a positive distance from the excluded graph.

For weak compactness and lower semicontinuity see Appendix B.4. Identify weak gradients against smooth tests, take the lower bound on each compact region, and exhaust the domain to bound the global nonnegative integral without assuming global unweighted gradient control.
