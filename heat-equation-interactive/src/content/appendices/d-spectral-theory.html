Appendix D · Preliminary material

Appendix D · Spectral theory and stationary forms

When to use this appendix. Read D.1–D.3 when Chapter 8 turns negative form values into exponential amplification. A form-domain vector need not be an operator-domain eigenfunction.

This appendix supplies the spectral tools used in Chapter 8. We take the spectral theorem for bounded self-adjoint operators as a standard foundational result and derive the particular closed-form representation and heat evolution needed here. All Hilbert spaces may be complexified for spectral calculus; the real forms and solutions are recovered by restriction to real functions. We use inner products and forms linear in the first variable and conjugate-linear in the second; all diagonal values of Hermitian forms are real. A real symmetric form is extended sesquilinearly.

D.1 Spectral measures and functional calculus

A densely defined operator \(A\) on a Hilbert space \(\mathscr X\) is self-adjoint if \(A=A^*\), including equality of domains. The spectral theorem represents a self-adjoint operator using an orthogonal projection-valued measure \(E_A\) on the real line. For pairwise disjoint Borel sets \(B_j\), the projections have orthogonal ranges, \(E_A(\mathbb R)=I\), and \(E_A(\bigcup_j B_j)v=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^m E_A(B_j)v\) in \(\mathscr X\) for each \(v\in\mathscr X\). For any vector \(v\), the scalar measure \(\mathfrak m_v(J)=\langle E_A(J)v,v\rangle\) has total mass \(\|v\|^2\).

Functional calculus. For a complex Borel function \(F\) defined on the real line, the operator \(F(A)\) has domain and squared norm

\[\begin{aligned} D(F(A))&=\left\{v\in\mathscr X:\int_{\mathbb R}|F(\ell)|^2\,d\mathfrak m_v(\ell)<\infty\right\},\\ \|F(A)v\|^2&=\int_{\mathbb R}|F(\ell)|^2\,d\mathfrak m_v(\ell). \end{aligned}\]

Bounded functions yield bounded operators; real functions yield self-adjoint operators on the stated domain. Products of spectral multipliers agree with the corresponding operator products on vectors in the domain of the composition.

Example D.1 · Two orthogonal eigenspaces

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(-3,1)\) and \(v=(1,1)/\sqrt2\), the scalar measure is \(\tfrac12\delta_{-3}+\tfrac12\delta_1\). The energy is \(-1\), while \(\langle v,e^{-hA}v\rangle=(e^{3h}+e^{-h})/2\). The example in Figure 8.2 evaluates this exact expression. In general the measure need not consist of point masses.

Example D.1a · Negative continuous spectrum

On \(L^2([-3,1])\), let \((Af)(s)=sf(s)\). This bounded self-adjoint operator has spectrum \([-3,1]\): outside the interval the inverse multiplier \((s-z)^{-1}\) is bounded; inside, normalized indicators of shrinking neighborhoods show that \(A-z\) is not bounded below. It has no eigenvalues because each level set \(\{s=z\}\) has zero measure. With \(v(s)=1/2\), the scalar spectral measure is uniform with density 1/4. Its first moment is −1 and its pairing is \((e^{3h}-e^{-h})/(4h)\) for \(h\) > 0. The negative energy therefore does not require any negative eigenvalue.

Example D.1b · An unbounded multiplication operator

On \(\mathscr X=L^2([1,\infty))\), define \((Af)(s)=sf(s)\) with maximal domain \(D(A)=\{f:sf\in L^2\}\). This multiplication operator is self-adjoint: testing the adjoint relation against functions of bounded support identifies the adjoint action as multiplication by \(s\), and its domain is the same maximal domain. Spectral projections multiply by indicators of Borel subsets, and

\[D(A^{1/2})=\{f:\sqrt{s}f\in L^2\},\quad q[f]=\int_1^\infty s|f(s)|^2ds.\]

For \(f(s)=s^{-p}\), the ambient space requires \(p>1/2\), the form domain requires \(p>1\), and the operator domain requires \(p>3/2\). Hence \(p=3/4\) is an ambient-space-only example, while \(p=5/4\) belongs to the form domain but not the operator domain. Here \(e^{-hA}\) multiplies by \(e^{-hs}\), a bounded function for \(h\ge0\). This exemplifies why functional calculus may define a bounded semigroup even when A is unbounded.

D.2 From closed forms to self-adjoint operators

Theorem D.1 · Representation of a lower-bounded closed form

Let \(\mathscr X\) be a Hilbert space and \(q\) a symmetric form with dense domain \(\mathscr V\subset\mathscr X\). Assume

  1. \(q[v]+C\|v\|^2\ge\|v\|^2\) on \(\mathscr V\) for a constant \(C\in\mathbb R\);
  2. \(\mathscr V\) is complete in the norm induced by \(q_C=q+C\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\).

There is a unique self-adjoint \(A\) with \(A+C\ge I\) such that

\[D((A+C)^{1/2})=\mathscr V,\quad q_C[v]=\|(A+C)^{1/2}v\|^2.\tag{D.1}\]

Its operator domain consists exactly of those \(v\in\mathscr V\) for which \(q(v,w)=\langle g,w\rangle\) for some \(g\in\mathscr X\) and all \(w\in\mathscr V\); then \(Av=g\).

Proof of the closed-form representation

By Riesz representation in the Hilbert space with inner product \(q_C\), for each \(f\in\mathscr X\) there exists a unique \(Rf\in\mathscr V\) satisfying \(q_C(Rf,w)=\langle f,w\rangle\) for all \(w\in\mathscr V\). In the ambient space \(\|Rf\|\le\|f\|\) by testing with \(Rf\), and Hermitian symmetry gives \(R=R^*\). The pairing \(\langle f,Rf\rangle=q_C[Rf]\) is nonnegative. Density of \(\mathscr V\) makes \(R\) injective; hence the range of the self-adjoint \(R\) is dense. The bounded spectral theorem, with the reciprocal function on \((0,1]\), yields a positive self-adjoint inverse \(T=R^{-1}\) on \(\operatorname{Ran}R\), with \(T\ge I\). There is no spectral mass at zero since the kernel of \(R\) is trivial. Put \(A=T-C\).

The defining equation for \(R\) shows exactly that \(q_C(v,w)=\langle Tv,w\rangle\) on \(D(T)\times\mathscr V\); conversely if the form functional is represented by \(g\), then \(v=Rg\). On \(D(T)\) we have \(q_C[v]=\langle Tv,v\rangle=\|T^{1/2}v\|^2\).

To extend this identity, observe that \(\operatorname{Ran}R\) is dense in \(\mathscr V\) in the form norm: any vector form-orthogonal to all \(Rf\) is ambient-orthogonal to every \(f\), so vanishes. Independently, spectral truncations to \([1,k]\) show that \(D(T)\) is dense in \(D(T^{1/2})\) in the square-root norm, by monotone/dominated convergence of the squared norm integral. That norm is complete because the square root is a closed operator and dominates the ambient norm. The two completions of \(D(T)\) in the same norm therefore agree as subspaces of \(\mathscr X\). This proves (D.1). The domain characterization fixes A uniquely. □

Applied to the bounded Robin form (8.6), the shifted norm is equivalent to the \(H^1\) norm by the half-space trace inequality (B.1). This verifies the completeness hypothesis. For the singular supercritical form, no such lower-bounded shift exists by (8.3); we use the theorem only for the bounded approximations.

Which domain is needed?
\(\mathscr X=L^2(\mathcal H)\)All square-integrable data
\(\mathscr V=D((A_V+C)^{1/2})=H^1(\mathcal H)\)Finite shifted form energy; the concentrating test vector lives here
\(D(A_V)\)Operator action in \(L^2\); smooth members satisfy the Robin condition

These inclusions are continuous in the respective graph norms. Closedness of a form means completeness in the shifted form norm, not boundedness of its associated operator on the ambient space. The multiplication example above shows that the inclusions can be strict. Spectral Jensen needs the middle space only.

D.3 Heat evolution and positivity

For \(A=A_V\ge-C+1\) as above, set \(S_V(t)=e^{-tA}\). It is a bounded self-adjoint operator for each fixed \(t\ge0\) with \(\|S_V(t)\|\le e^{Ct}\). The scalar exponential multiplication law gives the semigroup identity. For \(v\in\mathscr X\) and \(0\le t\le1\), the spectral norm of \(S_V(t)v-v\) satisfies \(\|S_V(t)v-v\|^2=\int|e^{-t\ell}-1|^2\,d\mathfrak m_v(\ell)\). On the spectrum the integrand is bounded by \((e^{|C|}+1)^2\). The finite scalar measure and pointwise limit zero give strong continuity at zero by dominated convergence. The semigroup identity gives continuity at all later times. For \(t>0\), \(S_V(t)f\in D(A)\), since \(\ell^2e^{-2t\ell}\) is bounded on the spectrum. Differentiation in the spectral norm on compact positive-time intervals gives \(\partial_tS_V(t)f=-AS_V(t)f\).

For \(T_0>0\) and \(f\in L^2\), use Tonelli and (D.1) to obtain

\[\int_0^{T_0}\|(A+C)^{1/2}S_V(t)f\|^2dt\le e^{2CT_0}\int\int_0^{T_0}(\ell+C)e^{-2t(\ell+C)}dt\,d\rho_f(\ell)\le \tfrac12e^{2CT_0}\|f\|^2.\tag{D.2}\]

Thus the evolution lies in the Bochner space \(L^2(0,T_0;\mathscr V)\). For positive times its derivative is the form functional \(-q_V(S_V(t)f,\cdot)\); the boundedness of the form on \(\mathscr V\) gives a derivative in \(L^2(0,T_0;\mathscr V^*)\) distributionally on the open interval. Strong continuity at zero supplies its initial value. The weak evolution uniqueness in Appendix B.6 and the identification in §7.2 show that for compact continuous nonnegative data it equals the positive bounded integral construction. In particular \(S_V(t)f\ge P_tf\ge0\).

For general nonnegative \(L^2\) data, approximate by nonnegative compact continuous functions in \(L^2\) (truncate and mollify a nonnegative extension). For each fixed \(t\), boundedness of both operators gives \(L^2\) convergence; the nonnegative cone is closed in \(L^2\). This proves domination and preservation of order in the full \(L^2\) space. Pointwise assertions in Chapter 8 are made only after identifying continuous representatives or evaluating the positive free kernel.

Self-adjointness is not order preservation

For example, the real symmetric matrix \(A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) is self-adjoint, but \(e^{-tA}=\cosh(t)I-\sinh(t)A\) maps \((1,0)\) to a vector with negative second component for \(t>0\). For the Robin operators, order preservation and free-flow domination come instead from the positive integral iteration of Chapter 7 and density in \(L^2\). On spectral values \(\ell<0\), the multiplier \(e^{-t\ell}\) grows; this fact by itself gives no spatial positivity or pointwise propagation.

D.4 Relation to logarithmic testing

The revised thesis also explains an older variational mechanism. For a smooth strictly positive regularized solution \(v\) and a compact smooth real test \(\psi\) on the closed half-space, assume the local smoothness needed to differentiate and integrate by parts. Then \((\log v)_t=\Delta\log v+|\nabla\log v|^2\) and \(\partial_\nu\log v=V\). Multiply by \(\psi^2\) and integrate:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathcal H}\psi^2\log v\,dx &=\int_{\partial\mathcal H}V\psi^2d\sigma-2\int_{\mathcal H}\psi\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\log v+\int_{\mathcal H}\psi^2|\nabla\log v|^2\\&=-q_V[\psi]+\int_{\mathcal H}|\psi\nabla\log v-\nabla\psi|^2\,dx\ge -q_V[\psi].\end{aligned}\tag{D.3}\]

This explains why negative energy forces logarithmic growth in a suitable average. It is not alone a pointwise divergence proof. The spectral argument in Chapter 8 provides the required propagation and limit without assuming a smooth singular solution. The original Ishige–Ishiwata proof develops additional estimates for its truncation method; it is not reproduced here.
