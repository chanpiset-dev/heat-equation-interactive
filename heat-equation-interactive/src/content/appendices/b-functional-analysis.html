Appendix B · Functional analysis

When to use this appendix. Use B.1–B.3 before the energy identity, B.4–B.8 for weak evolution and local estimates, and B.9 before the weighted contraction.

A reference for the energy and existence arguments, including the Hilbert-triple chain rule, form evolution theorem and Sobolev inequality used in Chapter 7.

B.1 Lebesgue spaces and energy norms

Lebesgue spaces. Use Lebesgue measure on the half-space \(\mathcal H\). For \(1\le p<\infty\), \(L^p(\mathcal H)\) consists of measurable functions of finite norm, modulo equality almost everywhere. The norm is

\[\|f\|_{L^p(\mathcal{H})}=\left(\int_{\mathcal{H}}|f|^p\,dx\right)^{1/p}.\]

For \(p=\infty\), the norm is the essential supremum. The real Hilbert space \(L^2(\mathcal{H})\) has inner product \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{H}} fg\,dx\). We write \(\|\cdot\|_2\) for its norm unless a different domain is specified. The squared norm is an energy observable, not the total heat \(\int_{\mathcal{H}}u\).

B.2 Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces

Weak derivative. For \(f,g_j\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal H)\), write \(g_j=\partial_j f\) if

\[\int_{\mathcal H}f\,\partial_j\varphi\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal H}g_j\varphi\,dx \qquad(\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\mathcal H)).\]

First Sobolev space. Require \(f\) and all first weak derivatives to be square integrable.

\[H^1(\mathcal{H})=\{f\in L^2(\mathcal{H}):\partial_jf\in L^2(\mathcal{H}),\ 1\le j\le N\},\quad \|f\|_{H^1}^2=\|f\|_2^2+\|\nabla f\|_2^2.\]

For example, \(f(x)=e^{-|x|^2}\) belongs to \(H^1(\mathcal{H})\) since both the function and its first derivatives are square integrable.

B.3 Boundary traces

Values of an \(L^2\) equivalence class cannot simply be restricted to a set of zero volume measure. The continuous trace operator \(\operatorname{Tr}:H^1(\mathcal{H})\to L^2(\partial \mathcal{H})\) extends boundary restriction for smooth functions.

Lemma B.1 · Elementary half-space trace estimate (paper (3.1))
\[\|\operatorname{Tr}w\|_{L^2(\partial \mathcal{H})}^2\le2\|w\|_2\|\partial_N w\|_2\le\eta\|\nabla w\|_2^2+\eta^{-1}\|w\|_2^2,\quad\eta>0.\tag{B.1}\]
Proof of the half-space trace bound

For a smooth \(w\) compactly supported on the closed half-space, integrate \(-\partial_N |w|^2\) along each normal ray and then integrate over the boundary:

\[\begin{aligned} \|w(\cdot,0)\|_{L^2(\partial\mathcal H)}^2 &=-2\int_{\mathcal H} w\,\partial_Nw\,dx\\ &\le 2\|w\|_2\|\partial_Nw\|_2\\ &\le\eta\|\nabla w\|_2^2+\eta^{-1}\|w\|_2^2. \end{aligned}\]

The last steps are Cauchy–Schwarz and the adjustable Young inequality. Reflection, cutoff and mollification give density in \(H^1(\mathcal H)\); (B.1) therefore extends boundary restriction by continuity.

Why we need this. The Robin term depends on boundary values, while the energy controls bulk quantities. The ordinary trace estimate handles bounded coefficients; Chapter 6 introduces the sharper weighted Kato inequality needed for the singular coefficient.

B.4 Strong and weak convergence

Strong convergence \(f_n\to f\) in a Hilbert space means \(\|f_n-f\|\to0\). Weak convergence \(f_n\rightharpoonup f\) means \(\langle f_n,g\rangle\to\langle f,g\rangle\) for every vector \(g\) in the space. Bounded sequences in a Hilbert space have weakly convergent subsequences, and

\[\|f\|\le\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|f_n\|\quad\text{if }f_n\rightharpoonup f.\]

If \(f_n\rightharpoonup f\) and \(f\ne0\), then \(\|f\|=\lim_n\langle f_n,f/\|f\|\rangle\le\liminf_n\|f_n\|\) in the real Hilbert space (take real parts in the complex case). For \(f=0\) use nonnegativity. An orthonormal sequence converges weakly to zero by Bessel's inequality while each norm stays one. The bound controls energy without giving pointwise convergence.

For a dual space \(X^*\), weak-* convergence means \(\ell_n(x)\to\ell(x)\) for every \(x\in X\); weak convergence in \(X^*\) instead tests against every element of \(X^{**}\). In a Hilbert space, the Riesz identification makes the weak topology of the dual agree with its weak-* topology.

B.5 Time-dependent spaces

For a Hilbert space \(\mathscr X\), \(L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\) is the space of strongly measurable \(\mathscr X\)-valued functions whose squared norm is integrable in time. In particular,

\[\|u\|_{L^2(0,T;H^1(\mathcal{H}))}^2=\int_0^T\big(\|u(t)\|_2^2+\|\nabla u(t)\|_2^2\big)\,dt.\]

These functions are defined up to equality almost everywhere in time. In a weak evolution argument one may take \(\mathscr X=H^1(\mathcal{H})\) and its continuous dual \(\mathscr X^*\), with the dense continuous embeddings \(\mathscr X\hookrightarrow L^2(\mathcal{H})\hookrightarrow \mathscr X^*\) via the \(L^2\) pairing. A time derivative interpreted in \(\mathscr X^*\) is weaker than a classical derivative. See B.6 for the precise weak evolution theorem and proof.

Why we need this. Strictly subcritical energy bounds control a time-integrated gradient, while at critical coupling that global gradient bound is not supplied by the same estimate. Local estimates off the singular graph are therefore particularly important.

B.6 Hilbert triples and measurable form evolution

Here the pivot space is a real Hilbert space \(Y\), and \(\mathscr X\subset Y\) is a separable Hilbert space with dense continuous inclusion. Identify \(Y\) with a subspace of \(\mathscr X^*\) using the \(Y\)-inner product. Write

\[\mathcal W=\{v\in L^2(0,T;\mathscr X):v_t\in L^2(0,T;\mathscr X^*)\},\]

where the derivative is a distribution in time. The basic Hilbert-triple result is that \(\mathcal W\) has a continuous embedding into \(C([0,T];Y)\), and

\[\|v(t)\|_Y^2-\|v(s)\|_Y^2=2\int_s^t\langle v_r,v\rangle_{\mathscr X^*,\mathscr X}\,dr.\tag{B.2}\]
Proof of the chain rule and continuity

Since \(v\) is in the ordinary Hilbert-valued space \(H^1(0,T;\mathscr X^*)\), it has continuous dual-space values and may be extended by time reflection past each endpoint. Reflection preserves both local \(L^2(\mathscr X)\) and the weak time derivative in \(L^2(\mathscr X^*)\), with no jumps. Convolve the extension in time with a smooth approximate identity. These smooth \(\mathscr X\)-valued functions converge in both defining norms of \(\mathcal W\). For any smooth difference \(z\) between two such approximations, choose a time \(s\) with \(\|z(s)\|_Y^2\le T^{-1}\int_0^T\|z\|_Y^2\). Integrating the classical squared-norm derivative from that time gives

\[\sup_t\|z(t)\|_Y^2\le T^{-1}\|z\|_{L^2(Y)}^2+2\|z_t\|_{L^2(\mathscr X^*)}\|z\|_{L^2(\mathscr X)}.\]

The approximations are therefore Cauchy in \(C([0,T];Y)\), and their limit represents \(v\). The dual products converge in \(L^1\) by Cauchy–Schwarz, so the classical identity passes to (B.2). Polarization gives the corresponding dual-pairing product rule for two functions in \(\mathcal W\). The ordinary Hilbert-valued fundamental theorem used for the reflection follows by integrating the weak derivative and identifying the difference as a constant distribution.

Theorem B.2 · Nonautonomous form evolution

Let \(a_t:\mathscr X\times\mathscr X\to\mathbb R\) be bilinear for \(t\in(0,T)\). Assume:

  1. For each \(v,w\in\mathscr X\), \(t\mapsto a_t(v,w)\) is measurable.
  2. With constants independent of \(t\), \(M\ge0\), \(c_0\in\mathbb R\), \(c_1>0\),
    \[|a_t(v,w)|\le M\|v\|_{\mathscr X}\|w\|_{\mathscr X}, \qquad a_t(w,w)+c_0\|w\|_Y^2\ge c_1\|w\|_{\mathscr X}^2.\]

For every \(f\in Y\) there is exactly one \(v\in\mathcal W\) such that

\[v(0)=f,\qquad \langle v_t(t),w\rangle+a_t(v(t),w)=0 \quad(w\in\mathscr X,\ \text{a.e. }t).\]
Galerkin proof of the form theorem

Choose a countable dense sequence in \(\mathscr X\), discard linear dependencies and Gram–Schmidt it in \(Y\). Its first \(m\) elements span a space \(E_m\), the union of which is dense in \(\mathscr X\) and \(Y\). Solve the finite-dimensional linear ODE on \(E_m\) using the projected initial value \(f_m\) (orthogonal projection in \(Y\)). The matrices are measurable and bounded for each \(m\); successive time integrals of the integral equation converge by the exponential series, giving an absolutely continuous solution. Testing with that solution gives

\[\tfrac12\frac d{dt}\|v_m\|_Y^2+c_1\|v_m\|_{\mathscr X}^2\le c_0\|v_m\|_Y^2.\]

Multiplication by \(e^{-2\max(c_0,0)t}\) bounds the volume norm and integrated \(\mathscr X\) norm uniformly. Take a weak subsequential limit in \(L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\). Test the ODE against any fixed basis vector times a smooth scalar time function vanishing at \(T\), then integrate by parts in time. For large \(m\) that vector belongs to \(E_m\). Both terms are continuous linear functionals of the weakly convergent sequence, by the uniform form bound and the continuous pivot embedding. The initial term tends to \((f,w)_Y\) since \(f_m\to f\) in \(Y\). Density extends this integrated identity to every \(w\in\mathscr X\). In particular the distributional time derivative equals \(-a_t(v,\cdot)\in L^2(0,T;\mathscr X^*)\) (strong measurability follows by approximating the strongly measurable \(v\) by simple \(\mathscr X\)-valued functions: for fixed vectors, the weakly measurable \(\mathscr X^*\)-valued form map is strongly measurable since the dual of a separable Hilbert space is separable; the uniform bound then permits passage to the pointwise norm limit). No uniform dual-norm bound on the projected derivatives is needed. Apply (B.2) to get the continuous representative, and integrate its pairing with fixed vectors to identify its initial trace as \(f\). The energy inequality for the difference of two solutions and the same exponential factor give uniqueness. This proves the theorem in the case used in §7.2.

B.7 The Sobolev gain used in Moser iteration

For \(N\ge3\), the Euclidean Sobolev inequality is

\[\|g\|_{2N/(N-2)}\le C_N\|\nabla g\|_2\qquad(g\in H^1(\mathbb R^N)).\]

First establish \(\|F\|_{N/(N-1)}\le C_N\sum_i\|\partial_iF\|_1\) for \(F\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^N)\), using the coordinate-line argument below.

Derivation of the spatial and parabolic inequalities

Along each coordinate line, the fundamental theorem bounds \(|F(x)|\) by \(A_i(\widehat x_i)=\int_{\mathbb R}|\partial_i F(x)|dx_i\). Multiply the \(N\) bounds after raising each to power \(1/(N-1)\). The integral of \(\prod_i A_i(\widehat x_i)^{1/(N-1)}\) is at most \(\prod_i(\int A_i)^{1/(N-1)}\). To see this last product inequality, integrate in \(x_N\) first, using Hölder with \(N-1\) factors other than \(A_N\). Then apply Hölder with exponents \(N-1\) and \((N-1)/(N-2)\) to separate \(A_N^{1/(N-1)}\) from the other product, and apply the same inequality in dimension \(N-1\) to the functions \(\int A_i\,dx_N\), \(i<N\). The base case \(N=2\) is Fubini. Taking the power \((N-1)/N\) and the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives the stated \(L^1\) derivative bound.

Take \(g\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^N)\) and set \(p=2N/(N-2)\) and \(a=2(N-1)/(N-2)\). Apply the derivative bound to \(|g|^a\) (using smooth approximation), then apply Cauchy–Schwarz:

\[\begin{aligned} \|g\|_p^a&\le C_N\int_{\mathbb R^N}|g|^{a-1}|\nabla g|\,dx\\ &\le C_N\|g\|_p^{a-1}\|\nabla g\|_2,\qquad 2(a-1)=p . \end{aligned}\]

Cancel the common power if the norm is nonzero. Density and Fatou extend the bound to \(H^1\); even reflection gives the half-space version. For \(g\in L^\infty(I;L^2)\cap L^2(I;H^1)\) on the half-space, spatial Hölder followed by Sobolev gives

\[\begin{aligned} \int_I\int_{\mathcal H}|g|^{2+4/N}\,dx\,dt &\le \int_I\|g\|_2^{4/N}\|g\|_{2N/(N-2)}^2\,dt\\ &\le C_N\Big(\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{t\in I}\|g(t)\|_2^2\Big)^{2/N} \int_I\|\nabla g\|_2^2\,dt . \end{aligned}\]

This is the exponent gain in §7.4.

B.8 Time averages and nonlinear tests

The concrete Hilbert triple in Chapter 7 is \(H^1(\mathcal H)\hookrightarrow L^2(\mathcal H)\hookrightarrow (H^1(\mathcal H))^*\). For example, a square-integrable function \(g\) acts by \(w\mapsto\int_{\mathcal H}gw\), a bounded linear functional even if \(g\) has no weak spatial derivative. The weak time derivative in this dual space is characterized by

\[\int_0^T\langle v_t(t),w\rangle\zeta(t)dt=-\int_0^T(v(t),w)_2\zeta'(t)dt\quad(w\in H^1(\mathcal H),\ \zeta\in C_c^\infty(0,T)).\]
Steklov averages

On an open time interval \(I\), define \(v_\ell(t)=\ell^{-1}\int_t^{t+\ell}v(s)ds\) whenever the integration interval lies in \(I\). If \(v\in L^2_{\mathrm{loc}}(I;X)\) for a Hilbert space \(X\), the averages converge strongly to \(v\) in \(L^2(J;X)\) for every compact subinterval \(J\subset I\), and their derivative is \((v(t+\ell)-v(t))/\ell\) there in the distributional sense.

Proof and application to the boundary

The derivative formula follows by testing the moving integral against a smooth time function and changing the order of integration. For convergence, restrict to a slightly larger compact time interval, extend by zero, and use continuity of translations in \(L^2(\mathbb R;X)\), proved first for continuous compactly supported functions and then by density. Minkowski's inequality bounds the difference by the average of the translation differences. With \(X=H^1\) locally, the trace map is continuous, so averages and traces commute and the traces converge strongly in boundary space-time \(L^2\). The averaged variational inequality follows by integrating the original one against translated nonnegative tests. For fixed \(\ell\), the averaged time derivative is locally in \(L^2\) with spatial values, permitting the ordinary chain rule for bounded powers.

Chain rules and admissible nonnegative tests

For a bounded real Sobolev function \(w\) and a continuously differentiable function \(F\) with bounded derivative on the range, \(\nabla F(w)=F'(w)\nabla w\) locally. The same rule holds for the positive part, with derivative \(1_{w>0}\nabla w\), and for \(\max(w,M_0)\).

Justification used in the Moser and truncation arguments

Extend \(F\) outside the bounded range with bounded derivative. On compact patches, approximate \(w\) in \(H^1\) by smooth bounded functions using reflection across the plane and local mollification. The Lipschitz bound gives convergence of the values; the derivative difference splits into \(F'(w_j)\nabla(w_j-w)+(F'(w_j)-F'(w))\nabla w\). Strong convergence and dominated convergence along an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence give the gradient rule. For truncations, approximate by smooth Lipschitz functions with derivatives converging away from the truncation level. The gradient vanishes almost everywhere on any fixed level set: along almost every coordinate line a Sobolev function is absolutely continuous, whose derivative is zero almost everywhere on a level set (at density points where the derivative exists). Fubini gives the assertion for each coordinate. This justifies the limiting derivative even at the corner. Time chain rules for the averaged functions follow by the same absolutely continuous line argument in time. Nonnegative bounded tests with compact support in the spatial ball and open time interval can be approximated by nonnegative smooth tests: extend evenly across the flat plane and by zero outside the support, then convolve with nonnegative smooth kernels, using a slightly larger compact support still inside the cylinder. For the averaged inequality only spatial \(H^1\) and space-time \(L^2\) convergence are needed after the time derivative is moved to the average; bounded flux and trace continuity justify passage. Upper-time indicator cutoffs are handled by smooth approximations and almost-everywhere endpoint limits. These details justify the nonlinear test in §7.4 and the convex truncation in §7.5.

B.9 Complete normed spaces and contraction mapping

A Banach space is a normed vector space complete for its norm metric. On a nonempty measurable space \((E,\mathcal E)\), the bounded measurable real functions form a Banach space for the pointwise norm \(\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in E}|f(x)|\).

Proof of completeness in the supremum norm

Let \((f_n)\) be Cauchy in the supremum norm. Then \(f_n(x)\) converges for every \(x\), and its pointwise limit \(f\) is measurable. For every \(\varepsilon>0\), take \(n,m\) sufficiently large that \(\sup_{x\in E}|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|<\varepsilon\). Let \(m\to\infty\) at each \(x\), then take the supremum to get \(\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon\). In particular, \(f\) is bounded. ∎

In Definition 9.1, the strictly positive Borel weight \(\Phi_\alpha\) makes \(W\mapsto W/\Phi_\alpha\) an isometric bijection onto the bounded Borel functions on \(D_0(I)\). Its inverse multiplies by \(\Phi_\alpha\). The pointwise norm retains boundary values.

Contraction mapping theorem

Let \((X,d)\) be a nonempty complete metric space and \(T:X\to X\) satisfy \(d(Tv,Tw)\le qd(v,w)\) for all \(v,w\in X\), where \(0\le q<1\). Then \(T\) has a unique fixed point \(v\). From any \(v_0\in X\), the iterates \(v_{j+1}=Tv_j\) satisfy

\[d(v_j,v)\le\frac{q^j}{1-q}d(v_1,v_0),\qquad j\ge0.\]
Proof of the contraction mapping theorem

By induction \(d(v_{\ell+1},v_\ell)\le q^\ell d(v_1,v_0)\). For \(k>j\),

\[\begin{aligned} d(v_k,v_j)&\le\sum_{\ell=j}^{k-1}d(v_{\ell+1},v_\ell)\\ &\le d(v_1,v_0)\sum_{\ell=j}^{k-1}q^\ell \le \frac{q^j}{1-q}d(v_1,v_0). \end{aligned}\]

Thus the sequence is Cauchy. Completeness gives \(v\), continuity of \(T\) gives \(Tv=v\), and two fixed points have distance at most \(q\) times that distance. Let \(k\to\infty\) for the claimed error bound. ∎

Affine fixed point

Let \(F\) belong to a Banach space and let \(A\) be bounded linear on that space with \(\|A\|\le q<1\). Then \(T(v)=F+Av\) has a unique fixed point, with

\[v=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A^k F,\qquad \left\|v-\sum_{k=0}^j A^kF\right\|\le\frac{q^{j+1}}{1-q}\|F\|\qquad(j\ge0).\]

In a function space with a norm-closed cone of nonnegative functions, the fixed point is nonnegative if \(F\ge0\) and \(A\) preserves that cone.

Derivation of the affine series and its tail

Iteration from zero gives the partial sums, and \(\|A^kF\|\le q^k\|F\|\) gives absolute convergence and the stated tail. Continuity of \(A\) permits passing to the limit in the recurrence. Since \(T\) has Lipschitz constant at most \(q\), the contraction theorem gives uniqueness. In \(X_\alpha(I)\), the cone is closed because \(|W_n(x,t)-W(x,t)|\le\Phi_\alpha(x)\|W_n-W\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\). If \(F\ge0\) and \(A\) preserves that cone, each term is nonnegative, hence \(v\ge0\). See the weighted Picard construction. ∎
