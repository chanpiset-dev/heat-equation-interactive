Appendix E

Appendix E · Probability and stochastic calculus

When to use this appendix. Read E.1–E.3 for stochastic derivations and E.4 for the numerical sampling algorithm. The foundational probability results are identified separately from their application.

Probability prerequisites for Chapter 10. Construction of Wiener measure, the Brownian strong Markov property, the reflection principle, Doob's maximal inequality and the general Itô formula are standard stochastic-calculus inputs (see [8]); they are not consequences of the heat estimates. The derivations below explain the particular normalization and formulas used in this exposition.

E.1 Probability spaces, conditioning and martingales

A probability space consists of a sample space \(\Omega_{\rm prob}\), a sigma-algebra \(\mathcal F\) and a countably additive measure \(\mathbb P\) of total mass one. A random variable is a measurable function, and its law is the pushforward of \(\mathbb P\). Expectation is integration against this measure. A filtration \((\mathcal F_s)\) is an increasing family of sigma-algebras, representing information available by time s. We use the completed right-continuous Brownian filtration. A process is adapted if its value at each s is \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable. A stopping time τ satisfies \(\{\tau\le s\}\in\mathcal F_s\) for every s. Conditional expectation \(\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]\) is the \(\mathcal G\)-measurable integrable variable with equal integrals to Y on every set in \(\mathcal G\), determined almost surely. In particular, it preserves positivity and obeys the tower rule.

A martingale is an adapted integrable process M such that \(\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s\) for s≤t. A local martingale has a sequence of stopping times increasing to infinity for which the stopped processes are martingales. To remove localization on a bounded horizon it is sufficient that the stopped values converge and are dominated by a common integrable random variable; dominated convergence in the martingale expectation identity then gives equality of endpoint expectations. The strong Markov property of Brownian motion states that after an almost surely finite stopping time the future increments are Brownian and independent of the stopped past. The same assertion on the event τ<t follows by stopping at τ∧t. Restarting the deterministic reflection map transfers this property to X and the future increments of L.

E.2 Stochastic integrals and the Itô correction

For a simple adapted integrand \(J_r=\sum_j J_j\mathbf1_{(s_j,s_{j+1}]}(r)\), with square-integrable \(\mathcal F_{s_j}\)-measurable J_j, define \(\int_0^t J_r\cdot dW_r\) by the sum of J_j dotted with the corresponding stopped Brownian increments (truncate the last interval at t). Independence and zero means of successive increments give zero expectation; the cross terms vanish by conditioning on the later left endpoint, and covariance gives

\[\mathbb E\left|\int_0^t J_r\cdot dW_r\right|^2=\mathbb E\int_0^t |J_r|^2\,dr.\]

Completion extends the integral by this isometry to square-integrable predictable integrands, using density of simple predictable processes (predictability is measurability for the sigma-algebra generated by adapted left-continuous processes). The conditional expectation property of simple sums passes to the L² limit. Locally square-integrable integrands are defined by stopping. We use Doob's L² inequality \(\mathbb E\sup_{s\le t}|M_s|^2\le4\mathbb E|M_t|^2\) for continuous square-integrable martingales.

Along deterministic partitions with mesh tending to zero the sums \(\sum\Delta W_i\Delta W_j\) converge in L² to \(t\delta_{ij}\), since independence makes their variance O(mesh) on a fixed time interval. A continuous finite-variation path has sums of squared increments tending to zero, bounded by its total variation times its largest increment. Mixed sums tend to zero in probability by Cauchy–Schwarz. The second-order Taylor expansion for a smooth f of \(X=x+\sqrt2W+e_NL\) therefore retains the second-order Brownian contribution \(\Delta f\,ds\) while the regulator contributes its first-order Stieltjes term. More precisely, the standard Itô formula for continuous semimartingales (with localization to compact sets and the time-dependent first derivative for a C¹,² function) gives (10.4). The Taylor argument explains the coefficient; it is not intended as a proof of the general stochastic calculus theorem.

E.3 Reflection principle and exponential moments

For a Brownian motion starting at a, reflect the portion of a path after its first hit of level m≤a. By strong Markov and symmetry the reflected future has the same law. Thus the paths crossing m and ending at b≥m correspond in law to paths ending at 2m−b≤m. For the variance-2 driver the density of the latter endpoint is \(g_t^{(1)}(a+b-2m)\). This establishes the crossing formula of §10.2. Integrating it, or applying symmetry at zero start, gives the maximum of \(-\sqrt2 W_s\) up to t the law \(\sqrt{2t}|Z_0|\), where Z₀ is standard normal. The regulator is its excess above the initial height. The union bound and the reflection principle also give \(\mathbb P(\sup_{s\le t}|W_s|>r)\le4\mathbb P(W_t>r)\) for one coordinate and r>0. For each finite b and finite T, Gaussian tails imply \(\mathbb E e^{b\sqrt2\sup_{s\le T}|W_s|}<\infty\) for b≥0, by integrating the tail. This bound is independent of the nonnegative starting height.

E.4 Sampling a Brownian-bridge minimum

For a variance-2 Brownian driver starting at a and ending at b after a step Δs, the conditional probability that its minimum m_step is at most m≤min(a,b) is the ratio of the crossing density to the unrestricted density:

\[\mathbb P(m_{\rm step}\le m\mid a,b)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{(a-m)(b-m)}{\Delta s}\right).\]

Invert this distribution using an independent uniform random variable U in (0,1). The smaller root of the quadratic equation is

\[m_{\rm step}=\frac{a+b-\sqrt{(a-b)^2-4\Delta s\log U}}2.\]

Conditional on the grid endpoints the bridge segments are independent by the Gaussian increment law, so independent uniforms give the correct conditional minima. Updating the running minimum and applying (10.2) gives the exact joint grid-time law of the normal endpoint and regulator. Tangential increments are independent Gaussians of variance 2Δs. This samples minima, not their exact occurrence times or locations along the tangential Brownian path; for variable V the right-endpoint Stieltjes sum still has discretization error. On any common continuous path its error is bounded by the modulus of continuity of \(s\mapsto V((X_s',0),t-s)\) at the mesh times L_t. This tends to zero pathwise. For bounded V and f the exponential-tail bound in E.3 supplies an integrable dominator, giving convergence of expectations as the mesh decreases. The Monte Carlo estimator at a fixed mesh is an ordinary sample mean of independent path functionals. Its variance is the single-path variance divided by the sample count, finite by the same bound with twice the coefficient. The usual sample variance estimates sampling uncertainty, not bias of the Stieltjes approximation. The browser uses seeded pseudorandom uniform draws and Box–Muller normals, approximating ideal independent draws.

E.4 supplement · Joint laws and paired estimators

Continuous process laws on path space are determined by finite-dimensional distributions at rational times: rational evaluations determine continuous functions and generate the Borel sigma-algebra of the uniform topology on compact time intervals. One-time laws alone do not determine joint laws. Gaussian increment grids reproduce the Brownian finite-dimensional distributions; bridge minima give additional exact regulator grid laws, not full within-step paths.

To couple meshes, use fine driver endpoints and conditional minima, then running minima. A minimum over a coarse interval is the smallest of its constituent minima. Restricting endpoints and regulators to the coarse grid therefore gives their correct joint coarse law. For paired differences Dᵢ, the standard error of their average is the sample standard deviation of Dᵢ divided by √n, not the difference of the two independent standard errors. Equation (10.11) and dominated convergence establish mesh convergence for bounded data and fixed bounded regularization. The numerical experiment assesses finite-resolution sensitivity, not the unknown remaining bias.

For iid Yᵢ with finite variance, expansion of the square of \(\bar Y-\mathbb EY\) gives variance \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)/n\). Expanding \(\sum_i(Y_i-\bar Y)^2\) about the true mean gives expectation \((n-1)\operatorname{Var}(Y)\), proving unbiasedness of the usual sample variance. The law of large numbers and central limit theorem concern increasing sample counts, not guaranteed small error in a given sample. Strong exponential weights may be poorly sampled.
