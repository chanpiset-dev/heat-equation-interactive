Appendix E

Appendix E · Probability and stochastic calculus

When to use this appendix. Read E.1–E.3 for stochastic derivations and E.4 for the numerical sampling algorithm. The foundational probability results are identified separately from their application.

Probability prerequisites for Chapter 10. Construction of Wiener measure, the Brownian strong Markov property, the reflection principle, Doob's maximal inequality and the general Itô formula are standard stochastic-calculus inputs (see [8]); they are not consequences of the heat estimates. The derivations below explain the particular normalization and formulas used in this exposition.

E.1 Probability spaces, conditioning and martingales

On a probability space \((\Omega_{\rm prob},\mathcal F,\mathbb P)\), a random variable is a measurable map. For a real-valued \(Y\), its law is \(Y_\#\mathbb P\), given by \((Y_\#\mathbb P)(B)=\mathbb P(Y\in B)\) for Borel sets \(B\).

Filtration, adaptedness and stopping times

A filtration \((\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a family of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras satisfying

\[\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_t\subseteq\mathcal F\qquad(0\le s\le t).\]

We use the completed, right-continuous Brownian filtration. A process \(Y=(Y_t)_{t\ge0}\) is adapted if \(Y_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable for every \(t\ge0\). A random variable \(\tau:\Omega_{\rm prob}\to[0,\infty]\) is a stopping time if

\[\{\tau\le t\}\in\mathcal F_t\qquad(t\ge0).\]

Conditional expectation

For \(Y\in L^1(\mathbb P)\) and a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F\), the conditional expectation \(Z=\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]\) is the almost surely unique integrable, \(\mathcal G\)-measurable variable satisfying

\[\int_A Z\,d\mathbb P=\int_A Y\,d\mathbb P\qquad(A\in\mathcal G).\]

Positivity and the tower property give, almost surely,

\[\begin{aligned} Y\ge0&\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]\ge0,\\ \mathbb E[\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G]\mid\mathcal G_0] &=\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal G_0]\qquad(\mathcal G_0\subseteq\mathcal G). \end{aligned}\]

The first assertion follows by testing on \(\{Z<0\}\). For the second, the two sides have the same integrals over all events of \(\mathcal G_0\).

Martingales and localization

An adapted process \(M=(M_t)_{t\ge0}\) is a martingale if \(M_t\in L^1(\mathbb P)\) for all \(t\ge0\) and

\[\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s\quad\text{a.s.}\qquad(0\le s\le t).\]

It is a local martingale if there are stopping times \(\tau_n\uparrow\infty\) almost surely such that each stopped process \(M_t^{\tau_n}=M_{t\wedge\tau_n}\) is a martingale.

To pass to the limit at fixed endpoints \(s\le t\), suppose the stopped values at both endpoints are bounded in absolute value by a common integrable random variable. Since they converge almost surely, dominated convergence gives

\[\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A M_t] =\lim_n\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A M_{t\wedge\tau_n}] =\lim_n\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A M_{s\wedge\tau_n}] =\mathbb E[\mathbf1_A M_s]\qquad(A\in\mathcal F_s).\]

Taking \(A=\Omega_{\rm prob}\) recovers equality of endpoint expectations.

Brownian strong Markov property

If \(W\) is standard Brownian motion and \(\tau\) is an almost surely finite stopping time for its usual filtration, then \((W_{\tau+r}-W_\tau)_{r\ge0}\) is standard Brownian motion started at zero and is independent of the stopped past \(\mathcal F_\tau\). For a possibly infinite \(\tau\), apply the result to \(\tau\wedge t\) on the event \(\{\tau<t\}\).

For the reflected process, restart the deterministic reflection map at \(X_\tau\) using the new increments. This yields \(X_{\tau+r}\) and the regulator increments \(L_{\tau+r}-L_\tau\) without using the pre-\(\tau\) path beyond its endpoint.

E.2 Stochastic integrals and the Itô correction

Let \(W\) be standard \(N\)-dimensional Brownian motion with increments independent of the past filtration. Fix \(0=t_0<\cdots<t_m=T\) and \(J_k\in L^2(\Omega_{\rm prob},\mathcal F_{t_k},\mathbb P;\mathbb R^N)\). For the simple predictable integrand \(J_s=\sum_k J_k\mathbf1_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(s)\), set

\[I_t(J)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}J_k\cdot (W_{t_{k+1}\wedge t}-W_{t_k\wedge t}),\qquad 0\le t\le T.\]

Itô isometry. The process \(I_t(J)\) is a centered square-integrable martingale, and

\[\mathbb E|I_t(J)|^2=\mathbb E\int_0^t|J_s|^2\,ds.\]
Proof of the isometry for simple integrands

First take \(t=T\). Write \(\Delta W_k=W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\), \(\Delta t_k=t_{k+1}-t_k\) and \(Z_k=J_k\cdot\Delta W_k\). Independence and the covariance of the centered Gaussian increment give

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\Delta W_k\mid\mathcal F_{t_k}]&=0,\\ \mathbb E[\Delta W_k\Delta W_k^\top\mid\mathcal F_{t_k}]&=\Delta t_k I_N,\\ \mathbb E[Z_k^2\mid\mathcal F_{t_k}]&=\Delta t_k|J_k|^2. \end{aligned}\]

For \(k<\ell\), \(Z_k\) is \(\mathcal F_{t_\ell}\)-measurable and \(\mathbb E[Z_\ell\mid\mathcal F_{t_\ell}]=0\), so \(\mathbb E[Z_kZ_\ell]=0\). These products are integrable by Cauchy–Schwarz and the diagonal bound. Expanding the square leaves \(\sum_k\Delta t_k\mathbb E|J_k|^2\). Each summand of \(I_T(J)\) has mean zero. Refining the deterministic grid at \(s\le t\) gives the same isometry through \(t\) and \(\mathbb E[I_t(J)-I_s(J)\mid\mathcal F_s]=0\) by successive conditioning on the left endpoints. ∎

Square-integrable predictable integrands

The space \(\mathcal P_T^2\) consists of \(\mathbb R^N\)-valued predictable processes on \([0,T]\) with \(\|J\|_{\mathcal P_T^2}^2=\mathbb E\int_0^T |J_s|^2\,ds<\infty\), identified up to \(ds\otimes d\mathbb P\)-almost-everywhere equality. Predictability means measurability for the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by left-continuous adapted processes. Simple predictable processes are dense in this norm [8].

Extension by isometry. The integral extends uniquely by continuity to \(\mathcal P_T^2\), retains the isometry, and gives a square-integrable martingale on \([0,T]\).

Proof of extension and the martingale identity

Choose simple \(J^{(n)}\to J\) in \(\mathcal P_T^2\). By linearity and the simple isometry,

\[\mathbb E|I_T(J^{(n)})-I_T(J^{(m)})|^2 =\|J^{(n)}-J^{(m)}\|_{\mathcal P_T^2}^2\longrightarrow0.\]

Completeness of \(L^2(\mathbb P)\) gives the limit. Comparing two sequences by the same estimate gives its uniqueness. Continuity of the two norms gives \(\mathbb E|I_T(J)|^2=\|J\|_{\mathcal P_T^2}^2\). Applying the extension to \(J\mathbf1_{(0,t]}\) gives \(I_t(J)\). The approximating values converge in \(L^2\) at every \(t\), and the limit is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable. Since conditional expectation contracts \(L^2\), passing to the limit in \(\mathbb E[I_t(J^{(n)})\mid\mathcal F_s]=I_s(J^{(n)})\) proves the martingale identity. As usual we use the continuous version of the Brownian stochastic integral [8]. ∎

Localization of the stochastic integral

For predictable \(J\) with \(\int_0^t|J_s|^2 ds<\infty\) almost surely on every finite interval, the integral is defined consistently as a continuous local martingale. Use the stopping times

\[\tau_n=\inf\left\{t\ge0:\int_0^t|J_s|^2\,ds\ge n\right\}, \qquad \inf\varnothing=\infty.\]
Proof of consistency under localization

On an event of probability one, local square-integrability holds on every finite interval by checking integer horizons. The time integral is continuous, adapted and increasing. In the completed filtration \(\{\tau_n\le t\}=\{\int_0^t|J_s|^2ds\ge n\}\) up to null sets, and \(\tau_n\uparrow\infty\) almost surely. The stopped integrand \(J\mathbf1_{(0,\tau_n]}\) is predictable and its integrated square is at most \(n\). For \(m\ge n\), the identity for stopping a stochastic integral [8] gives

\[I_{t\wedge\tau_n}(J\mathbf1_{(0,\tau_m]}) =I_t(J\mathbf1_{(0,\tau_n]}).\]

The stopped continuous versions therefore agree on the overlapping intervals. At any fixed finite \(t\) they eventually agree with the localized value almost surely. This construction requires no global \(L^2\) bound for the unstopped integral. ∎

Doob's inequality

For any continuous square-integrable martingale \(M\) on \([0,t]\), Doob's \(L^2\) inequality gives

\[\mathbb E\sup_{0\le s\le t}|M_s|^2\le4\,\mathbb E|M_t|^2.\]

In the occupation-limit proof, this turns the terminal isometry bound for the stochastic error into a bound uniform over the whole time interval.

Quadratic variation of Brownian motion

Let \(W\) be standard \(N\)-dimensional Brownian motion, and fix \(t>0\). For a deterministic partition \(\pi=\{0=t_0<\cdots<t_m=t\}\), put \(\Delta t_k=t_{k+1}-t_k\), \(|\pi|=\max_k\Delta t_k\) and \(\Delta W_k^i=W^i_{t_{k+1}}-W^i_{t_k}\). For \(1\le i,j\le N\),

\[S_\pi^{ij}:=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Delta W_k^i\Delta W_k^j \xrightarrow[|\pi|\to0]{L^2(\mathbb P)} t\delta_{ij}.\]
Proof of the quadratic variation estimate

The summands on distinct intervals are independent. On one interval the product has mean \(\delta_{ij}\Delta t_k\). Its variance is \((\Delta t_k)^2\) when \(i\ne j\), using independence of the coordinates, and \(2(\Delta t_k)^2\) when \(i=j\), using the centered Gaussian fourth moment. Thus \(\mathbb E S_\pi^{ij}=t\delta_{ij}\) and

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E|S_\pi^{ij}-t\delta_{ij}|^2 &=(1+\delta_{ij})\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(\Delta t_k)^2\\ &\le 2t|\pi|\longrightarrow0. \end{aligned}\]

The estimate is at fixed \(t\) along deterministic partitions. It does not by itself give almost-sure convergence along arbitrary mesh sequences or uniform convergence in time.

Finite variation and mixed increments

For a continuous finite-variation process \(A\), the sums \(\sum_k|\Delta A_k|^2\) tend to zero pathwise and \(\sum_k\Delta W_k^i\Delta A_k\to0\) in probability at fixed \(t\).

Proof of the finite-variation and mixed bounds
\[\sum_k|\Delta A_k|^2 \le\max_k|\Delta A_k|\sum_k|\Delta A_k| \le\max_k|\Delta A_k|\,\operatorname{Var}_{[0,t]}(A)\longrightarrow0.\]

The maximum tends to zero by continuity on the compact time interval. Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[\left|\sum_k\Delta W_k^i\Delta A_k\right| \le \left(\sum_k|\Delta W_k^i|^2\right)^{1/2} \left(\sum_k|\Delta A_k|^2\right)^{1/2}.\]

The first factor is bounded in probability by the Brownian estimate. The second converges almost surely to zero, so their product converges in probability to zero. ∎

Reflected Itô correction

For \(X_t=x+\sqrt2 W_t+e_N L_t\), the continuous increasing regulator \(L\) has \([L,L]_t=[W^i,L]_t=0\). Consequently \([X^i,X^j]_t=2t\delta_{ij}\), and the second-order term in Itô's formula is \(\Delta f\,dt\).

Derivation of the reflected Itô coefficients

Expand the products of increments of \(X\). All terms involving \(\Delta L\) vanish in probability by the preceding bounds, leaving twice the Brownian bracket. For \(f\in C^{1,2}\), the continuous-semimartingale Itô formula [8] yields

\[\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}f\,d[X^i,X^j] =\sum_i\partial_{ii}f\,dt=\Delta f\,dt.\]

The first-order finite-variation term is \(\partial_N f\,dL\) and the explicit time dependence contributes \(\partial_t f\,dt\). A driver \(W\) alone would give \(\tfrac12\Delta\). The factor \(\sqrt2\) gives the generator in (10.4) with the regulator convention of (10.2). The increment computation identifies the coefficients in the cited general formula. ∎

E.3 Reflection principle and exponential moments

Reflection and endpoint density

For one-dimensional standard Brownian motion \(B\) started at zero and \(t>0\), let \(M_t=\sup_{0\le s\le t}B_s\). Then \(\mathbb P(M_t\ge r)=2\mathbb P(B_t\ge r)\) for \(r\ge0\). For the variance-two driver \(Z_s=a+\sqrt2 B_s\) and \(m\le a\), the density for crossing \(m\) and ending above it is

\[\mathbb P\left(\min_{s\le t}Z_s\le m,\ Z_t\in db\right) =g_t^{(1)}(a+b-2m)\,db,\quad b\ge m,\qquad g_t^{(1)}(z)=\frac{e^{-z^2/(4t)}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}.\]
Proof of reflection and the crossing density

For \(r>0\), let \(\tau_r=\inf\{s:B_s=r\}\). On \(\{\tau_r\le t\}\), reflect the increments after \(\tau_r\) using the strong Markov property and symmetry. Paths ending below \(r\) on this event are mapped to paths ending above \(r\), all of which hit \(r\). The event \(B_t=r\) has probability zero. Adding the two parts gives the reflection identity, with \(r=0\) following directly from symmetry of \(B_t\).

For \(Z\), reflect after the first hit of \(m\); an endpoint \(b\ge m\) is sent to \(2m-b\le m\). Since \(a\ge m\), any path ending strictly below \(m\) must hit it. The unrestricted density at \(2m-b\) is \(g_t^{(1)}(2m-b-a)=g_t^{(1)}(a+b-2m)\), and reflection has unit absolute Jacobian. When \(m=a\), the first hit is at time zero, and the same calculation applies. Endpoint equalities do not affect a density. This is the crossing formula used in §10.2. ∎

Maximum and regulator laws

For \(G\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(M_t\overset d=\sqrt t\,|G|\). With \(a=x_N\ge0\) and \(B=W^N\), the regulator of (10.2) has the marginal law

\[L_t=\left(\sup_{s\le t}(-\sqrt2 B_s)-a\right)_+ \ \overset d=\ (\sqrt{2t}|G|-a)_+.\]
Proof of the maximum and regulator laws

For \(r>0\), the reflection identity gives the same tail as \(|B_t|\). Taking limits at zero identifies the full law, and \(B_t\sim\mathcal N(0,t)\). Apply this to \(-B\) and use the positive-part formula for the regulator. This is a one-time law. The endpoint and running minimum share the same path and their joint law uses the crossing density. ∎

Exponential moments

With \(\Phi_{\rm G}\) the standard-normal distribution function, \(t\ge0\) and \(c\ge0\),

\[\mathbb E e^{cM_t}=2e^{c^2t/2}\Phi_{\rm G}(c\sqrt t),\qquad \mathbb E e^{cL_t}\le2 e^{c^2t}\Phi_{\rm G}(c\sqrt{2t}).\]

The bound is uniform in the initial normal height \(a\ge0\).

Proof of the exponential moment bounds

By the half-normal law, integrate \(2e^{c\sqrt t\,z-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) over \(z\ge0\). Completing the square gives the first formula. For \(L_t\), drop \(a\) in the positive part and use the maximum law with \(c\sqrt2\) in place of \(c\). The two-sided maximum also has finite exponential moments: for \(r>0\),

\[\mathbb P(\sup_{s\le t}|B_s|\ge r) \le\mathbb P(\sup_{s\le t}B_s\ge r)+\mathbb P(\sup_{s\le t}(-B_s)\ge r) =4\mathbb P(B_t\ge r).\]

Integrating the Gaussian tail against \(c e^{cr}\,dr\) gives finiteness for each \(c\ge0\), including for \(\sqrt2 B\) after rescaling \(c\). ∎

E.4 Sampling a Brownian-bridge minimum

Conditional law and inverse sampling

For a variance-two Brownian step of duration \(h>0\), condition on the endpoints \(a,b\). The minimum \(m_{\rm step}\) has conditional distribution

\[\mathbb P(m_{\rm step}\le m\mid a,b)= \exp\!\left(-\frac{(a-m)(b-m)}{h}\right), \qquad m\le\min(a,b).\]

The distribution function is one above \(\min(a,b)\). To sample it, take \(U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)\) and set

\[m_{\rm step}=\frac{a+b-\sqrt{(a-b)^2-4h\log U}}2.\]
Derivation of the bridge law and its inverse

Divide the crossing density from E.3 by the strictly positive endpoint density \(g_h^{(1)}(b-a)\). The exponents differ by

\[(a+b-2m)^2-(b-a)^2=4(a-m)(b-m).\]

The quotient gives an increasing distribution function in \(m\), tending to zero as \(m\to-\infty\) and to one at \(\min(a,b)\). Solving \((a-m)(b-m)=-h\log U\) gives two roots. The displayed smaller root is below \(\min(a,b)\) because \(-4h\log U>0\); the larger root is above \(\max(a,b)\). Equal endpoints still give a strictly positive radicand for \(0<U<1\). At \(U=1\) the inverse reaches \(\min(a,b)\), and \(U=0\) has no finite inverse. These are endpoint conventions for the ideal law. ∎

Joint exactness at grid times

For a deterministic grid \(0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=T\), sample the driver endpoints using independent Gaussian increments with variance \(2(t_{k+1}-t_k)\) in each coordinate. Given all normal endpoints, the Gaussian bridges in the disjoint steps are conditionally independent. Independent uniforms give their conditional minima. If \(m_k\) is the minimum on \([t_k,t_{k+1}]\), the sampled running minimum at \(t_j\) is \(\min\{x_N,m_0,\ldots,m_{j-1}\}\). Applying (10.2) gives the joint grid-time law of the driver, reflected endpoints and regulator. This does not sample the minimum occurrence times, the within-step reflected paths, or the tangential positions at unobserved contacts. More generally, continuous path laws are determined by the joint evaluations at rational times, whereas one-time marginals alone do not determine the joint law.

Boundary quadrature and convergence

Fix a bounded continuous coefficient \(V\) on the plane over the finite time interval, and let the terminal datum \(f\) be bounded and measurable. This includes the soft inverse-distance coefficient at fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) with continuous trajectory. On a common reflected path, set \(g(s)=V((X_s',0),T-s)\), let \(\omega_g\) be the modulus of continuity on \([0,T]\), and define

\[A=\int_0^Tg(s)\,dL_s,\qquad A_\pi=\sum_k g(t_{k+1})(L_{t_{k+1}}-L_{t_k}).\]

Then \(|A-A_\pi|\le\omega_g(|\pi|)L_T\) pathwise and \(\mathbb E[f(X_T)e^{A_\pi}]\to\mathbb E[f(X_T)e^A]\).

Proof of the Stieltjes error and expectation limit

The path \(g\) is continuous because the reflected tangential coordinate and the physical time argument are continuous. Subtract the right-endpoint value in each Stieltjes integral:

\[\begin{aligned} |A-A_\pi|&\le\sum_k\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} |g(s)-g(t_{k+1})|\,dL_s\\ &\le \omega_g(|\pi|)L_T . \end{aligned}\]

Here \(L\) is increasing with \(L_0=0\). Continuity of \(g\) makes the last bound vanish as \(|\pi|\to0\). Also \(|A_\pi|\le\|V\|_\infty L_T\), so \(\|f\|_\infty e^{\|V\|_\infty L_T}\) dominates all weighted terms and is integrable by E.3. Dominated convergence gives the expectation limit, using no continuity of \(f\) because \(X_T\) is fixed across meshes on the common path. The argument is for fixed bounded regularization; see §10.7 for the quadrature estimate with a quantitative modulus. ∎

Sampling uncertainty and paired meshes

At a fixed mesh take independent identically distributed copies \(Y_i\) of \(f(X_T)e^{A_\pi}\). Their variance is finite by the same exponential bound with twice the coefficient. For \(n\ge2\), define

\[\widehat\mu_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY_i,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat\mu_n)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(Y_1)}n,\qquad s_Y^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\mu_n)^2.\]
Derivation of the variance and standard error

Independence cancels covariances between draws. Subtract \(n(\widehat\mu_n-\mu)^2\) from \(\sum_i(Y_i-\mu)^2\), with \(\mu=\mathbb E Y_1\), to get \(\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\mu_n)^2\). Taking expectations gives \((n-1)\operatorname{Var}(Y_1)\), so \(s_Y^2\) is unbiased. The estimated standard error is \(s_Y/\sqrt n\). The law of large numbers and central limit theorem concern limits as the sample count increases. Large exponential weights can be poorly sampled at finite sample sizes. ∎

For nested grids, compute a coarse step minimum as the smallest fine minimum in that step. Restricting the fine endpoints and running minima to the coarse grid preserves their joint coarse law. For independent pairs, use \(D_i=Y_i^{\rm fine}-Y_i^{\rm coarse}\) and estimate the variance of \(\bar D\) by \(s_D^2/n\). Indeed

\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar D)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_1)}n,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(D_1)=\operatorname{Var}(Y_1^{\rm fine})+ \operatorname{Var}(Y_1^{\rm coarse})- 2\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1^{\rm fine},Y_1^{\rm coarse}).\]

The paired statistic measures the difference between the chosen meshes. Standard error describes sampling uncertainty, while time-discretization bias is the difference between the mesh expectation and the continuous expectation. Floating-point error is separate from both. The browser uses seeded pseudorandom uniforms and Box–Muller normals as numerical substitutes for the ideal draws.
