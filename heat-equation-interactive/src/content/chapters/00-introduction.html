Part I

0. Introduction

0.1 The governing initial-boundary value problem

Let \(N\ge3\) be the spatial dimension and \(T>0\) the time horizon. Write \(x=(x',x_N)\in\mathbb R^N\), with \(x'\in\mathbb R^{N-1}\). The fixed upper half-space is \(\mathcal H=\{x:x_N>0\}\) and its boundary \(\partial\mathcal H=\{x:x_N=0\}\). Let \(\xi:[0,T]\to\partial\mathcal H\) be a continuous prescribed path. The outward normal is \(\nu=-e_N\), where \(e_N\) is the last coordinate unit vector.

Initial-boundary value problem
\[\boxed{\begin{cases}\partial_tu=\Delta u &\text{in }\mathcal H\times(0,T),\\[3pt]\displaystyle\partial_\nu u=\frac{\lambda}{|x-\xi(t)|}u &\text{on }\Sigma_\xi,\\[3pt]u(x,0)=u_0(x)&\text{in }\mathcal H.\end{cases}}\]

Here \(\Sigma_\xi=\{(x,t)\in\partial\mathcal H\times(0,T):x\ne\xi(t)\}\). The coefficient \(\lambda/|x-\xi(t)|\) is undefined at \(x=\xi(t)\), so the Robin condition is written only for boundary points \(x\ne\xi(t)\). The integral formulation in Chapter 2 specifies what is meant by a solution.

SymbolMeaning
\(u(x,t),u_0(x)\)Unknown temperature and its prescribed initial distribution.
\(\partial_tu,\Delta u\)Time derivative and Laplacian \(\sum_{j=1}^N\partial_{x_j}^2u\): interior diffusion with diffusivity one.
\(\partial_\nu u=-\partial_{x_N}u\)Derivative in the outward normal direction.
\(\lambda>0\)Coupling strength of the boundary amplification.
\(\xi(t), |x-\xi(t)|\)Prescribed boundary pole and Euclidean distance from it.

The red segment marks strong boundary amplification; the blue point marks a boundary observation. This is a geometric diagram, not a temperature field.

0.2 What makes the boundary condition singular?

Inside the domain, heat diffuses normally. On the boundary, the Robin condition makes the outward normal derivative proportional to the temperature, with coefficient \(V(x,t)=\lambda/|x-\xi(t)|\) on the boundary. For nonnegative temperature, this sign gives inward heat flux (Chapter 1). The coefficient diverges as a boundary point approaches the pole. Only the coefficient’s center moves; the domain stays fixed.

Example. If \(\lambda=1\) and the boundary distances are 1, 0.1 and 0.01, the coefficients are 1, 10 and 100. These are coefficient values, not temperatures: the actual heat flux also depends on \(u\). A bounded approximation replaces the denominator by \(\sqrt{|x-\xi(t)|^2+\varepsilon^2}\), where \(\varepsilon>0\). The regularized and singular problems are different; Chapter 7 justifies the limit.

0.4 Why these powers of distance?

For a stationary singularity at the origin, rescale space and time by setting \(u_r(x,t)=u(rx,r^2t)\), with \(r>0\). The chain rule gives \(\partial_tu_r=r^2u_t\), \(\Delta u_r=r^2\Delta u\), and \(\partial_\nu u_r=r\partial_\nu u\), with the original derivatives evaluated at the rescaled point. An interior coefficient proportional to \(|x|^{-2}\) therefore has the same scaling order as the Laplacian; a boundary coefficient proportional to \(|x|^{-1}\) has the same order as the normal derivative.

\[\begin{aligned} (u_r)_t&=\Delta u_r+\lambda|x|^{-2}u_r &&\text{(interior model)},\\ \partial_\nu u_r&=\lambda|x|^{-1}u_r &&\text{(boundary model)}.\end{aligned}\]

In the moving boundary model, the trajectory rescales to \(\xi_r(t)=r^{-1}\xi(r^2t)\). Scale invariance motivates a critical coupling but does not determine its value.

0.5 The mathematical questions

Can diffusion offset the singular boundary input? Is there a threshold in \(\lambda\)? If a finite nonnegative solution exists, how large can it become near the pole, and is it uniquely determined by the data? Motion raises a further question: does the answer depend on how quickly the pole moves relative to diffusion?

We use an integral solution: free heat diffusion plus the accumulated boundary contribution, with finite values and continuity away from the moving singular point in space-time. A minimal solution is one no larger than any other admissible nonnegative solution with the same data. This is not unrestricted uniqueness. Chapter 2 gives the precise definition. A weighted class limits the allowed spatial growth near the pole and at infinity using a positive reference function, introduced in Chapter 3.

0.6 Preview of the main results

Manuscript results · precise statements in Chapter 3

For nonzero, nonnegative, compactly supported continuous initial data and any continuous boundary trajectory, the paper proves existence of a minimal integral solution up to and including a positive threshold \(\lambda_N\). Above this threshold the paper rules out finite nonnegative integral solutions if the motion is small relative to diffusion on all short time intervals. Below the threshold, with the same motion condition, it obtains upper singularity estimates and uniqueness in a specified weighted class.

Existence at critical coupling does not settle the precise critical profile. Failure of the motion condition does not by itself imply existence above the threshold.

Exact threshold and motion condition (optional preview)

The threshold is expressed using the Gamma function, \(\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty r^{z-1}e^{-r}\,dr\) for \(z>0\). Chapter 6 derives the constant. For short time intervals of length at most \(h>0\), the normalized displacement compares the movement of the pole with the diffusion length.

\[\lambda_N=2\frac{\Gamma(N/4)^2}{\Gamma((N-2)/4)^2}.\tag{0.1}\]

Define the uniform normalized displacement

\[\omega(h)=\sup_{\substack{0\le s<t\le T\\t-s\le h}}\frac{|\xi(t)-\xi(s)|}{\sqrt{t-s}}.\]

The comparison condition in the research paper is \(\omega(h)\to0\) as \(h\downarrow0\). For instance, a trajectory \(\xi(t)=at e_1\) has \(\omega(h)\le |a|\sqrt h\). In contrast, \(\xi(t)=\sqrt t\,e_1\) has \(\omega(h)=1\) for \(0<h\le T\). The denominator is the diffusion scale (Chapter 5). Existence through criticality does not require this condition.

Chapter 0 · Takeaways

The half-space is fixed and the inverse-distance coefficient acts only on the boundary. The threshold concerns finite integral solvability, not a numerical temperature threshold. Existence through criticality requires continuity of the path; the supercritical nonexistence theorem requires an additional displacement condition.
