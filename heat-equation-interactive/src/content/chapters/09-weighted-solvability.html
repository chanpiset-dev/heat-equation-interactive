Part III

9. Weighted solvability and upper singularity estimates

What additional information does a harmonic weight give beyond existence?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Harmonic profiles; upper Gaussian comparison; Appendix B.9 for complete normed spaces and contraction.

Choose an admissible power → harmonic heat identity → weighted bound and contraction → local continuity → restart preserving history → uniqueness and upper bound.

Chapter 7 constructs the minimal solution for compact continuous data even at critical coupling. It does not determine its pointwise growth near the pole. Here we prove Theorem 3.4 first and then Theorem 3.3. Throughout, assume \(N\ge3\), \(T>0\), \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\), and \(\xi\in C([0,T];\partial\mathcal H)\) satisfying (M). The strict coupling gap will absorb the losses in the upper Gaussian comparison. Unlike Chapter 7, the method admits data that may be unbounded near the initial singular point and need not be in \(L^2\).

9.1 Characteristic roots and the choice of weight

Recall the Poisson extension \(\Phi_\alpha=\mathcal P_{x_N}*|\cdot|^{-\alpha}\) from Lemma 6.4. Its trace is \(|x'|^{-\alpha}\), and its outward normal derivative is \(C_N(\alpha)|x'|^{-\alpha-1}\). It is homogeneous of degree \(-\alpha\), with two positive constants \(a_\alpha,A_\alpha\) such that

\[a_\alpha|x|^{-\alpha}\le\Phi_\alpha(x)\le A_\alpha|x|^{-\alpha}\quad(x\in\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{0\}).\tag{9.1}\]

On \(S_+=\{\theta\in\overline{\mathcal H}:|\theta|=1\}\), continuity and positivity give \(a_\alpha=\min_{S_+}\Phi_\alpha>0\) and \(A_\alpha=\max_{S_+}\Phi_\alpha<\infty\). Use \(\Phi_\alpha(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}\Phi_\alpha(x/|x|)\) to obtain the bound. The coefficient in the normal derivative is

\[C_N(\alpha)=2\frac{\Gamma((N-1-\alpha)/2)\Gamma((\alpha+1)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)\Gamma((N-2-\alpha)/2)},\qquad 0<\alpha<N-2.\tag{9.2}\]
Proposition 9.1 · Two characteristic roots

For \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\), the equation \(C_N(\alpha)=\lambda\) has exactly two roots \(\alpha_-(\lambda),\alpha_+(\lambda)\). They obey \(0<\alpha_-<\alpha_*<\alpha_+<N-2\), \(\alpha_*=(N-2)/2\) and \(\alpha_-+\alpha_+=N-2\). The coefficient exceeds \(\lambda\) strictly between the roots.

Proof of the characteristic-root bounds

Put \(d_0=N-2\) and \(L(a)=\log\Gamma((a+1)/2)-\log\Gamma(a/2)\). Then \(\log C_N(\alpha)=\log2+L(\alpha)+L(d_0-\alpha)\). As proved in Chapter 6, \(L\) is strictly concave (equivalently use \(L''(a)=[\psi'((a+1)/2)-\psi'(a/2)]/4<0\) since the trigamma series decreases). The sum is symmetric and strictly concave; it strictly increases up to its midpoint and decreases thereafter. The Gamma pole at zero makes both endpoint limits of \(C_N\) zero, and its midpoint value is \(\lambda_N\) by (6.12). Continuity and strict monotonicity give the roots and the sign assertion. □

We take \(\alpha\in(\alpha_-,\alpha_*)\), so \(\mu=C_N(\alpha)>\lambda\). A profile with the exact smaller-root exponent has no strict margin; the chosen interval also restricts the allowed singular growth to the smaller-exponent side of critical homogeneity. The manuscript theorem uses the smaller-exponent interval. Sections 9.3–9.7 prove the same conclusions for every \(\alpha\in(\alpha_-,\alpha_+)\); see the separately stated supplementary theorem. The larger root is not used to infer the asymptotic of the solution.

Example 9.1 · Explicit roots in dimension four

When \(N=4\), Gamma recurrence gives \(C_4(1/2)=C_4(3/2)=1/2\). At \(\lambda=1/2\), the admissible interval in the theorem is therefore \((1/2,1)\). For any weight strictly inside it the coefficient exceeds 1/2. At \(\lambda=2/\pi\), the two roots merge and this interval is empty. This is a limitation of the weighted comparison proof, not a nonexistence assertion.

9.2 The harmonic heat identity

To control the moving boundary integral, we need more than the elliptic relation for \(\Phi_\alpha\). Its exact heat representation provides a finite, quantitative bound on the entire boundary integral. The following statement holds for the wider exponent range \(0<\alpha<N-2\), independently of (M).

Lemma 9.1 · Harmonic heat representation (paper Lemma 5.1)

Let \(0<\alpha<N-2\), \(\mu=C_N(\alpha)\), and \(x\in\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{0\}\). For every \(t>0\),

\[\Phi_\alpha(x)=P_t\Phi_\alpha(x)+\mu\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)\frac{\Phi_\alpha(y)}{|y|}\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\tag{9.3}\]

Both terms are finite; in particular \(0<P_t\Phi_\alpha\le\Phi_\alpha\).

Proof. Fix \(0<\vartheta<t\) and remove the half-ball of radius \(r\) from the half-ball of radius \(R\), with \(0<r<R\). Write \(G_s(y)=G(x,y,t-s)\). Integrating \(\partial_s G_s=-\Delta_y G_s\) against the harmonic profile and using Green's second identity on this region yields
\[\frac{d}{ds}\int_{\mathcal H\cap\{r<|y|<R\}}G_s\Phi_\alpha\,dy=\int_{\partial(\mathcal H\cap\{r<|y|<R\})}(G_s\partial_\nu\Phi_\alpha-\Phi_\alpha\partial_\nu G_s)\,d\sigma.\tag{9.4}\]

Here the normal in the integral is the outward normal of the truncated region. On the planar part \(\partial_\nu G_s=0\), and the profile's normal derivative is \(\mu\Phi_\alpha/|y|\). Integrate from \(s=0\) to \(t-\vartheta\).

Technical proof · Removal of spatial and time cutoffs

Homogeneity and smoothness on the unit hemisphere yield \(|\nabla\Phi_\alpha(y)|\le C|y|^{-\alpha-1}\). On the inner hemisphere the two surface integrals are bounded by \(C_\vartheta(r^{N-2-\alpha}+r^{N-1-\alpha})\). Both vanish as \(r\downarrow0\), uniformly in \(s\in[0,t-\vartheta]\), because the kernel and its first derivatives are bounded on this elapsed-time range. On the outer hemisphere the same power factors multiply a Gaussian bounded by \(C_\vartheta e^{-cR^2}\) for large \(R\) (a derivative polynomial can be absorbed in the Gaussian). These vanish as \(R\to\infty\). The planar singular density is integrable near zero:

\[\int_{\partial\mathcal H\cap B_1}\frac{\Phi_\alpha(y)}{|y|}\,d\sigma=|\mathbb S^{N-2}|\int_0^1 v^{N-3-\alpha}\,dv=\frac{|\mathbb S^{N-2}|}{N-2-\alpha}.\tag{9.5}\]

The volume density is locally integrable since \(\alpha<N\). Gaussian decay provides integrable majorants at infinity for every fixed positive elapsed-time cutoff. Therefore the two truncated volume terms converge by dominated convergence, and the planar term by monotone convergence (or domination). This gives \(P_\vartheta\Phi_\alpha(x)=P_t\Phi_\alpha(x)+\mu\int_0^{t-\vartheta}\int G_s\Phi_\alpha/|y|\).

For \(x\) on the plane away from zero, the same identity follows by approaching it from the interior at fixed \(\vartheta\): the separated-time majorants just described work uniformly on compact observation sets. As \(\vartheta\downarrow0\), \(P_\vartheta\Phi_\alpha\to\Phi_\alpha\) locally uniformly away from zero. To justify the approximate identity for the unbounded datum, separate a small ball about zero, disjoint from the observation compact set. Its contribution is bounded by \(C\vartheta^{-N/2}e^{-c/\vartheta}\int_{B_r\cap\mathcal H}|y|^{-\alpha}dy\to0\). Off this ball the datum is bounded, and the Gaussian approximate identity gives uniform convergence using local uniform continuity. The boundary integral increases to its full value. Its finiteness follows from the finite left-hand limit. Equivalently, on recent times the source near zero has Gaussian separation and the bounded source away from zero has kernel mass \((\pi h)^{-1/2}\). This proves (9.3) at every stated point.

The conclusion is a decomposition of the fixed harmonic profile into free diffusion and a nonnegative boundary contribution. It does not assert that the moving solution is harmonic. □

Example 9.2 · An exact decomposition on the normal axis (supplementary)

In dimension four the critical profile \(\Phi_1(0,z)=2/(\pi z)\) is known from (6.13). For this stationary profile and \(z>0\), the tangential three-dimensional Gaussian integral of \(|y|^{-2}\) is \(1/(2h)\), by radial integration. The Neumann boundary kernel consequently gives

\[\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^t\frac{e^{-z^2/(4h)}}{2\sqrt\pi\,h^{3/2}}\,dh=\frac{2}{\pi z}\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{z}{2\sqrt t}\right).\tag{9.6}\]

Using (9.3), the free fraction of the profile is \(\operatorname{erf}(z/(2\sqrt t))\) and the boundary fraction is its complement. The substitution \(v=z/(2\sqrt h)\) proves (9.6). This example illustrates the identity at a critical stationary exponent; it is not an application of the strict weighted theorem to critical coupling.

Technical remark · What happens at \(\alpha=N-2\)?

At the excluded endpoint the Poisson extension is \(\Phi_{N-2}(x)=|x|^{2-N}\). To see this directly, put \(p_z=\mathcal P_z\) and \(\kappa_N=(N-2)|\mathbb S^{N-1}|/2\). Integration in the height variable gives \(\kappa_N\int_z^\infty p_s(x')\,ds=(|x'|^2+z^2)^{-(N-2)/2}\). At \(z=0\) this is the boundary power (away from zero). The Poisson semigroup identity \(p_z*p_s=p_{z+s}\) follows from the multiplier in Chapter 6; Tonelli permits integrating it in \(s\) from zero to infinity. Thus the Poisson extension of the boundary power is exactly the displayed radial function.

Its outward normal derivative at height \(z\) is \((N-2)z(|x'|^2+z^2)^{-N/2}\). This is \(\kappa_N\mathcal P_z(x')\), with \(\kappa_N=(N-2)|\mathbb S^{N-1}|/2\). The normalized Poisson kernel is an approximate identity, so the normal trace converges in distributions to \(\kappa_N\delta_0\), although it tends pointwise to zero off the pole. On the inner hemisphere of radius \(r\) the term \(G_s\partial_\nu\Phi\) has limit \(\kappa_N G(x,0,t-s)\), because the normal points toward the origin and \(\partial_\nu\Phi=(N-2)r^{1-N}\); the other term is \(O(r)\). For the endpoint Green formula this gives \[\Phi_{N-2}(x)=P_t\Phi_{N-2}(x)+\kappa_N\int_0^tG(x,0,h)\,dh,\quad x\ne0.\] Remove the outer and time cutoffs as in Lemma 9.1; the point-source time integral is finite by Gaussian separation. This is not (9.3) with zero coefficient. The missing flux is concentrated at a single boundary point. This supplements the endpoint observation in the research paper Appendix A; see Lemma 6.4 for the strict-range normal trace.

9.3 Weighted data and a complete function space

The intended estimate is \(u\le M\) times the positive harmonic profile centered at the pole. Dividing by that profile gives a bounded quotient. This is both a near-pole growth restriction and a far-field decay restriction, not an ordinary maximum norm on \(u\).

Let \(I=[a,b]\subset[0,T]\) and \(D_0(I)=(\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{0\})\times I\). In moving coordinates, the initial value at a restart time \(a\) is \(f_a(x)=u(x+\xi(a),a)\); for a=0 use the prescribed \(u_0\). Assume \(f_a\) is continuous off zero and \(0\le f_a\le M_a\Phi_\alpha\). The bound simultaneously restricts the allowed singularity and the behavior far from the origin.

Definition 9.1 · Weighted space
\[\begin{aligned}X_\alpha(I)&=\{W:D_0(I)\to\mathbb R\text{ Borel}:\|W\|_{X_\alpha(I)}<\infty\},\\ \|W\|_{X_\alpha(I)}&=\sup_{(x,t)\in D_0(I)}\frac{|W(x,t)|}{\Phi_\alpha(x)}.\end{aligned}\tag{9.7}\]

Use the pointwise supremum, not the essential supremum: boundary values are essential to the operator. The space includes signed functions so that differences of candidates belong to it.

The positive weight identifies \(X_\alpha(I)\) isometrically with the bounded Borel functions on \(D_0(I)\), so it is complete by Appendix B.9. Continuity will follow from the kernel estimates in §9.5.

Example 9.3 · Which data fit the weight?

\(\Phi_\alpha\) itself has norm one, whereas the constant function one is not in the space, since the weight decays at infinity. A continuous radial cutoff \(\chi\) equal to one for \(|x|\le1\) and zero for \(|x|\ge2\) isolates local singularity: \(\chi\Phi_\beta/\Phi_\alpha\asymp |x|^{\alpha-\beta}\) near zero. For \(0<\beta<N-2\), the cutoff datum belongs to the weight class exactly when \(\beta\le\alpha\). A bounded compact continuous datum is dominated by every such weight. For the uncut profile, the two-sided comparison (9.1) gives \(\Phi_\beta/\Phi_\alpha\asymp r^{\alpha-\beta}\) globally. Boundedness near zero requires \(\beta\le\alpha\); boundedness at infinity requires \(\beta\ge\alpha\). Hence \(\Phi_\beta\in X_\alpha\) exactly when \(\beta=\alpha\). The cutoff removes only the restriction at infinity. In particular global weight classes are not nested in general. The uncut profile has infinite \(L^2\) mass at infinity for \(\alpha<\alpha_*\), so the theorem is not merely an application of Chapter 7.

9.4 Upper comparison and the weighted contraction

Choose \(\alpha_-<\alpha<\alpha_+\) (the original theorem uses the smaller interval) and put \(\mu=C_N(\alpha)>\lambda\). From (5.5), for \(0<\delta<1\), \(M=\omega(\tau)\), let

\[D=D_+=(1-\delta)^{-1},\qquad c=c_+=(1-\delta)^{-N/2}\exp\!\left(\frac{(1-\delta)M^2}{4\delta}\right).\tag{9.8}\]

The upper comparison \(K_\xi(x,y;t,s)\le cG(x,y,D(t-s))\) is valid whenever \(0<t-s\le\tau\). The quantity that matters is the effective coupling \(\mu_+=c\lambda/D\). For fixed \(\delta\) its limit as \(\tau\downarrow0\) is \(\lambda(1-\delta)^{1-N/2}\); this tends to \(\lambda\) as \(\delta\) decreases to zero. First choose \(\delta\) so this limit is strictly below \(\mu\); next choose \(\tau\) using (M). The result is a single pair of constants and a length that work on every window of length at most \(\tau\), including windows starting at positive times.

\[q_\alpha:=\frac{c\lambda}{D\mu}=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}(1-\delta)^{1-N/2}\exp\!\left(\frac{(1-\delta)\omega(\tau)^2}{4\delta}\right)<1.\tag{9.9}\]
Why the margin is strict

Choosing \(\delta\) too small while the displacement is fixed can enlarge the exponential penalty. The order of choice is important. At \(\alpha=\alpha_-\) we have \(\mu=\lambda\) and \(q_\alpha\ge1\), so this estimate does not prove contraction. At critical coupling there is no \(\mu\) above \(\lambda\) among the profiles. Chapter 7 obtains critical existence by a different argument.

For a window \(I=[a,b]\) of length at most \(\tau\), define \(\mathcal A_I\) and its free term \(F_a\) by

\[\begin{aligned}(\mathcal A_I W)(x,t)&=\lambda\int_a^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}K_\xi(x,y;t,s)\frac{W(y,s)}{|y|}\,d\sigma(y)\,ds,\\ F_a(x,t)&=\int_{\mathcal H}K_\xi(x,y;t,a) f_a(y)\,dy\quad(t>a),\\ F_a(x,a)&=f_a(x),\qquad (\mathcal A_I W)(x,a)=0.\end{aligned}\tag{9.10}\]

The source at \(y=0\) and the time diagonal may be assigned zero in the integrand; they are null sets. The kernel and candidate give a jointly Borel integrand, as do the kernel and datum in the free term. Countable bases identify the product Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra with the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of the product. The parameter-integral result in A.1 gives Borel outputs for nonnegative inputs, and the bound below makes them finite. Treat positive and negative parts separately for a signed candidate.

Proposition 9.2 · Local weighted contraction

Under (9.9), \(\mathcal A_I\) is a positive bounded linear operator on \(X_\alpha(I)\) of norm at most \(q_\alpha\). The map \(W\mapsto F_a+\mathcal A_I W\) has a unique fixed point there (see the affine fixed-point result in B.9), and it is nonnegative with \(\|U\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\le cM_a/(1-q_\alpha)\).

Proof. Apply upper comparison to the stationary weight in (9.10); change the time variable to \(r=D(s-a)\), so \(ds=dr/D\). Lemma 9.1 gives
\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal A_I\Phi_\alpha(x,t)&\le\frac{c\lambda}{D}\int_0^{D(t-a)}\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,D(t-a)-r)\frac{\Phi_\alpha(y)}{|y|}\,d\sigma(y)dr\\ &=q_\alpha[\Phi_\alpha(x)-P_{D(t-a)}\Phi_\alpha(x)]\le q_\alpha\Phi_\alpha(x).\end{aligned}\tag{9.11}\]

Because \(|W|\le\|W\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\Phi_\alpha\), positivity gives \(|\mathcal A_I W|\le\mathcal A_I|W|\le q_\alpha\|W\|\Phi_\alpha\). This proves absolute integrability for signed \(W\), boundedness and the norm estimate. Similarly \(0\le F_a\le cM_a P_{D(t-a)}\Phi_\alpha\le cM_a\Phi_\alpha\) for \(t>a\); the inequality holds at a since \(c\ge1\).

Apply the affine fixed-point result with \(A=\mathcal A_I\) and \(F=F_a\). The fixed point is nonnegative and satisfies

\[U=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\mathcal A_I^kF_a,\qquad \|U\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\le\frac{cM_a}{1-q_\alpha}.\tag{9.12}\]

For \(S_j=\sum_{k=0}^j\mathcal A_I^kF_a\), the truncation estimate in B.9 gives \(\|U-S_j\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\le cM_aq_\alpha^{j+1}/(1-q_\alpha)\). At the left endpoint, \((\mathcal A_I W)(x,a)=0\), so \(U(x,a)=f_a(x)\). □

Supplementary Proposition 9.3 · Sharper harmonic-barrier estimate

With the same assumptions and notation as Proposition 9.2, the positive solution satisfies \(\|U\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\le cM_a\). If \(S_j=\sum_{k=0}^j\mathcal A_I^kF_a\), then \(\|U-S_j\|_{X_\alpha(I)}\le cM_aq_\alpha^{j+1}\).

Proof. Let \(W=cM_a\Phi_\alpha\), constant in moving time. From (9.11) and the undiscarded free-term estimate, for \(t>a\), writing \(P=P_{D(t-a)}\),
\[F_a+\mathcal A_I W\le cM_a[P\Phi_\alpha+q_\alpha(\Phi_\alpha-P\Phi_\alpha)]\le cM_a\Phi_\alpha=W.\tag{9.12a}\]

The last step uses the nonnegative boundary term in the harmonic heat identity, hence \(0\le P\Phi_\alpha\le\Phi_\alpha\), together with \(q_\alpha<1\). At \(t=a\), the inequality holds since \(f_a\le M_a\Phi_\alpha\) and \(c\ge 1\). By induction the positive iterates from zero stay below W. Proposition 9.2 proves norm convergence to \(U\), so the inequality survives pointwise. Iterating the fixed point identity gives \(U-S_j=\mathcal A_I^{j+1}U\), whence the asserted sharper remainder. These estimates complement, rather than replace as a source quotation, the manuscript bounds (9.12). □

9.5 Continuity of the weighted heat potentials

A Borel fixed point is not automatically an integral solution in Definition 2.3. We must recover continuity up to the nonsingular boundary, initial time and terminal time. The argument differs from Chapter 7: here a global spatial weight supplies the dominating densities instead of a local Moser bound.

Lemma 9.2 · Weighted heat-potential continuity (paper §5.3)

Assume only continuity of \(\xi\), \(0<\alpha<N-2\), and a Borel boundary density \(J\) with \(|J(y,s)|\le L_J|y|^{-\alpha-1}\). Then \(\int_a^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}K_\xi J\) is absolutely convergent and continuous off zero on \(D_0(I)\), with value zero at a. If \(f_a\) is continuous away from zero and \(|f_a|\le M_a\Phi_\alpha\), its moving free evolution is continuous on the same set including its initial value.

Proof · Boundary term. Fix a compact spatial observation set \(K\) in the closed half-space avoiding zero, and put \(4r=\min_{x\in K}|x|>0\). For sufficiently small \(h=t-s>0\), uniform continuity of \(\xi\) gives \(|\xi(t)-\xi(s)|<r\). For \(|y|<r\), \(|x-y+\xi(t)-\xi(s)|\ge2r\). Because \(y\) is on the plane its reflection is itself. Thus the kernel there is at most \(C_Nh^{-N/2}e^{-r^2/h}\), and the density is integrable by (9.5). For \(|y|\ge r\) the density is bounded; tangential translation does not change the boundary mass estimate (2.10).
\[\sup_{x\in K,t\in I}\int_{\max(a,t-\vartheta)}^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}|K_\xi J|\,d\sigma ds\le C\int_0^\vartheta(h^{-N/2}e^{-r^2/h}+h^{-1/2})\,dh\longrightarrow0.\tag{9.13}\]

The first integrand is integrable near zero by the substitution \(v=r^2/h\), and the second has integral \(2\sqrt\vartheta\). Constants depend on \(K\), the datum bound and exponent, not the observation or the source time. For elapsed times at least \(\vartheta>0\), define \(R_K=\max_K|x|+2\max_{[0,T]}|\xi|\). A joint dominating function in \((y,s)\) is

\[C_\vartheta |y|^{-\alpha-1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{((|y|-R_K)_+)^2}{4T}\right)\mathbf1_{[a,b]}(s).\tag{9.14}\]

On the boundary the radial power at zero is \(r^{N-3-\alpha}\), integrable precisely when \(\alpha<N-2\). The Gaussian controls the tail and the time interval has finite length. Include the indicator \(\mathbf1_{s\le t-\vartheta}\) when integrating. For a converging observation sequence the kernels converge pointwise for every \(s\) except the moving cutoff time, a null set; the density need not be continuous in \(s\). Dominated convergence proves continuity of the separated-time term. The uniform bound (9.13) then proves continuity of the full term, including its locally uniform vanishing as \(t\) approaches a and continuity at \(b\).

Free term. For elapsed time at least \(\vartheta\), replace the power in (9.14) by \(|y|^{-\alpha}\) and integrate over volume. Both direct and reflected terms satisfy this bound since reflection preserves \(|y|\). It is integrable because \(\alpha <N\). For \(h=t-a\) tending to zero, the portion from \(|y|<r\) tends uniformly to zero by Gaussian separation and local integrability of the volume power. On the complement the datum is bounded. The kernel has unit volume mass and concentrates at \(x\) uniformly for \(x\) in \(K\) as \(h\to0\) and \(\xi(t)-\xi(a)\)→0. More explicitly, both Gaussian terms have exponentially small mass on \(|y-x|\ge d\) for any \(d>0\) once the tangential increment is smaller than \(d/2\): for \(x,y\) in the closed half-space \(|x-y^*|\ge|x-y|\). Split the remaining integral into \(|y-x|<d\), controlled by uniform continuity of the datum on a compact neighborhood away from zero, and its complement, controlled by the Gaussian tail. Subtracting \(f_a(x)\) uses mass one; the integral of that bounded value over the small pole neighborhood also vanishes. This proves uniform convergence to \(f_a\) on \(K\), including observations on the plane. □

For the fixed point in (9.12), its boundary density \(J=\lambda U/|y|\) satisfies the hypothesis because the trace of the weight is exactly \(|y|^{-\alpha}\). Applying the lemma to \(F_a+\mathcal A_IU\) shows that U is continuous. The same lemma extends any weighted integral solution's interior equation to every nonsingular observation at positive time, by continuity on both sides. This is stronger than the Fatou inequality alone and will be used in uniqueness.

9.6 Continuation on arbitrary finite intervals

The global trajectory modulus permits a common \(\tau\) in (9.9). Partition the full interval \(0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_J=T\) so every window has length at most \(\tau\). On the first window take \(f_0(x)=u_0(x+\xi(0))\); for later windows use the moving-coordinate terminal value of the preceding one. In each case the datum is continuous away from zero and satisfies a finite weight bound. Let \(M_j=\sup_{x\ne0} f_{t_j}(x)/\Phi_\alpha(x)\) with \(x\) in the closed half-space. Then with \(L_\alpha=c/(1-q_\alpha)\),

\[\|U\|_{X_\alpha([t_j,t_{j+1}])}\le L_\alpha M_j,\qquad M_{j+1}\le L_\alpha M_j,\qquad M_j\le L_\alpha^j M_0.\tag{9.15}\]

Since \(L_\alpha\ge1\), the concatenation is bounded by \(L_\alpha^J M_0\Phi_\alpha\) on all windows. The values match at their shared endpoints and Lemma 9.2 gives continuity there. The bound can be large, but is finite for any fixed \(T\) with finitely many windows; it is not a statement of uniform-in-time boundedness on an infinite interval.

Supplementary consequence · Sharper continuation

By Proposition 9.3 on every window, \(M_{j+1}\le cM_j\) and hence \(M_j\le c^jM_0\), while \(\|U\|_{X_\alpha([t_j,t_{j+1}])}\le c^{j+1}M_0\). Therefore the global norm is at most \(c^JM_0\). This follows by finite induction, with the same uniform \(c\); it does not assert a uniform bound as \(T\) tends to infinity. The original estimate (9.15) is retained for comparison.

Recovering the original integral equation

Window solutions alone do not establish the full equation: we must show that their restarts encode earlier boundary contributions. Return to original coordinates \(u(x,t)=U(x-\xi(t),t)\). Suppose the full identity holds through a partition point \(a\) and put \(g=u(\cdot,a)\) (the value at its singular point is immaterial in volume integrals). For \(t>a\), use the Neumann composition identity, including planar source \(y\):

\[\int_{\mathcal H}G(x,z,t-a)G(z,y,a-s)\,dz=G(x,y,t-s),\quad s<a<t.\tag{9.16}\]

This follows by even reflection and Gaussian convolution; when \(y\) is on the plane it also follows directly from \(G(z,y,h)=2g_h(z-y)\), or by continuous extension of the composition identity. For the latter, fixed positive elapsed times give an integrable Gaussian majorant in \(z\). Both kernels are nonnegative. Inserting the identity at a into \(P_{t-a}g\) and interchanging integrals by Tonelli yields

\[P_{t-a}g(x)=P_tu_0(x)+\lambda\int_0^a\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)\frac{u(y,s)}{|y-\xi(s)|}\,d\sigma(y)ds.\tag{9.17}\]

The left side is finite by the local free-term estimate after translation. The local window equation adds precisely the part from a to \(t\). Starting with a=0, induction proves the global equation at every nonsingular positive-time observation, including terminal and intermediate partition points. Positivity, Tonelli and the finite weight bound justify the history decomposition; no differentiability of \(\xi\) or Markov property for an unknown singular evolution is assumed.

9.7 Minimality and uniqueness in the weighted class

We now identify the constructed solution with the full positive Volterra series. This step proves minimality even against competitors that need not be in the weight space.

Proof of Theorem 3.4 (paper Theorem 1.4)

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.4, the preceding construction gives a nonnegative continuous integral solution \(u\), with a global finite bound relative to the moving weight.

Use the full original-frame operator \(A_\xi\) and define the nonnegative series (pointwise extended-valued initially)

\[S(x,t)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty A_\xi^k(P_\cdot u_0)(x,t).\tag{9.18}\]

At time zero define the sum to equal \(u_0\). Because \(u\) is a positive fixed point off the graph, induction bounds every partial sum by \(u\). Hence \(S\) is finite there, Borel, and satisfies the same global weight bound. Monotone convergence applied to the positive integrands gives \(S=P_\cdot u_0+A_\xi S\), with finite right side. At this point we do not assume continuity of \(S\): its weighted bound and Lemma 9.2 imply continuity in the moving frame, including the initial trace, by applying that lemma to its full-interval integral representation (the lemma does not need (M) or a short interval).

On the first window \(S\) and \(u\) have the same datum and satisfy the same contraction equation, so their moving versions coincide. At each subsequent restart use (9.17) with \(S\) in place of \(u\). The composition formula and positivity give this identity even for a Borel fixed point with the global weight bound, and the terminal equality provides the same datum for the next window. Induction proves \(S=u\) everywhere on \(D_\xi\).

For another nonnegative integral solution \(v\) in the stated weighted class, Lemma 9.2 extends its interior identity to nonsingular boundary and terminal points. Its free term and boundary integral are continuous there; equality on the dense interior therefore implies equality throughout. The same restart argument then gives equality with \(u\) by local uniqueness and induction. For an arbitrary finite nonnegative integral competitor \(v\) with the same datum (without the weight bound), Lemma 2.3 gives \(v\ge P_\cdot u_0+A_\xi v\) at nonsingular observations. Positivity implies \(v\ge\sum_{k=0}^j A_\xi^k(P_\cdot u_0)\) for every \(j\); passage to the increasing limit gives \(v\ge S=u\). This proves minimality, including equality at the prescribed initial points. □

Two different conclusions
Uniqueness in \(X_\alpha\)Contraction: any two weighted fixed points coincideMinimality among all nonnegative integral solutionsPositive comparison: every competitor lies above the constructed one

Minimality gives a lower ordering, not an upper ordering for an arbitrary competitor. No general uniqueness theorem outside the weight class is proved. If the nonnegative datum is nonzero, strict positivity at positive time follows from the strictly positive free kernel; if the datum is zero, the minimal weighted solution is zero.

Supplementary Theorem 9.1 · Full characteristic-coefficient interval

Let \(N\ge 3\), \(T>0\), \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\) and \(\xi\) be continuous with (M). For any \(\alpha\in(\alpha_-(\lambda),\alpha_+(\lambda))\), suppose \(u_0\ge0\) is continuous on the closed half-space away from the initial pole and \(M_0=\sup_{x\ne\xi(0)}u_0(x)/\Phi_\alpha(x-\xi(0))<\infty\). There is exactly one nonnegative integral solution in the corresponding global moving weight class; it is minimal among all nonnegative integral solutions with this datum. The sharper global bound is \(c^JM_0\) for the uniform admissible partition above.

Proof. Proposition 9.1 gives \(0<\alpha<N-2\) and \(\mu=C_N(\alpha)>\lambda\). The two-sided profile estimates and Lemma 9.1 hold on this entire strict range, including the midpoint. Choose \(\delta\) then \(\tau\) as in (9.9), using only the strict gap and (M). The complete signed weighted space, the bounded positive operator and the free term estimates in §9.3–9.4 depend only on those inequalities. Proposition 9.2 gives a unique local fixed point; Proposition 9.3 gives the stronger endpoint bound. Lemma 9.2 requires only \(0<\alpha<N-2\) and continuity of \(\xi\), so it supplies continuity at the endpoints. The finite partition, history identity (9.17), and positive series (9.18) use no additional restriction on \(\alpha\). They yield a continuous full-interval integral solution, and the same local contraction on each window proves weighted uniqueness. The extended Lemma 2.3 applies to these data, with no weight restriction on the competitor; iteration therefore gives minimality. The proof of the sharper continuation bound is the finite induction just given. □

This extension is a supplementary consequence of the manuscript argument, not a restatement of Theorem 3.4. It includes the midpoint at strictly subcritical \(\lambda\), but excludes both roots and gives no weighted uniqueness result at critical coupling. The classes for different exponents are not automatically nested; if a datum belongs to several, the corresponding minimal solutions coincide by mutual minimality.

9.8 Upper singularity estimates

Return to the compact-data hypotheses of Theorem 3.1 and the strict subcritical condition with (M). For any admissible exponent, the compact continuous initial translate is bounded by a finite multiple of \(\Phi_\alpha\) by (9.1) on its compact support (including near the pole). The minimal solution from Theorem 3.4 equals the minimal solution of Theorem 3.1: each is a nonnegative integral solution, so each is below the other. The same reasoning identifies all constructions obtained with different admissible weights.

Proof of Theorem 3.3 (paper Theorem 1.3)

Given \(\eta>0\), select \(\alpha\in(\alpha_-,\min\{\alpha_*,\alpha_-+\eta\})\), a nonempty interval because both upper endpoints exceed \(\alpha_-\). The global estimate (9.15) and (9.1) give, with \(r=|x-\xi(t)|\),

\[u(x,t)\le C\Phi_\alpha(x-\xi(t))\le C A_\alpha r^{-\alpha}\le C_\eta r^{-\alpha_-(\lambda)-\eta},\qquad 0<r\le1.\tag{9.19}\]

This holds on the entire nonsingular domain \(D_\xi\), including initial, boundary and terminal observations. The constants depend on \(N\), \(\lambda\), \(T\), \(\xi\), \(u_0\) and \(\eta\) through the profile bound and finite continuation estimate. □

Observe that \(r^{-\alpha}\le r^{-\alpha_--\eta}\) for \(r\le1\), not generally for large r. One cannot simply let \(\eta\) tend to zero in (9.19): the constants, comparison lengths and contraction margins depend on the chosen weight. The upper bound neither proves that the solution diverges near the pole nor yields a matching lower bound. In particular, an exact singular asymptotic at the smaller root and a critical singular asymptotic are not established by this proof.

Chapter 9 · Takeaways

A strict characteristic-coefficient margin produces a weighted contraction. The harmonic heat identity controls the boundary operator, the weighted kernel estimates recover continuity without differentiating the trajectory, and Tonelli plus composition preserve the entire past across a finite partition. The full positive series is minimal; contraction proves uniqueness only within the weighted class. Arbitrarily close admissible weights yield the upper singularity estimate for the compact-data minimal solution.
