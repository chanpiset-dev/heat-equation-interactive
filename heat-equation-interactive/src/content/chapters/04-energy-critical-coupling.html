Part II

4. Energy and critical coupling

Can the boundary input be controlled by the dissipation in the interior?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Appendix B.1 for the squared \(L^2\) norm; B.2 and B.3 for gradients and traces.

Multiply the equation by \(u\) → integrate by parts → bound the boundary form → interpret the remaining margin.

4.1 Deriving the energy identity

In this chapter first work with smooth or bounded-Robin solutions for which integration by parts is justified. Diffusion removes squared \(L^2\) energy, while positive Robin input adds it. The singular limit requires Chapter 7.

The total heat discussed in Chapter 1 is an integral of \(u\). The energy method instead studies its squared \(L^2\) norm. These quantities measure different features of the field. To justify the following computation first use a bounded continuous nonnegative boundary coefficient \(b(x,t)\) with sufficiently regular solutions and sufficient decay (or the weak formulation below). Multiplying the PDE by \(u\), integrating over the half-space, and integrating by parts in space gives

\[\begin{aligned}\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|_2^2&=\int_{\mathcal H}u\Delta u\,dx\\&=-\int_{\mathcal H}|\nabla u|^2\,dx+\int_{\partial\mathcal H}b|\operatorname{Tr}u|^2\,d\sigma.\end{aligned}\tag{4.1}\]

The interior term is dissipative; the positive Robin term adds to the squared norm. For the original singular coefficient this identity is initially formal, because the regularity and boundary integrability required for it are not given a priori. We will apply the justified version to regularized solutions.

Weak formulation and the energy identity

Let \(\mathscr X=H^1(\mathcal H)\). For a weak solution in \(L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\) with time derivative in \(L^2(0,T;\mathscr X^*)\), the identity for almost every time and each \(w\in\mathscr X\) is

\[\langle u_t,w\rangle_{\mathscr X^*,\mathscr X}+\int_{\mathcal H}\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\,dx=\int_{\partial\mathcal H}b\operatorname{Tr}u\operatorname{Tr}w\,d\sigma.\]

The Hilbert-triple energy chain rule gives an \(L^2\)-continuous representative and \(\frac12(\|u\|_2^2)'=\langle u_t,u\rangle\) almost everywhere. The bounded coefficient and ordinary trace bound (B.1) make the boundary pairing meaningful. Chapter 7 will justify this regularized solution framework.

4.2 The sharp boundary inequality

Theorem 4.1 · Sharp boundary Kato inequality

For \(N\ge3\), every \(w\in H^1(\mathcal H)\), and every \(a\in\partial\mathcal H\),

\[\lambda_N\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|\operatorname{Tr}w(x)|^2}{|x-a|}\,d\sigma(x)\le\int_{\mathcal H}|\nabla w|^2\,dx.\tag{4.2}\]

The coefficient \(\lambda_N\) is optimal. The proof is developed in Chapter 6.

Define the bulk Dirichlet energy and weighted boundary integral for a test function, with a boundary center \(a\in\partial\mathcal H\) (distinct from the scalar trajectory coordinate \(a(t)\) in Figure 1.4):

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal E[w]&=\int_{\mathcal H}|\nabla w|^2\,dx,\\B_a[w]&=\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|\operatorname{Tr}w|^2}{|x-a|}\,d\sigma,\\Q_{\lambda,a}[w]&=\mathcal E[w]-\lambda B_a[w].\end{aligned}\tag{4.3}\]

The inequality says precisely that \(B_a[w]\le \mathcal E[w]/\lambda_N\). Thus

\[Q_{\lambda,a}[w]\ge\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_N}\right)\mathcal E[w].\tag{4.4}\]

Below criticality the form controls the full gradient norm. At equality it remains nonnegative but the uniform positive gradient factor disappears. Above criticality, sharpness ensures that some test function has negative form, not that all test functions do.

4.3 Why motion does not change the energy bound

For each fixed time, put \(a=\xi(t)\) and translate tangentially, defining \(w_a(z)=w(z+a)\). This preserves the half-space and both measures, so a change of variables gives

\[\mathcal E[w_a]=\mathcal E[w],\qquad B_0[w_a]=B_a[w].\]

Applying (4.2) with center zero to \(w_a\) proves the translated inequality. No derivative of \(\xi\) occurs. This is why the existence proof does not need condition (M).

For example, take \(b_n(x,t)=\lambda(|x-\xi(t)|^2+n^{-2})^{-1/2}\) on the boundary. For the regularized weak solution \(u_n\), since \(b_n\le\lambda/|x-\xi(t)|\) almost everywhere, (4.1) and (4.2) imply

\[\|u_n(t)\|_2^2+2\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_N}\right)\int_0^t\|\nabla u_n(s)\|_2^2\,ds\le\|u_0\|_2^2.\tag{4.5}\]

For \(\lambda\le\lambda_N\) this gives a uniform bound for all approximations. The gradient control is uniform only for strict subcriticality. Finiteness and continuity of the singular limit are separate arguments; see Chapter 7.

4.4 Why the threshold is sharp

Optimality of (4.2) means that the infimum of \(\mathcal E[w]/B_a[w]\) over \(w\in H^1(\mathcal H)\) with \(B_a[w]>0\) is \(\lambda_N\). Hence for \(\lambda>\lambda_N\) we can choose a test \(w\) with \(Q_{\lambda,0}[w]<0\). Normalize \(\|w\|_2=1\) and set \(w_\rho(x)=\rho^{-N/2}w(x/\rho)\) for \(\rho>0\). Changing variables in the volume and boundary integrals gives

\[\|w_\rho\|_2=1,\qquad \mathcal E[w_\rho]=\rho^{-2}\mathcal E[w],\qquad B_0[w_\rho]=\rho^{-2}B_0[w].\tag{4.6}\]

Thus \(Q_{\lambda,0}[w_\rho]=\rho^{-2}Q_{\lambda,0}[w]\to-\infty\) as \(\rho\downarrow0\). The stationary quadratic form is unbounded below on the unit sphere. Chapter 8 will show how this leads to divergence of stationary regularizations, and how a kernel comparison transfers the result to slow trajectories. Neither scaling nor this form calculation alone establishes nonexistence for arbitrary moving coefficients.

Example 4.1 · Comparing energy margins

At \(\lambda=\lambda_N/2\), the gradient margin is \(1/2\). At \(\lambda=0.99\lambda_N\) it is only \(0.01\). The energy estimate deteriorates as criticality is approached, even though the \(L^2\) bound persists at equality. This explains why the compactness argument cannot simply assume the same integrated-gradient bound in the critical case.

Chapter 4 · Takeaways

The threshold comes from a sharp trace estimate. Translation gives uniform control for arbitrary continuous motion. Criticality loses coercivity, not the nonnegative form bound.
