Part III

6. Sharp Kato inequality

Why does a harmonic function determine the optimal boundary trace constant?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Appendix B.2–B.3 for Sobolev functions and traces; Appendix C.1–C.4 for Fourier transforms, distributions and Gamma identities.

Variational quotient → harmonic extension → normal derivative multiplier → characteristic coefficient → ground-state identity → logarithmic test functions.

6.1 The variational question

In Theorem 4.1, the constant controlling the boundary interaction was given without proof. We now determine it. Throughout this chapter write \(d=N-1\), \(x=(x',x_N)\), and let \(a=0\); tangential translation recovers the general boundary center.

\[\lambda_N=\inf_{\substack{w\in C_c^\infty(\overline{\mathcal H})\\ B_0[w]>0}}\frac{\mathcal E[w]}{B_0[w]},\qquad B_0[w]=\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|w|^2}{|x'|}\,d\sigma.\tag{6.1}\]

The test functions are restrictions of \(C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^N)\) functions, so the trace may be nonzero. Equation (6.1) requires both a universal lower bound and an approximating sequence:

\[\mathcal E[w]\ge\lambda_N B_0[w]\quad\text{for every test }w,\qquad B_0[w_j]>0,\quad \frac{\mathcal E[w_j]}{B_0[w_j]}\longrightarrow\lambda_N.\]

The ground-state identity gives the bound. The logarithmic cutoffs give the sequence.

For \(w_\rho(x)=w(x/\rho)\), changing variables in the volume and surface integrals gives

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal E[w_\rho]&=\rho^{N-2}\mathcal E[w],\\ \int_{\partial\mathcal H}|x'|^{-p}|w_\rho|^2d\sigma&=\rho^{N-1-p}\int_{\partial\mathcal H}|x'|^{-p}|w|^2d\sigma.\end{aligned}\tag{6.2}\]

The exponents coincide only for \(p=1\). For example, at \(N=4\), halving the length scale multiplies both terms with \(p=1\) by \(1/4\). The dimensionless ratio is unchanged. This identifies the critical *power*, not yet the critical coefficient.

6.2 Harmonic extension and the Poisson kernel

Given a boundary function \(f\), consider the elliptic problem \(\Delta v=0\) in \(\mathcal H\), \(v(x',0)=f(x')\). Unlike the original heat equation, the extension has no time variable. It is a tool for constructing a positive function with the desired normal derivative.

Definition 6.1 · Tangential Fourier transform

For \(f\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R^d)\), the space of smooth rapidly decreasing functions (all derivatives decrease faster than any power), use the unitary convention

\[\widehat f(\zeta)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb R^d}e^{-ix'\cdot\zeta}f(x')\,dx'.\tag{6.3}\]

The inverse has the positive sign in the exponential. Integration by parts gives \(\widehat{\partial_j f}=i\zeta_j\widehat f\), and Plancherel gives \(\int|f|^2=\int|\widehat f|^2\). Plancherel extends the transform to square-integrable functions by density; the derivative identity then holds whenever the corresponding weak derivative lies in \(L^2\). See Appendix C for the precise conventions and proofs.

Transform only the tangential coordinates. The equation becomes \(\partial_{x_N}^2\widehat v-|\zeta|^2\widehat v=0\). At each nonzero frequency its decaying solution is \(e^{-x_N|\zeta|}\widehat f(\zeta)\). At zero frequency the bounded solution is constant. This motivates the following representation.

Lemma 6.1 · Poisson extension

For \(z>0\), set

\[\mathcal P_z(x')=\frac{\Gamma((d+1)/2)}{\pi^{(d+1)/2}}\frac{z}{(|x'|^2+z^2)^{(d+1)/2}},\qquad v(x',z)=(\mathcal P_z*f)(x').\tag{6.4}\]

Then \(\mathcal P_z\) has mass one, is positive, and has convolution multiplier \(e^{-z|\zeta|}\). For Schwartz data, \(v\) is harmonic and has boundary trace \(f\).

Proof

Normalization. In polar coordinates, substituting \(r=zs\) reduces the integral to a constant times \(\int_0^\infty s^{d-1}(1+s^2)^{-(d+1)/2}ds=\tfrac12 B(d/2,1/2)\). Here \(B(a,b)=\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)\), obtained by changing variables in the product of two gamma integrals. With \(|S^{d-1}|=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)\), the stated prefactor makes the integral one.

Harmonicity and multiplier. Direct differentiation of \(z(|x'|^2+z^2)^{-(d+1)/2}\) shows its Laplacian in \(d+1\) variables vanishes. Its Fourier multiplier \(m(\zeta,z)=\int e^{-ix'\cdot\zeta}\mathcal P_z(x')dx'\) thus solves the above ODE. Differentiation and integration by parts are justified at positive heights by the integrable decay of the kernel derivatives. Positivity and unit mass imply \(|m|\le1\), and scaling \(\mathcal P_z(x')=z^{-d}\mathcal P_1(x'/z)\) gives \(m(\zeta,z)\to1\) as \(z\downarrow0\) by dominated convergence. Hence \(m=e^{-z|\zeta|}\). The same scaling proves the approximate identity property: for any fixed \(\delta>0\), the mass outside \(|x'|\le\delta\) tends to zero. This yields the trace for continuous bounded data, in particular Schwartz data. □

6.3 Dirichlet-to-Neumann map and energy

Before defining the normal derivative operator abstractly, think of a boundary wave \(\cos(kx_1)\). Its bounded harmonic extension is \(e^{-kx_N}\cos(kx_1)\) for \(k>0\), so the outward derivative on the boundary multiplies the wave by \(k\). Fourier analysis makes this observation systematic.

The outward normal is \(-e_N\), so differentiation at the plane has the opposite sign to differentiation into the domain.

Definition 6.2 · Square-root Laplacian

For Schwartz functions define \((-\Delta_{x'})^{1/2}\) by its Fourier multiplier:

\[\widehat{(-\Delta_{x'})^{1/2}f}(\zeta)=|\zeta|\widehat f(\zeta).\tag{6.5}\]

Thus the harmonic extension has outward derivative \(\partial_\nu v=(-\Delta_{x'})^{1/2}f\). Applying the multiplier twice gives the usual Laplacian with negative sign. This is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map: it converts prescribed boundary values into normal derivatives.

Lemma 6.2 · Least-energy extension

For \(w\in C_c^\infty(\overline{\mathcal H})\) with trace \(f\), and \(v=\mathcal P_{x_N}*f\),

\[\mathcal E[v]=\int_{\mathbb R^d}|\zeta|\,|\widehat f(\zeta)|^2d\zeta,\qquad \mathcal E[w]=\mathcal E[v]+\mathcal E[w-v].\tag{6.6}\]

Proof

Use Plancherel in the tangential variables. Both the tangential and normal gradients of the extension give the squared multiplier \(|\zeta|^2 e^{-2z|\zeta|}\). Integrating their sum in \(z\) produces \(|\zeta|\), proving the first equality. To see orthogonality, set \(W=\widehat w\) and \(k=|\zeta|\). For each nonzero frequency integrate

\[\int_0^\infty\left(k^2e^{-kz}\widehat f\overline W-ke^{-kz}\widehat f\,\partial_z\overline W\right)dz=k|\widehat f|^2.\]

The integrand is the derivative of \(-ke^{-kz}\widehat f\overline W\); the lower endpoint uses \(W(\zeta,0)=\widehat f(\zeta)\). Both gradient energies are finite, so Cauchy–Schwarz permits integration over frequencies. The cross inner product of \(\nabla v\) with \(\nabla(w-v)\) is zero. Expanding the squared norm gives the result. Only gradient integrability is needed for the identity. □

Why this matters. The bulk energy needed to realize a boundary trace is measured by a fractional-order operator on the boundary. We can therefore find the special Robin profile by studying powers under this operator rather than solving a new differential equation in all spatial variables.

6.4 Singular powers and the characteristic coefficient

The boundary power is singular at zero and does not have an ordinary absolutely integrable Fourier transform. The following computation uses distributions; review Appendix C.1 if that language is unfamiliar.

We need to transform \(|x'|^{-\alpha}\), which is not a Schwartz function. It is locally integrable for \(0<\alpha<d\) and defines a tempered distribution by integration against Schwartz tests. A tempered distribution is a continuous linear functional on the Schwartz class; its Fourier transform is defined by duality with the Fourier transform of test functions. This allows the singular power to be treated without assuming an absolutely integrable Fourier integral.

Lemma 6.3 · Fourier transform of a radial power
\[\mathcal F(|x'|^{-\alpha})(\zeta)=a(d,\alpha)|\zeta|^{\alpha-d},\qquad a(d,\alpha)=2^{d/2-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma((d-\alpha)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)},\quad 0<\alpha<d.\tag{6.7}\]
Proof of the Riesz transform identity by Gaussian mixtures

The gamma integral \(\Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty r^{s-1}e^{-r}dr\) for \(s>0\) implies, by substitution,

\[|x'|^{-\alpha}=\frac1{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\int_0^\infty r^{\alpha/2-1}e^{-r|x'|^2}\,dr.\tag{6.8}\]

First restrict the integral to \([1/m,m]\). The resulting function \(f_m\) is Schwartz and increases pointwise off zero to the power. Its Fourier transform can be taken inside the truncated integral by absolute integrability. The unitary transform of \(e^{-r|x'|^2}\) is \((2r)^{-d/2}e^{-|\zeta|^2/(4r)}\). Thus at nonzero frequencies the limit of the transforms is

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{2^{-d/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\int_0^\infty r^{(\alpha-d)/2-1}e^{-|\zeta|^2/(4r)}dr\ &=2^{d/2-\alpha}\frac{\Gamma((d-\alpha)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}|\zeta|^{\alpha-d}.\end{aligned}\]

The last step substitutes \(s=|\zeta|^2/(4r)\). On the physical side the majorant \(|x'|^{-\alpha}|\psi(x')|\) is integrable for every Schwartz test \(\psi\); on the frequency side the majorant \(a(d,\alpha)|\zeta|^{\alpha-d}|\psi(\zeta)|\) is integrable because the radial exponent near zero is \(\alpha-1>-1\). Dominated convergence on both sides and the dual definition of the transform establish (6.7) as a distributional identity. □

In the narrower range \(0<\alpha<d-1\), multiplication by \(|\zeta|\) gives a density proportional to the transform of \(|x'|^{-\alpha-1}\). Consequently the coefficient is a quotient of the two gamma factors:

\[\begin{aligned}(-\Delta_{x'})^{1/2}|x'|^{-\alpha}&=C_N(\alpha)|x'|^{-\alpha-1},\\ C_N(\alpha)&=\frac{a(d,\alpha)}{a(d,\alpha+1)}\\&=2\frac{\Gamma((N-1-\alpha)/2)\Gamma((\alpha+1)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)\Gamma((N-2-\alpha)/2)}.\end{aligned}\tag{6.9}\]

The multiplier here is applied to an explicitly locally integrable Fourier density, so no multiplication of an arbitrary distribution by a nonsmooth function is assumed. At \(\alpha=d-1\), the limiting multiplier density is constant, whose inverse transform is a multiple of the Dirac mass at zero, not the displayed power. This explains the open exponent interval.

6.5 The positive harmonic profiles

Lemma 6.4 · Harmonic profile and boundary identity

For \(0<\alpha<N-2\), define

\[\Phi_\alpha(x',z)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}\mathcal P_z(x'-w)|w|^{-\alpha}\,dw.\tag{6.10}\]

It is positive, harmonic in \(\mathcal H\), smooth up to the plane away from zero, and

\[\begin{aligned}\Phi_\alpha(x',0)&=|x'|^{-\alpha},\quad x'\ne0,\\\partial_\nu\Phi_\alpha(x',0)&=\frac{C_N(\alpha)}{|x'|}\Phi_\alpha(x',0),\\\Phi_\alpha(\rho x)&=\rho^{-\alpha}\Phi_\alpha(x),\qquad \rho>0.\end{aligned}\tag{6.11}\]

There exist constants \(0<c\le C<\infty\) such that \(c|x|^{-\alpha}\le\Phi_\alpha(x)\le C|x|^{-\alpha}\) on \(\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{0\}\).

Proof

Near \(w=0\), the integrand is integrable since \(\alpha<d\); at infinity the Poisson tail is \(O(|w|^{-d-1})\). Positivity is immediate; differentiation under the integral at positive heights gives harmonicity. Substitution \(w=\rho v\) in (6.10) gives homogeneity. For the normal derivative, use the truncated Gaussian mixtures from Lemma 6.3. At each positive height their convolutions increase to \(\Phi_\alpha\); they are uniformly bounded at that height (split \(|w|\le1\) and its complement), so convergence holds against Schwartz tests. Their transforms converge by the same frequency majorant, now multiplied by \(e^{-z|\zeta|}\). Thus the Fourier density of the extension is \(a(d,\alpha)e^{-z|\zeta|}|\zeta|^{\alpha-d}\). At positive height its inverse transform and differentiated inverse transform are absolutely convergent. Taking \(z\downarrow0\), dominated convergence against Schwartz tests gives the distributional limit of the negative height derivative as (6.9).

Justification of the classical boundary derivative

Fix \(x'_0\ne0\). Split the boundary data into a smooth compactly supported part equal to the power on a neighborhood of \(x'_0\), and a remainder supported a positive distance away. The first part is Schwartz, so its extension and all derivatives converge locally up to the plane by the Fourier multiplier representation. For the separated part, on a smaller neighborhood the Poisson kernel and its derivatives remain bounded near the integration singularity and have integrable tails against the power, uniformly for heights between zero and one. Dominated convergence extends the derivatives to the plane; the separated term has zero boundary value. The continuous local limit of the normal derivative must coincide with the distributional limit there. This proves the pointwise trace and derivative formulas away from zero. Homogeneity and the positive continuous profile on the compact closed unit hemisphere give the two-sided bounds (and similarly a bound on its gradient by \(C|x|^{-\alpha-1}\)).

□

Why choose the midpoint \(\alpha_*=(N-2)/2\)? Let \(r=|x|\), \(\vartheta=x/r\), and write \(\Phi_\alpha(x)=r^{-\alpha}p_\alpha(\vartheta)\). For a cutoff \(\chi(s)\) with \(s=\log r\), the radial derivative is \(\chi'(s)/r\). In polar coordinates the weighted gradient cost factors as

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{\mathcal H}\Phi_\alpha^2|\nabla\chi(\log r)|^2dx &= A_\alpha\int_0^\infty r^{N-3-2\alpha}|\chi'(\log r)|^2dr\\&=A_\alpha\int_{\mathbb R}e^{(N-2-2\alpha)s}|\chi'(s)|^2ds,\\ A_\alpha&=\int_{S^{N-1}\cap\mathcal H}p_\alpha(\vartheta)^2d\vartheta.\end{aligned}\]

Here the angular integral is finite and positive by Lemma 6.4. At \(\alpha=\alpha_*\), the radial measure becomes \(dr/r=ds\); translating a fixed cutoff transition along the logarithmic radius does not change its cost. On the boundary the squared profile divided by distance likewise gives the radial measure \(dr/r\). Thus the critical exponent makes both terms scale invariant. This is the mechanism behind the minimizing sequence in §6.7.

Substitution in (6.9) yields

\[C_N(\alpha_*)=2\frac{\Gamma(N/4)^2}{\Gamma((N-2)/4)^2}=\lambda_N.\tag{6.12}\]
Why the characteristic coefficient has exactly one maximum

Let \(s=\alpha/2\) and \(b=(N-2-\alpha)/2\); their sum is fixed. Put \(R(s)=\Gamma(s+1/2)/\Gamma(s)\). Then \(C_N(\alpha)=2R(s)R(b)\), which is symmetric about \(\alpha_*\). Since \(R(s)=\Gamma(1/2)/B(s,1/2)\), and

\[ B(s,1/2)=\int_0^1 t^{s-1}(1-t)^{-1/2}dt,\]

Hölder's inequality for this integral shows that \(\log B(s,1/2)\) is strictly convex (equality would require two distinct powers of \(t\) to be proportional almost everywhere). Therefore \(\log R\) is strictly concave. The symmetric strictly concave function \(\log C_N\) strictly increases before the midpoint and strictly decreases after it. The endpoint limits of \(C_N\) are zero by \(s\Gamma(s)=\Gamma(s+1)\) as \(s\downarrow0\). It follows that \(C_N(\alpha)=\lambda\) has two roots if \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\), one at equality, and none in this interval above the maximum. This justifies the roots used in §3.4; The weighted solvability theorem chooses exponents strictly above the smaller root, as specified in §9.1.

Example 6.1 · An exact profile in dimension four

For \(N=4\), \(\alpha_*=1\) and \(\lambda_4=2/\pi\). With \(r=|x'|\) and \(z=x_4>0\),

\[\Phi_1(r,z)=\frac{2}{\pi r}\arctan\frac r z,\qquad \Phi_1(0,z)=\frac{2}{\pi z}.\tag{6.13}\]

For \(r>0\), the radial Laplacian in three tangential dimensions is \(\partial_{rr}+2r^{-1}\partial_r\). Writing \(F=r\Phi_1\) gives \(\Delta\Phi_1=(F_{rr}+F_{zz})/r=0\), since \(\arctan(r/z)\) is harmonic in the positive quadrant. The even expansion at \(r=0\) extends harmonicity there. At the boundary \(\Phi_1(r,0)=1/r\) and \(-\partial_z\Phi_1(r,0)=2/(\pi r^2)\), as required.

Derive the explicit Poisson integral

By radial convolution in three dimensions, the spherical average of \(|x'-\rho\theta|^{-1}\) is

\[\frac12\int_{-1}^1\frac{ds}{\sqrt{r^2+\rho^2-2r\rho s}}=\frac1{\max(r,\rho)}.\]

Integrating the Poisson kernel against this average reduces the convolution to

\[\frac{4z}{\pi}\left[\frac1r\int_0^r\frac{\rho^2}{(\rho^2+z^2)^2}d\rho+\int_r^\infty\frac{\rho}{(\rho^2+z^2)^2}d\rho\right]=\frac{2}{\pi r}\arctan(r/z).\]

Use the primitives \(\arctan(\rho/z)/(2z)-\rho/[2(\rho^2+z^2)]\) and \(-1/[2(\rho^2+z^2)]\) respectively. The rational terms cancel.

6.6 Ground-state representation

Set \(\Phi_*=\Phi_{\alpha_*}\). The normal derivative of this positive harmonic function is exactly \(\lambda_N\Phi_*/|x'|\). Factoring out \(\Phi_*\) will turn the difference between bulk dissipation and boundary amplification into a nonnegative integral.

Proposition 6.1 · Ground-state identity

If \(w\in C_c^\infty(\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{0\})\) (compact support avoiding the origin), then

\[\mathcal E[w]-\lambda_N B_0[w]=\int_{\mathcal H}\Phi_*^2\left|\nabla\left(\frac{w}{\Phi_*}\right)\right|^2dx\ge0.\tag{6.14}\]

Proof

For real-valued \(w\), put \(v=w/\Phi_*\); the complex case follows by applying the result to real and imaginary parts. Expand the derivative:

\[|\nabla(\Phi_*v)|^2=\Phi_*^2|\nabla v|^2+\nabla\Phi_*\cdot\nabla(\Phi_*v^2).\]

Integrate the cross term by parts, using the compact support away from zero and \(\Delta\Phi_*=0\). Only the planar boundary term remains:

\[\int_{\partial\mathcal H}(\partial_\nu\Phi_*)\Phi_*v^2d\sigma=\lambda_N\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|w|^2}{|x'|}d\sigma.\]

Rearrange to obtain (6.14). The right side is nonnegative. □

6.7 Logarithmic annuli and sharpness

Proving an inequality gives a lower bound on the quotient. Proving sharpness additionally requires a sequence whose quotient approaches the bound. The logarithmic annuli below provide that sequence.

First extend the lower bound to smooth functions whose support includes zero. Use a radial cutoff \(\chi_\delta\) equal to zero for \(|x|\le\delta\), equal to one for \(|x|\ge2\delta\), with \(|\nabla\chi_\delta|\le C/\delta\). Choose \(0\le\chi_\delta\le1\). For fixed smooth compactly supported \(w\), the product rule and the support of \(1-\chi_\delta\) give

\[\begin{aligned} \|\nabla((1-\chi_\delta)w)\|_2^2 &\le C_w(\delta^N+\delta^{N-2}),\\ 0\le B_0[w]-B_0[\chi_\delta w] &\le C_w\int_0^{2\delta}r^{N-3}\,dr=O(\delta^{N-2}). \end{aligned}\]

In the gradient estimate the second term is the volume \(O(\delta^N)\) of the annulus times the squared cutoff bound \(O(\delta^{-2})\). Both errors vanish for \(N\ge3\), so the lower bound from (6.14) passes to \(w\).

Optimality of the coefficient

For \(R>2\) choose a smooth radial cutoff \(\chi_R\) equal to one for \(R^{-1}\le |x|\le R\), zero for \(|x|\le(2R)^{-1}\) and \(|x|\ge2R\), with \(|\nabla\chi_R|\le C/|x|\). Such a function is obtained by composing two fixed smooth transitions with rescaled radii. Set \(w_R=\Phi_*\chi_R\). This is a smooth compactly supported test on the closed half-space, avoiding zero.

By homogeneity, \(\Phi_*^2\le C|x|^{-(N-2)}\). The right side of (6.14) is supported on two annuli of ratio two. Each contributes at most

\[C\int_a^{2a}r^{N-1}r^{-(N-2)}r^{-2}dr=C\log2.\]

On the boundary, the critical trace is exactly \(|x'|^{-(N-2)/2}\). Hence

\[B_0[w_R]\ge |S^{d-1}|\int_{1/R}^R\frac{dr}{r}=2|S^{d-1}|\log R.\]

Dividing the identity by this denominator yields

\[\frac{\mathcal E[w_R]}{B_0[w_R]}=\lambda_N+O\left(\frac1{\log R}\right)\longrightarrow\lambda_N.\tag{6.15}\]

Thus no larger constant can hold for all admissible competitors. The formal profile \(\Phi_*\) itself has divergent weighted boundary integral at both radial endpoints; the proof constructs a minimizing sequence, not an admissible extremizer. □

6.8 Extension to Sobolev functions and consequences

For \(w\in H^1(\mathcal H)\), smooth compactly supported restrictions are dense. Indeed even reflection across the plane gives an \(H^1(\mathbb R^N)\) function (the weak derivatives are the even tangential and odd normal reflections); spatial cutoff followed by convolution with a smooth approximate identity approximates it in \(H^1\), and restriction preserves convergence. By the trace estimate in Lemma B.1, the traces converge in boundary \(L^2\). A subsequence converges almost everywhere; Fatou's lemma with the nonnegative weight yields the sharp inequality for \(w\). Translating tangentially preserves both energy and trace measure, giving the result for all centers.

Theorem 6.1 · Sharp Kato inequality (proof of Theorem 4.1)
\[\lambda_N\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|\operatorname{Tr}w(x)|^2}{|x-a|}\,d\sigma(x)\le\mathcal E[w],\quad w\in H^1(\mathcal H),\ a\in\partial\mathcal H.\tag{6.17}\]

For \(N\ge3\), the constant is optimal. The minimizing sequence above consists of admissible \(H^1\) functions; translating it proves optimality at every boundary center.

Proposition 6.2 · Fractional Hardy inequality (supplementary)

For \(d=N-1\ge2\) and \(f\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R^d)\),

\[\lambda_N\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{|f(x')|^2}{|x'|}\,dx'\le \int_{\mathbb R^d}|\zeta|\,|\widehat f(\zeta)|^2d\zeta.\tag{6.18}\]

The coefficient is optimal on this class.

Proof

Let \(v=\mathcal P_{x_N}*f\). By tangential Plancherel and Tonelli,

\[\|v\|_{L^2(\mathcal H)}^2=\int_{\mathbb R^d}\frac{|\widehat f(\zeta)|^2}{2|\zeta|}\,d\zeta.\]

This is finite: near zero the radial weight is proportional to \(r^{d-2}\), which is integrable for \(d\ge2\), and at infinity the Fourier transform decays rapidly. The gradient energy is finite by Lemma 6.2 (its calculation applies to all Schwartz data), so \(v\in H^1(\mathcal H)\) and its trace is \(f\). Apply Theorem 6.1 and use (6.6).

For optimality take the smooth logarithmic-annulus competitors \(w_R\) of §6.7 and put \(f_R=\operatorname{Tr}w_R\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^d)\subset\mathcal S\). The minimum-energy identity gives

\[\lambda_N\le\frac{\int |\zeta||\widehat f_R|^2d\zeta}{B_0[w_R]}\le\frac{\mathcal E[w_R]}{B_0[w_R]}\longrightarrow\lambda_N.\]

Thus the sharp bulk trace inequality and the fractional boundary inequality have the same constant. □

Proposition 6.3 · Nonattainment (supplementary)

Equality in Theorem 6.1 holds for \(w\in H^1(\mathcal H)\) only when \(w=0\). In particular, the sharp quotient is an infimum, not a minimum among nonzero functions with positive boundary mass.

Proof

By translation take \(a=0\). Approximate \(w\) in \(H^1(\mathcal H)\) by smooth compactly supported restrictions \(v_j\). For each fixed \(j\), the near-origin cutoff estimate from §6.7 lets us choose a cutoff radius so that the resulting \(w_j\) avoids the origin and \(\|w_j-v_j\|_{H^1}\le1/j\). Thus \(w_j\to w\) in \(H^1\). Applying Theorem 6.1 to the differences gives

\[\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|\operatorname{Tr}(w_j-w)|^2}{|x'|}d\sigma\le\lambda_N^{-1}\mathcal E[w_j-w]\to0.\]

Hence both bulk energy and weighted boundary mass converge, so \(Q_{\lambda_N,0}[w_j]\to Q_{\lambda_N,0}[w]\). For any compact subdomain \(K\Subset\mathcal H\), the positive smooth profile and its reciprocal have bounded first derivatives on a neighborhood of \(K\). Multiplication by \(1/\Phi_*\) therefore gives local strong \(H^1\) convergence. By (6.14),

\[\int_K\Phi_*^2|\nabla(w/\Phi_*)|^2dx=\lim_j\int_K\Phi_*^2|\nabla(w_j/\Phi_*)|^2dx\le Q_{\lambda_N,0}[w].\]

Exhausting the interior by compact subdomains gives the corresponding global inequality by monotone convergence. If equality holds in Kato, the right side is zero. The weak gradient of \(w/\Phi_*\) then vanishes locally throughout the connected half-space, so the ratio is a constant almost everywhere (by the local Poincaré inequality on overlapping balls). Thus \(w=c\Phi_*\). For \(c\ne0\) the trace on each boundary annulus equals \(c|x'|^{-\alpha_*}\), by local smoothness and uniqueness of traces. Its weighted mass is \(|c|^2|S^{d-1}|\int_0^\infty dr/r=\infty\), contradicting Theorem 6.1. Hence \(c=0\). □

This result completes the justification of the energy estimate in Chapter 4. For strictly subcritical coupling it gives a positive gradient margin. At equality the form is nonnegative but the sharpness sequence rules out any uniform positive gradient margin. In the supercritical case the same sequence supplies a negative-form test. Chapter 7 will construct the minimal solution at and below criticality, while Chapter 8 will exploit the negative form. The harmonic profile is a tool for those proofs, not a claim that the time-dependent solution is equal to that profile.

The local-to-global inequality in the proof of Proposition 6.3 holds for every translated critical profile. It is used in Theorem 7.5 to pass critical weighted-gradient control to the singular solution.

Chapter 6 · Takeaways

Harmonic extension translates normal differentiation into a tangential Fourier multiplier. Gaussian mixtures compute the characteristic gamma ratio. At critical homogeneity the positive profile absorbs the singular boundary term, leaving a nonnegative weighted gradient. Logarithmic radial scales prove optimality.
