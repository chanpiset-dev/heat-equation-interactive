Part II

3. Main theorems

For which couplings and trajectories can a finite solution exist?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Definition 2.3 and positive iteration. The harmonic profile is defined here; its derivation is deferred to Chapter 6.

Define the threshold and motion condition → state four theorems → distinguish hypotheses and solution classes.

3.1 The threshold and the motion condition

Call a coupling subcritical if it lies below the threshold, critical if equal, and supercritical if above. The constant is a mathematical boundary between regimes, not an observed peak temperature. The exact formula is a preview of Chapter 6.

We now return to the integral problem defined in Chapter 2. Throughout this chapter, assume \(N\ge3\), \(T>0\), \(\lambda>0\), and \(\xi\in C([0,T];\partial\mathcal H)\). The threshold is the constant

\[\lambda_N=2\left(\frac{\Gamma(N/4)}{\Gamma((N-2)/4)}\right)^2.\tag{3.1}\]

The Euler Gamma function is \(\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty r^{z-1}e^{-r}\,dr\) for \(z>0\). Its recurrence \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)\) follows by integration by parts. We call \(\lambda<\lambda_N\) subcritical, equality critical, and \(\lambda>\lambda_N\) supercritical. The names refer to the sharp form bound, not to a numerical temperature threshold.

Some results require the following additional property:

Assumption (M) · Uniformly vanishing parabolic displacement
\[\omega(h)=\sup_{\substack{0\le s<t\le T\\0<t-s\le h}}\frac{|\xi(t)-\xi(s)|}{\sqrt{t-s}},\qquad \lim_{h\downarrow0}\omega(h)=0.\tag{3.2}\]

Continuity alone does not imply (M). The supremum concerns every sufficiently short time interval, not merely increments from time zero. See Chapter 5 for examples.

3.2 Global existence

Theorem 3.1 · Global existence (paper Theorem 1.1)

Let \(u_0\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\) be nonnegative and nonzero, and assume \(0<\lambda\le\lambda_N\).

  1. A minimal nonnegative integral solution exists on \([0,T]\). Its integral representation is finite and valid at every \((x,t)\in D_\xi\) with \(t>0\), and \(u(x,t)>0\) there.
  2. Writing \(\|\cdot\|_2\) for the spatial \(L^2(\mathcal H)\) norm,
    \[\sup_{0\le t\le T}\|u(t)\|_2\le\|u_0\|_2,\qquad \lim_{t\downarrow0}\|u(t)-u_0\|_2=0.\tag{3.3}\]
  3. When \(\lambda<\lambda_N\), additionally \(u\in L^2(0,T;H^1(\mathcal H))\) and
    \[\|u(t)\|_2^2+2\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_N}\right)\int_0^t\|\nabla u(s)\|_2^2\,ds\le\|u_0\|_2^2\quad(0\le t\le T).\tag{3.4}\]

The critical case is included without a Hölder assumption on the trajectory. Minimality compares solutions by pointwise order; it does not claim that every solution agrees with the constructed one. The factor in front of the integrated gradient in (3.4) vanishes at equality. The theorem makes no global gradient claim there.

Proof roadmap

Approximate the singular coefficient by bounded nonnegative Robin coefficients that increase to it. Translation of the sharp Kato inequality gives a uniform spatial form estimate at each time without differentiating the trajectory. The energy bound, local boundedness and continuity away from the graph permit the limit. Positivity of the kernel yields strict positivity and the order argument in Chapter 2 establishes minimality. The detailed proof belongs to Chapter 7.

3.3 Supercritical nonexistence

Theorem 3.2 · Nonexistence (paper Theorem 1.2)

For the same compact nonnegative nonzero datum, assume \(\lambda>\lambda_N\) and (M). For every \(S\in(0,T]\), there is no finite nonnegative integral solution on \([0,S]\).

This excludes even local-in-time solvability in the specified class. It is stronger than a statement about a large temperature at the singular point: the integral formulation already excludes that point. The proof uses stationary supercritical divergence and a lower Gaussian comparison in moving coordinates. A negative quadratic form alone is not the entire nonexistence proof.

Example 3.1 · The role of the dimension

Using \(\Gamma(1)=1\) and \(\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt\pi\), we obtain \(\lambda_4=2/\pi\). For \(N=6\), \(\Gamma(3/2)=\sqrt\pi/2\) gives \(\lambda_6=\pi/2\). Thus the same coupling \(\lambda=1\) is supercritical in dimension four and subcritical in dimension six. With a Lipschitz trajectory and the stated compact data, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 give opposite conclusions in these two dimensions.

3.4 Harmonic weights and the singularity estimate

The remaining two theorems quantify the behavior close to the moving point. A useful way to guess a spatial weight is to freeze the center at zero and seek a stationary positive function with a power-law singularity. Since a stationary heat solution is harmonic in the interior, prescribe the boundary value \(|x'|^{-\alpha}\) and extend it harmonically. Differentiation in the normal direction decreases the homogeneity degree by one, exactly as multiplication by \(|x'|^{-1}\) does. The resulting normal derivative is \(C_N(\alpha)|x'|^{-1}\Phi_\alpha\); the scalar coefficient is computed in §6.4. A stationary harmonic weight is not an assertion that the moving solution itself is harmonic. When \(C_N(\alpha)>\lambda\), the excess supplies a useful margin in the upper comparison argument. More precisely, introduce the Poisson extension of the boundary power \(|x'|^{-\alpha}\), denoted here by \(\Phi_\alpha\) (the paper uses \(H_\alpha\)). For \(x_N>0\), define

\[\Phi_\alpha(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^{N-1}}\mathcal P_{x_N}(x'-y')|y'|^{-\alpha}\,dy',\quad \mathcal P_z(w)=\frac{\Gamma(N/2)}{\pi^{N/2}}\frac{z}{(|w|^2+z^2)^{N/2}}.\tag{3.5}\]

Here \(0<\alpha<N-2\). On the boundary away from zero the profile is defined by its trace \(|x'|^{-\alpha}\). It is positive, harmonic in the interior, homogeneous of degree \(-\alpha\), and comparable to \(|x|^{-\alpha}\). The homogeneity follows directly by substituting \(y'=r w'\) in (3.5); Chapter 6 develops the remaining properties and the normal derivative identity. Its characteristic coefficient is

\[C_N(\alpha)=2\frac{\Gamma((N-1-\alpha)/2)\Gamma((\alpha+1)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)\Gamma((N-2-\alpha)/2)}.\tag{3.6}\]

The maximum occurs at \(\alpha_*=(N-2)/2\) and equals \(\lambda_N\). For \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\), the two roots of \(C_N(\alpha)=\lambda\) satisfy \(0<\alpha_-<\alpha_*<\alpha_+<N-2\). This is a result proved using the Gamma-function properties in Chapter 6; the plotted curve below is an illustration, not its proof.

Theorem 3.3 · Upper singularity estimate (paper Theorem 1.3)

Assume \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\), (M), and the compact-data assumptions of Theorem 3.1. Given any \(\eta>0\), its minimal solution satisfies

\[u(x,t)\le C_\eta |x-\xi(t)|^{-\alpha_-(\lambda)-\eta},\quad (x,t)\in D_\xi,\quad 0<|x-\xi(t)|\le1.\tag{3.7}\]

The positive constant may depend on the dimension, coupling, terminal time, trajectory, datum and \(\eta\).

This is an upper bound, not an asymptotic equality. It neither proves a matching lower bound nor establishes that the endpoint exponent \(\alpha_-\) itself is attainable. We use \(\eta\) rather than the paper's \(\varepsilon\) here to distinguish the exponent loss from numerical regularization.

Theorem 3.4 · Weighted existence and uniqueness (paper Theorem 1.4)

Assume

  1. \(0<\lambda<\lambda_N\) and the trajectory satisfies (M);
  2. \(\alpha_-(\lambda)<\alpha<\alpha_*\);
  3. \(0\le u_0\in C(\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{\xi(0)\})\) with the bound
\[\sup_{x\in\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{\xi(0)\}}\frac{u_0(x)}{\Phi_\alpha(x-\xi(0))}<\infty.\]

There is exactly one nonnegative integral solution in the class

\[\sup_{(x,t)\in D_\xi}\frac{u(x,t)}{\Phi_\alpha(x-\xi(t))}<\infty.\tag{3.8}\]

This solution is also minimal among all nonnegative integral solutions for that datum.

The weight permits certain singular initial data, but imposes global control at infinity as well. Uniqueness here is restricted to the weighted class. No nonzero assumption is needed for this theorem. For a compact continuous datum, the ratio is bounded because the weight is positive and comparable to the radial power on the support.

How the upper estimate follows from weighted existence

Choose \(\alpha\in(\alpha_-,\min\{\alpha_*,\alpha_-+\eta\})\). Both constructions give a minimal solution for compact data, so they agree by comparison in both directions. The weighted estimate gives \(u\le M\Phi_\alpha\le C|x-\xi(t)|^{-\alpha}\). For distances at most one this implies (3.7). The construction itself uses a short-time contraction from the upper kernel comparison and the harmonic heat identity; it will be proved in Chapter 9.

Corollary 3.5 · Classification under (M) (paper Corollary 1.5)

For nonnegative nonzero compact continuous data and a trajectory satisfying (M), existence on \([0,T]\) is equivalent to \(0<\lambda\le\lambda_N\).

Proof. The forward implication is Theorem 3.2 by contradiction; the converse is Theorem 3.1. □

Chapter 3 · Takeaways

Existence includes criticality for all continuous paths. Nonexistence and weighted results require a separate motion condition. The weighted estimate is not an exact asymptotic or a general uniqueness theorem.

Check your understanding

At critical coupling, do we obtain a uniform integrated-gradient estimate? Does failure of (M) prove supercritical existence?

No to both. The displayed estimates and classification do not imply either conclusion.
