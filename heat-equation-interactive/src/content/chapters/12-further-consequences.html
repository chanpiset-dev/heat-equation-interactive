12. Further consequences

Which conclusions survive changes in dimension, motion, regularization or coupling?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Dimension obstruction → rough-motion certificates → robust approximation → time-dependent coupling → transformed diffusion and integrability.

12.1 The two-dimensional obstruction

Chapter 2 introduced the local integrability obstruction. Here we justify it for the stated nonnegative integral class and quantify what happens to regularized solutions.

The singular coefficient acts on a boundary of dimension \(N-1\). In polar coordinates on that boundary, the radial volume factor is \(r^{N-2}\). Thus multiplication by \(1/r\) leaves \(r^{N-3}\). In dimension two this is \(1/r\): its integral diverges at zero, even before any extra singularity in the solution is considered.

Proposition 12.1 · Dimension two (manuscript [4], Proposition 6.1)

Let \(\mathcal H=\mathbb R^2_+\), \(\lambda>0\), \(\xi\) be continuous and \(0\le f\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\), \(f\not\equiv0\). There is no finite nonnegative integral solution on any positive-length interval starting at zero with the continuous nonsingular trace of Chapter 2.

Proof

Suppose a solution exists. By positivity of the kernel and the boundary source, \(u\ge F=P_sf\) at all nonsingular positive-time observations, including boundary observations by Fatou's lemma. The free term is strictly positive: the datum is positive on a set of positive volume (also if its positive value is initially on the boundary, by continuity). Fix an interior observation \((x,t)\) and \(0<a<b<t\). For any \(R>0\), the boundary tube

\[K=\{(y,s):a\le s\le b,\ |y-\xi(s)|\le R\}\]

is compact, by continuity and boundedness of \(\xi\). Both \(F(y,s)\) and \(G(x,y,t-s)\) are continuous and strictly positive there, so their product has a positive minimum \(c\). Removing the single pole at each time does not change boundary measure. Tonelli's theorem now gives

\[\begin{aligned} (A_\xi u)(x,t)&\ge c\lambda\int_a^b\int_{0<|y-\xi(s)|<R}\frac{d\sigma(y)\,ds}{|y-\xi(s)|}\\&=2c\lambda(b-a)\int_0^R\frac{dr}{r}=+\infty.\end{aligned}\tag{12.1}\]

This contradicts finiteness of the integral equation. No modulus of motion is required. \(\square\)

Proposition 12.2 · Quantitative regularization obstruction (supplementary)

For the same dimension-two data, let \(u_\varepsilon\) be the bounded Robin solution with \(V_\varepsilon=\lambda/(|y-\xi(s)|^2+\varepsilon^2)^{1/2}\). At any fixed interior \((x,t)\), with the tube chosen as above,

\[u_\varepsilon(x,t)\ge F(x,t)+2c\lambda(b-a)\operatorname{arsinh}(R/\varepsilon).\tag{12.2}\]

In particular \(u_\varepsilon(x,t)\to+\infty\) at least logarithmically as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\).

Proof. The first positive Volterra iterate bounds \(u_\varepsilon\) below by \(F+A_{V_\varepsilon}F\). Restrict the integral to the same tube, on which \(GF\ge c\) independently of regularization. The integral of \((r^2+\varepsilon^2)^{-1/2}\) from zero to \(R\) is \(\operatorname{arsinh}(R/\varepsilon)\). Since \(\operatorname{arsinh}(q)=\log(q+\sqrt{1+q^2})\), the asserted divergence follows. This is a lower bound, not an asymptotic equality. \(\square\)

Corollary 12.2a · Divergence beyond logarithmic powers (supplementary)

Under Proposition 12.2, for every fixed integer \(k\ge1\), every fixed interior observation \((x,t)\) with \(t>0\), and any fixed \(R>0\), there is \(C_k>0\) independent of \(\varepsilon\) such that

\[u_\varepsilon(x,t)\ge C_k[\operatorname{arsinh}(R/\varepsilon)]^k.\tag{12.11}\]

In particular, for every real \(p>0\), \(u_\varepsilon(x,t)/[\log(1/\varepsilon)]^p\to+\infty\) as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\).

Proof by repeated positive integration

Choose closed intervals \(I_i=[a_i,b_i]\), each of positive length, with \(0<a_1<b_1<a_2<\cdots<b_k<t\). Write \((y_{k+1},s_{k+1})=(x,t)\). In the \(k\)-fold Volterra integral restrict \(s_i\in I_i\) and \(|y_i-\xi(s_i)|\le R\). The free evolution \(F(y_1,s_1)\) and each factor \(G(y_{i+1},y_i,s_{i+1}-s_i)\) have strictly positive minima on the corresponding compact sets: all time differences are bounded away from zero. Thus their product has a positive lower bound \(c_k\). Tonelli and translation in the one-dimensional boundary give

\[A_{V_\varepsilon}^kF(x,t)\ge c_k\prod_{i=1}^k\big[2\lambda|I_i|\operatorname{arsinh}(R/\varepsilon)\big].\]

Every iterate is bounded above by the bounded Robin solution. This proves (12.11) with \(C_k=c_k(2\lambda)^k\prod_i|I_i|\). Since \(\operatorname{arsinh}(R/\varepsilon)/\log(1/\varepsilon)\to1\), choosing any integer \(k>p\) proves the limit. The constants may deteriorate with \(k\); no power-law rate in \(1/\varepsilon\), exact asymptotic, or exponential rate follows. The argument also works at fixed nonsingular boundary observations by the bounded integral identity. \(\square\)

12.2 Half-Hölder motion: quantitative comparison

Condition (M) in Chapter 5 guarantees arbitrarily small normalized displacement on short windows. A uniformly half-Hölder trajectory may instead have a persistent nonzero bound \(L\): \(|\xi(t)-\xi(s)|\le L\sqrt{t-s}\). For example, \(\xi(t)=L\sqrt t\,e_1\) has exactly this optimal constant on every interval beginning at zero. The Gaussian comparison still supplies useful inequalities with finite losses.

\[\begin{aligned} R_+(\delta,L)&=(1-\delta)^{1-N/2}\exp\left(\frac{(1-\delta)L^2}{4\delta}\right),&0<\delta<1,\\ R_-(\delta,L)&=(1+\delta)^{1-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{(1+\delta)L^2}{4\delta}\right),&\delta>0.\end{aligned}\tag{12.3}\]

These are \(c_+/D_+\) and \(c_-/D_-\) evaluated with the uniform bound \(M\le L\); the factors \(D_\pm^{-1}\) arise from time substitution. For \(R_+\) the larger displacement bound increases the upper loss, whereas for \(R_-\) it decreases the lower multiplier.

Proposition 12.3 · Sufficient half-Hölder criteria (manuscripts [4], §6.1; [5], §8.6)

Under the uniform half-Hölder bound:

(i) If \(\lambda R_+(\delta,L)<C_N(\alpha)\) for some \(0<\alpha<N-2\) and \(0<\delta<1\), a datum bounded by a multiple of \(\Phi_\alpha\) in the initial moving coordinates admits a minimal integral solution, unique in that weighted class. In particular, if \(\lambda R_+<\lambda_N\), such a weight can be chosen (including one below \(\alpha_*\)).

(ii) For nonzero nonnegative compact data, if \(\lambda R_-(\delta,L)>\lambda_N\) for some \(\delta>0\), there is no finite nonnegative integral solution on an interval starting at zero.

Proof. For (i), the proof of weighted contraction needs only the strict inequality \(c_+\lambda/(D_+C_N(\alpha))<1\). Substituting the bound \(L\) gives this inequality uniformly on every window; continuity of the potentials requires only continuity of \(\xi\). Continuation and minimality are unchanged. The wider exponent range is the supplementary extension from Chapter 9; the manuscript's sufficient criterion chooses a weight below \(\alpha_*\). For (ii), the lower bound of Chapter 8 yields a stationary comparison at effective coupling at least \(\lambda R_-\). Its regularized solutions diverge, so positive iteration contradicts finiteness of the moving solution. Neither implication is reversible by this reasoning. \(\square\)

Proposition 12.4 · Optimized comparison losses (supplementary)

Set \(k=N/2-1>0\), \(m=L^2/4\). For \(L>0\), the best upper and lower multipliers within (12.3) are achieved at

\[\delta_+=\frac{2m}{m+\sqrt{m^2+4km}},\qquad\delta_-=\frac{m+\sqrt{m^2+4km}}{2k}.\tag{12.4}\]

The upper multiplier is minimized and the lower multiplier maximized. For \(L=0\) both limiting optima are one, approached as \(\delta\downarrow0\).

Proof and comparison thresholds

The log upper multiplier is \(-k\log(1-\delta)+m(1/\delta-1)\). Its derivative is \(k/(1-\delta)-m/\delta^2\); its sign equals the sign of \(k\delta^2+m\delta-m\). This polynomial is strictly increasing on the positive axis and changes sign between zero and one. Thus its unique root gives the minimum, and rationalizing the quadratic formula gives \(\delta_+\). The log lower multiplier is \(-k\log(1+\delta)-m(1/\delta+1)\), whose derivative is \(-k/(1+\delta)+m/\delta^2\). Its sign changes from positive to negative at the unique positive root of \(k\delta^2-m\delta-m\), giving \(\delta_-\). The endpoint limits at \(L=0\) follow directly from (12.3).

Write \(r_+(L)=\inf R_+\) and \(r_-(L)=\sup R_-\). The optimized sufficient nonexistence condition is \(\lambda/\lambda_N>1/r_-(L)\). Weighted solvability can be certified if \(\lambda/\lambda_N<1/r_+(L)\), with a suitable datum and exponent. At \(L=0\) these are strict inequalities, so the limiting nonattained optima cause no problem. For positive \(L\), \(r_+>1\), \(r_-<1\). These curves optimize only the Gaussian comparison family; they are not a sharp threshold for the moving problem. \(\square\)

Corollary 12.4a · Small-scale comparison criteria (supplementary)

Let \(L_0=\lim_{h\downarrow0}\omega(h)<\infty\). For an admissible weighted datum, weighted existence and uniqueness in that class hold if \(\lambda r_+(L_0)<C_N(\alpha)\). For nontrivial compact data, nonexistence holds if \(\lambda r_-(L_0)>\lambda_N\). Both inequalities are strict.

Proof and examples

The limit exists because \(\omega\) is nondecreasing in its argument. By (12.4), the optimized log multipliers are continuous for \(L>0\); at zero, taking \(\delta=L\) for sufficiently small \(L>0\) shows both losses tend to one, since \(R_+\ge1\) and \(R_-\le1\). Also \(r_+\) is nondecreasing and \(r_-\) nonincreasing in \(L\), by the corresponding inequalities for every fixed \(\delta\). By strictness choose \(L>L_0\) with the same strict inequality and then choose a permitted \(\delta\) realizing it (or approaching the limiting optimum). There exists \(\tau>0\) with \(\omega(\tau)\le L\). On each window of length at most \(\tau\), Chapter 9's contraction and restart apply in the upper case. For the lower case Chapter 8's stationary comparison on the first short window already contradicts finiteness. \(\square\)

For \(\xi(t)=L\sqrt t\,e_1+vt e_1\), \(\omega(h)\le L+|v|\sqrt h\), while the intervals \([0,h]\) give a lower bound tending to \(L\). Thus \(L_0=L\) even if the global half-Hölder bound is larger. When \(L_0=0\), this recovers condition (M); compact-data existence through criticality still follows from Chapter 7 without any such condition. Equality in a comparison threshold is not a certificate.

12.3 Order and approximation independence

Let \(B\ge0\) be a Borel boundary coefficient and define \(A_Bv=\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)B(y,s)v(y,s)d\sigma ds\). The extended-valued positive series

\[S(B,f)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty A_B^j(P_\cdot f)\tag{12.5}\]

is well-defined for nonnegative data with finite free evolution. Products with a zero factor are assigned zero, and pole values can be fixed arbitrarily on the null graph. Tonelli and monotone convergence give the extended fixed-point identity. A finite continuous representative with the prescribed trace is a minimal integral solution: any integral competitor with the boundary supersolution property dominates every partial sum. Chapters 7 and 9 establish this finiteness and continuity in their respective regimes.

Lemma 12.5 · Order and increasing approximation (manuscripts [4], Lemma 6.2; [5], Lemma 8.1)

For compact nonnegative continuous data, a common continuous trajectory and \(0\le\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\lambda_N\), if \(f_1\le f_2\), then \(u_{f_1,\lambda_1}\le u_{f_2,\lambda_2}\). If bounded continuous nonnegative \(B_j\uparrow V\) almost everywhere, their minimal solutions increase to the singular minimal solution on \(D_\xi\).

Proof

Order holds termwise by positivity and induction in (12.5). For a fixed series index \(k\), \(A_{B_j}^kF\uparrow A_V^kF\) by induction and monotone convergence; the product of the two nondecreasing nonnegative factors converges almost everywhere to the product at nonsingular source points. Monotone convergence for counting measure then exchanges the series and limit. The singular series equals the solution of Chapter 7 by applying this argument to its particular regularization. The same conclusion follows for every increasing approximation. The zero coupling case reduces to \(P_tf\). \(\square\)

Proposition 12.6 · Dominated, not necessarily monotone approximations (supplementary)

Suppose \(0\le B_j\le V\) almost everywhere, \(B_j\to V\) almost everywhere, each \(B_j\) is bounded and continuous, and the singular positive series \(S(V,f)\) is finite at the observation. For compact continuous nonnegative data, the bounded solutions converge there to \(S(V,f)\), even without monotonicity in \(j\).

Proof and scope

This is an application of Proposition 2.2; the argument is retained here for direct comparison with the approximation results.

Let \(F=P_\cdot f\). For each \(k\), the upper bound \(A_{B_j}^kF\le A_V^kF\) is immediate. We prove \(\liminf_j A_{B_j}^kF\ge A_V^kF\) by induction at every observation (including nonsingular boundary points). For \(k=0\) it is equality. For the induction step, \(B_j\to V\) with \(V\) finite off the null graph; at source points where \(V>0\), multiplication by the converging positive factors preserves the lower limit, and at \(V=0\) the desired integrand is zero. Fatou for the nonnegative kernel gives the claimed lower bound. Thus every fixed term converges in the extended sense. Another application of Fatou, now to counting measure, gives \(S(V,f)\le\liminf_j S(B_j,f)\le\limsup_j S(B_j,f)\le S(V,f)\). Finiteness gives ordinary convergence. The same argument works at any nonsingular point where the series equals the constructed solution. This proves pointwise convergence from below in the sense of domination, not monotonicity. The next corollary upgrades it and separates the case of divergence. \(\square\)

For example, one may choose the regularization index in the order \(1,4,2,6,3,8,\ldots\) with both subsequences tending to infinity. Each coefficient remains below the singular coefficient, although the sequence is not monotone. The proposition proves convergence whenever the minimal series is finite. It does not require that successive numerical temperatures increase.

Corollary 12.6a · Local uniform convergence and general divergence (supplementary)

Let \(f\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\), \(f\ge0\). Assume \(B_j\) are bounded continuous nonnegative coefficients and \(B_j\to V\) almost everywhere for boundary space-time measure. Define \(V\) arbitrarily on the null singular graph and assume it is finite elsewhere. Then:

(i) If \(B_j\le V\) almost everywhere and the singular positive series is a finite continuous minimal solution \(u\) on \(D_\xi\) (including the initial datum), then \(u_j\to u\) uniformly on every compact subset of \(D_\xi\).

(ii) If \(S(V,f)(x,t)=+\infty\), then \(u_j(x,t)\to+\infty\), with no upper domination assumption. These claims concern nonsingular observations at which the positive series is defined.

Proof by sequential Fatou and compactness

This is an application of Proposition 2.2; the argument is retained here for direct comparison with the approximation results.

Set \(F=P_\cdot f\), \(F(\cdot,0)=f\), and let \(z_j=(x_j,t_j)\to z=(x,t)\) in \(D_\xi\). We prove for each fixed \(k\) that

\[\liminf_j(A_{B_j}^kF)(z_j)\ge(A_V^kF)(z).\tag{12.12}\]

For \(k=0\), the free evolution is continuous up to the initial plane. For the step at \(t>0\), write the integrals on \(\partial\mathcal H\times(0,T)\) with the indicator \(s<t_j\), defining the integrand as zero outside that range. For almost every source with \(s<t\), the indicator is eventually one, the observation kernel tends to \(G(x,y,t-s)>0\), and the fixed-source instance of the induction hypothesis holds. Where \(V>0\), the factor \(B_j\) converges to a strictly positive finite number; multiplication preserves the lower bound even if the iterate diverges. Where \(V=0\), the required bound is zero (use the convention \(0\cdot\infty=0\)). Sources on the graph and the time slice \(s=t\) form null sets. Fatou gives (12.12). At \(t=0\), all positive-order terms on the right vanish, so nonnegativity suffices. The induction includes boundary and terminal observations because bounded-coefficient integral identities hold there.

Fatou for the counting measure gives \(\liminf_j u_j(z_j)\ge S(V,f)(z)\). In (i), comparison term by term yields \(u_j(z_j)\le u(z_j)\to u(z)\). This also covers time zero; if uniform convergence failed on a compact set, a subsequence of offending observations would converge in that set, contradicting the sequential conclusion. In (ii), take \(z_j=z\); the infinite lower bound proves the claim without any assumption \(B_j\le V\). \(\square\)

For example, at a supercritical compact-data observation where Chapter 8 proves stationary or moving comparison divergence, the singular series is infinite by comparison with its bounded regularizations. Even nonmonotone nonnegative approximations that overshoot the coefficient must diverge there if they converge almost everywhere. The finite-limit assertion does not cover overshooting sequences; neither result covers signed coefficients.

12.4 Time-dependent coupling and delayed supercriticality

Replace \(\lambda\) by a continuous nonnegative function \(\lambda(t)\) and define \(V(y,s)=\lambda(s)/|y-\xi(s)|\). The coefficient is evaluated at the source time inside the boundary integral, not at the observation time.

Proposition 12.7 · Time-dependent coupling (manuscripts [4], Proposition 6.3; [5], Proposition 8.1)

For nontrivial compact nonnegative continuous data:

(i) If \(0\le\lambda(t)\le\lambda_N\) on \([0,T]\), there exists a minimal nonnegative integral solution.

(ii) If (M) holds and \(\lambda(t)\ge\mu>\lambda_N\) throughout some initial interval \([0,S]\), no finite nonnegative integral solution exists on any positive-length interval starting at zero.

Proof

Set \(b_j(y,s)=\lambda(s)(|y-\xi(s)|^2+j^{-2})^{-1/2}\). The bounded construction of Chapter 7 applies, and its energy estimate now reads

\[\|u_j(t)\|_2^2+2\int_0^t\left(1-\frac{\lambda(s)}{\lambda_N}\right)\|\nabla u_j(s)\|_2^2ds\le\|f\|_2^2.\tag{12.6}\]

This follows by the translated Kato inequality at every source time. In (i), the coefficient in the gradient integral is nonnegative, hence the global \(L^2\) estimate is uniform. On cylinders away from the graph, the Robin coefficients are uniformly bounded by the maximum coupling divided by a positive distance. The local Moser and continuity estimates, including the initial extension, are therefore unchanged. Monotone convergence gives the finite integral identity and the Fatou/positive-iteration argument gives minimality. At critical times the estimate does not give uniform ordinary gradient control.

For (ii), on any shorter positive initial interval the hypothetical solution dominates the constant-\(\mu\) regularized iterates, by positivity and the boundary supersolution inequality. Chapter 8 establishes divergence under (M), giving a contradiction. \(\square\)

Corollary 12.7a · Time-weighted gradient estimate (supplementary)

In Proposition 12.7(i), put \(c(s)=1-\lambda(s)/\lambda_N\ge0\). The minimal solution satisfies

\[\|u(t)\|_2^2+2\int_0^t\int_{\mathcal H} c(s)|\nabla u(x,s)|^2dx\,ds\le\|f\|_2^2.\tag{12.13}\]

The gradient here is the local weak spatial gradient in the interior; the weighted integrand is defined to be zero where \(c=0\). No unweighted global gradient bound is asserted on critical time sets.

Proof: interior exhaustion and weak lower semicontinuity

By the monotone construction, \(u_j\uparrow u\) and the uniform \(L^2\) bound holds at every time. Monotone convergence of squares proves \(\|u_j(t)\|_2^2\to\|u(t)\|_2^2\). On each compact interior space-time cylinder away from time zero, the heat equation and the uniform \(L^2\) bound yield a uniform local gradient bound: testing against \(\chi^2 u_j\) with compact spatial and temporal cutoffs and absorbing the cross term gives \(\int\chi^2|\nabla u_j|^2\le C_\chi\int_{\operatorname{supp}\chi}u_j^2\). The same inequality works for cutoffs equal to one through a terminal time, by retaining the nonnegative terminal term. Local strong \(L^2\) convergence follows by domination by \(u\), so weak gradient limits are the distributional spatial gradient of \(u\). Multiplying by the bounded continuous scalar \(\sqrt{c(s)}\) preserves weak convergence in \(L^2\) on each cylinder. Consequently the integral of \(c|\nabla u|^2\) there is bounded by the liminf of the approximate weighted integrals. Equivalently, take any exhaustion of \(\mathcal H\times(0,t)\) by relatively compact interior subsets, obtain the bound on each by weak lower semicontinuity (using a subsequence attaining the global liminf), and let the exhaustion increase to the full set by monotone convergence. Together with (12.6) and the norm limit this proves (12.13) for each \(t>0\); at zero it is immediate. Where \(c\) is bounded below by a positive constant, this recovers ordinary integrated gradient control. \(\square\)

Proposition 12.8 · A later supercritical interval (supplementary)

Assume (M) and the compact-data hypotheses above. If \(\lambda\ge\mu>\lambda_N\) on a nondegenerate subinterval \([a,b]\subset[0,T]\), there is no finite integral solution on \([0,T]\). In particular, for continuous \(\lambda\), existence on a prescribed closed interval \([0,S]\) is equivalent to \(\lambda(s)\le\lambda_N\) for every \(s\in[0,S]\) (with (M) on that interval).

Proof: restart without assuming compactness of the intermediate datum

If \(a=0\), Proposition 12.7 applies. Otherwise choose \(a\) strictly inside the proposed solution interval, reducing the supercritical window if necessary. Put \(g(z)=u(z,a)\) in the interior. For an interior observation \(x\) and \(a<t<b\), the full identity at time \(a\), positivity, Tonelli and Neumann composition give

\[P_{t-a}g(x)=P_tf(x)+\int_0^a\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)V(y,s)u(y,s)d\sigma ds.\tag{12.7}\]

The right side is bounded by \(u(x,t)\), so is finite. We use only this interior restart and its nonnegative Fatou extension, not an assumption that \(g\) is compact or harmonic-weighted. In fact \(g\ge P_af>0\) throughout the interior. Choose a nonzero nonnegative continuous bump \(g_0\) supported in a compact interior ball, small enough that \(g_0\le P_af\) there. Then the equation split at \(a\) implies \(u(x,t)\ge P_{t-a}g_0(x)+\int_a^t\int G\,\mu|y-\xi(s)|^{-1}u\). The inequality extends to nonsingular boundary observations by Fatou, with continuous bounded free datum \(g_0\). Iterate against bounded constant-coupling regularizations on the shifted interval. The shifted trajectory inherits (M), so the lower Gaussian comparison and stationary divergence of Chapter 8 apply to \(g_0\). Sending the regularization index to infinity contradicts finiteness at any interior observation after \(a\) sufficiently close to \(a\).

If a continuous coupling exceeds \(\lambda_N\) anywhere on a positive-length closed interval, continuity supplies a nondegenerate subinterval with a strictly supercritical lower bound, even if the original point is an endpoint. Conversely Proposition 12.7(i) supplies existence when the entire coupling remains at or below the threshold. This does not assert anything about times beyond the prescribed interval or trajectories failing (M). \(\square\)

Corollary 12.8a · Local supercritical episodes (supplementary)

Keep the continuous nonnegative coupling, continuous trajectory and nontrivial compact nonnegative datum. Suppose a nondegenerate interval \([a,b]\subset[0,T]\) has \(\lambda(s)\ge\mu>0\), and the restricted trajectory has limiting displacement modulus \(L_{0,[a,b]}<\infty\) with \(\mu r_-(L_{0,[a,b]})>\lambda_N\). Then no finite nonnegative integral solution exists on \([0,T]\). In particular it suffices that \(\mu>\lambda_N\) and (M) holds only on \([a,b]\).

Proof. If \(a=0\), use the lower comparison of Corollary 12.4a directly. Otherwise the same Tonelli restart (12.7) and compact minorant \(g_0\le P_af\) used in Proposition 12.8 reduce the supersolution inequality to the shifted window. The restricted normalized modulus controls this window independently of the motion before \(a\) or after \(b\). Choose a still shorter window and \(\delta>0\) as in Corollary 12.4a so the constant-\(\mu\) effective lower coupling is strictly supercritical. Positive iteration against its bounded stationary comparison yields divergence at any interior observation in that window, a contradiction. \(\square\)

For instance, a trajectory can behave as \(t^{1/3}e_1\) near zero and then be Lipschitz on \([a,b]\) for \(a>0\). An episode with \(\lambda>\lambda_N\) on that later interval is enough. The entire trajectory need not satisfy (M). The converse is not claimed for a trajectory failing the comparison criteria.

12.5 Stationary ground-state transformation

Translate a stationary pole to zero. A positive harmonic function can remove the singular Robin coefficient when its characteristic coefficient matches the coupling. Define \(w=u/\Phi_\alpha\) with \(0<\alpha<N-2\), for smooth functions away from the pole.

\[\begin{aligned}\partial_tu&=\Phi_\alpha\partial_tw,\\\Delta u&=\Phi_\alpha\Delta w+2\nabla\Phi_\alpha\cdot\nabla w,\\\partial_\nu u&=\Phi_\alpha\partial_\nu w+w\partial_\nu\Phi_\alpha.\end{aligned}\]
Proposition 12.9 · Ground-state transformation (manuscripts [4], §6.3; [5], §8.8)
\[\boxed{\partial_tw=\Phi_\alpha^{-2}\operatorname{div}(\Phi_\alpha^2\nabla w),\quad \partial_\nu w=\frac{\lambda-C_N(\alpha)}{|x'|}w.}\tag{12.8}\]

The boundary condition is homogeneous Neumann if \(C_N(\alpha)=\lambda\). At strict subcriticality either characteristic root matches; at criticality only \(\alpha_*\) does.

Derivation. Harmonicity eliminates \(w\Delta\Phi_\alpha\). The identity \(\nabla\log\Phi_\alpha=\nabla\Phi_\alpha/\Phi_\alpha\) rewrites the interior operator in divergence form. On the plane use \(\partial_\nu\Phi_\alpha=C_N(\alpha)|x'|^{-1}\Phi_\alpha\), then divide by the positive profile. This is an identity away from the pole; it does not select a global operator realization or prescribe behavior at the singular point. \(\square\)

Proposition 12.10 · Weighted form identity for any harmonic exponent (supplementary)

For real smooth \(w\) compactly supported in \(\overline{\mathcal H}\setminus\{0\}\),

\[Q_{\lambda,0}[\Phi_\alpha w]=\int_{\mathcal H}\Phi_\alpha^2|\nabla w|^2dx+(C_N(\alpha)-\lambda)\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{\Phi_\alpha^2w^2}{|x'|}d\sigma.\tag{12.9}\]

Multiplication by \(\Phi_\alpha\) is an isometry from \(L^2(\Phi_\alpha^2dx)\) to \(L^2(dx)\). At a matching root the quadratic form on these tests is exactly the weighted gradient energy.

Proof

Expand \(|\nabla(\Phi_\alpha w)|^2=\Phi_\alpha^2|\nabla w|^2+\nabla\Phi_\alpha\cdot\nabla(\Phi_\alpha w^2)\). Integration by parts has no small-sphere or infinity contribution because of the support restriction. Harmonicity and the normal derivative identity reduce the second term to \(C_N(\alpha)\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\Phi_\alpha^2w^2/|x'|\). Subtract the original boundary term. The isometry follows by integration of \(|\Phi_\alpha w|^2\), and its inverse divides by the strictly positive profile. Extension to a closed weighted form requires specifying its domain; no unrestricted uniqueness claim follows. \(\square\)

In dimension four the critical profile has \(\Phi_1(0,z)=2/(\pi z)\). For \(\lambda_4=2/\pi\), the transformed normal boundary coefficient vanishes. The test function \(w=1\) corresponds formally to the harmonic stationary solution \(u=\Phi_1\), which is not a compact datum and is not an \(H^1\) extremizer. The transformation isolates the singular factor rather than making it disappear from the volume measure.

What happens to diffusion?

The transformation does not remove the singularity from every part of the equation. In the interior the operator becomes

\[\mathcal L_\alpha w=\Delta w+2\nabla\log\Phi_\alpha\cdot\nabla w,\qquad d\mu_\alpha=\Phi_\alpha^2dx.\tag{12.14}\]

For compact smooth test functions supported away from the pole and satisfying the matched Neumann condition where needed, integration by parts gives \(\int v\mathcal L_\alpha w\,d\mu_\alpha=-\int\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\,d\mu_\alpha\). Indeed, multiply the divergence-form expression by \(v\Phi_\alpha^2\) and the boundary term is zero when \(\partial_\nu w=0\). The weighted measure is therefore natural for the transformed diffusion. This calculation alone does not construct a global weighted semigroup or establish its boundary behavior at the pole.

Exact dimension-four drift example

In \(N=4\), \(\alpha=1\), write \(r=|x'|\), \(z=x_N>0\) and \(\vartheta=\arctan(r/z)\). From \(\Phi_1=2\vartheta/(\pi r)\), for \(r>0\) direct differentiation gives

\[b_r=2\left(\frac{z}{(r^2+z^2)\vartheta}-\frac1r\right),\qquad b_z=-\frac{2r}{(r^2+z^2)\vartheta}.\]

On the normal axis, taking the limit \(r\downarrow0\) gives \(b_r=0\), \(b_z=-2/z\). Thus the differential operator has a singular drift even at the exact matching coupling \(\lambda_4=2/\pi\). The radial derivative in the tangential variables means \(b_r x'/r\). This is the drift of the transformed operator, not a prescribed motion of the heat source.

12.6 Homogeneity and local integrability

The harmonic profile satisfies \(\Phi_\alpha(r\theta)=r^{-\alpha}\Phi_\alpha(\theta)\). Its angular factor is continuous and strictly positive on the closed unit hemisphere, so it has finite positive upper and lower bounds. The boundary trace is exactly \(|x'|^{-\alpha}\). These observations yield explicit integrability tests.

Proposition 12.11 · Local power integrability (manuscript criterion and supplementary generalization)

For \(p>0\), \(k\in\mathbb R\), and \(0<\alpha<N-2\),

\[\begin{aligned}\int_{B_1\cap\mathcal H}\Phi_\alpha^p|x|^{-k}dx<\infty &\iff p\alpha+k<N,\\\int_{B_1\cap\partial\mathcal H}\Phi_\alpha^p|x'|^{-k}d\sigma<\infty &\iff p\alpha+k<N-1.\end{aligned}\tag{12.10}\]

The first criterion with \(p=1,\alpha=\alpha_-\) is the manuscript's integrability observation. The general powers and boundary criterion are supplementary. At equality the truncated integral diverges logarithmically.

Proof. In volume polar coordinates the integrand and Jacobian separate. The angular integral of \(\Phi_\alpha(\theta)^p\) is finite and positive. The radial factor is \(r^{N-1-p\alpha-k}\). It integrates on \((0,1)\) precisely if its exponent exceeds \(-1\). On the boundary the angular factor is one and the Jacobian is \(r^{N-2}\); the same calculation gives the second condition. At the endpoint the radial factor is \(1/r\). \(\square\)

Examples. The bare boundary flux of a harmonic profile is locally integrable for \(\alpha<N-2\). The weighted boundary mass of its square with factor \(1/r\) is finite precisely when \(2\alpha<N-2\); at \(\alpha_*\) it diverges logarithmically, consistent with Chapter 6's sharpness argument. The volume \(L^2\) condition is instead \(2\alpha<N\). Local conditions do not control infinity. In particular \(k<N-\alpha_-\) is only an integrability test for a possible singular datum \(|x|^{-k}\) against \(\Phi_{\alpha_-}\), not a proved existence threshold.

Corollary 12.11a · Global powers and weighted volume (supplementary)

For the powers of Proposition 12.11, integrability on \(\mathcal H\setminus B_1\) holds precisely when \(p\alpha+k>N\); on the exterior boundary it holds precisely when \(p\alpha+k>N-1\). No unmodified homogeneous power of this type is integrable on the entire corresponding domain. At equality the radial integral is logarithmically divergent at both endpoints.

If \(2\alpha<N\), then with \(C_\alpha=(N-2\alpha)^{-1}\int_{\mathbb S^{N-1}_+}\Phi_\alpha(\theta)^2d\theta>0\),

\[\mu_\alpha(B_R\cap\mathcal H)=C_\alpha R^{N-2\alpha},\qquad \mu_{\alpha_*}(B_R\cap\mathcal H)=C_*R^2.\tag{12.15}\]
Proof and interpretation

The radial integral on \((1,\infty)\) of \(r^{d-1-p\alpha-k}\) converges exactly when the exponent is strictly less than \(-1\), where \(d=N\) or \(N-1\). This is incompatible with the corresponding strict near-zero condition. Taking \(p=2,k=0\) on \((0,R)\) gives (12.15). If \(2\alpha\ge N\), the weighted measure of every such ball is infinite, so the power-law finite-volume expression is unavailable. At criticality \(N-2\alpha_*=2\); the radial weight in the energy of a logarithmic cutoff is then proportional to \(dr/r\), as in Chapter 6. This is a volume-scaling identity, not an identification of the transformed operator with the ordinary two-dimensional heat equation. \(\square\)

Chapter 12 · Takeaways

Dimension two has a direct integrability obstruction. Uniform half-Hölder motion yields quantitative sufficient criteria, not a complete classification. Positive series yield comparison and robust approximation from below. Under (M), a later supercritical interval rules out a solution on the full interval. A stationary harmonic transformation reorganizes the equation; power integrability alone cannot determine solvability.

The approximation and order results in §12.3 follow from Proposition 2.2.

Bridge to Chapter 13

Local motion certificates and local supercritical episodes narrow the unclassified regime but do not determine its sharp boundary. Robust approximation at a fixed coefficient does not itself imply stability when the trajectory moves, since a shifted pole need not be dominated by the original potential. Critical weighted volume scaling suggests weighted kernel methods for lower singularity bounds and admissible data; it does not settle either question. Quantitative dimension-two divergence beyond logarithmic powers leaves the exact growth rate open. All corollaries labelled supplementary are deductions from the existing theory, not claims of priority in the literature.
