2.1 Domain and moving singularity

Definition 2.1 · Half-space and trajectory

Let \(N\ge3\) and \(T>0\). Define the upper half-space, its closure and its boundary by

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal{H}&=\{x=(x',x_N)\in\mathbb R^N:x_N>0\},\\\overline{\mathcal{H}}&=\{x_N\ge0\},\\\partial \mathcal{H}&=\mathbb R^{N-1}\times\{0\}\end{aligned}\tag{2.1}\]

The trajectory \(\xi:[0,T]\to\partial \mathcal{H}\) is continuous, and \(\nu=-e_N\) is the outward unit normal. Thus \(\partial_\nu=-\partial_{x_N}\).

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal G_\xi&=\{(\xi(t),t):0\le t\le T\},\\\Sigma_\xi&=(\partial \mathcal{H}\times(0,T))\setminus\mathcal G_\xi,\\D_\xi&=(\overline{\mathcal{H}}\times[0,T])\setminus\mathcal G_\xi.\end{aligned}\tag{2.2}\]
Problem 2.1 · Initial-boundary value problem (research paper (1.1))
\[\begin{cases}\partial_tu=\Delta u&\text{in }\mathcal{H}\times(0,T),\\[3pt]\displaystyle\partial_\nu u=\frac{\lambda}{|x-\xi(t)|}u&\text{on }\Sigma_\xi,\\[3pt]u(\cdot,0)=u_0&\text{in }\mathcal{H}.\end{cases}\tag{2.3}\]

Assume \(\lambda>0\), \(u_0\ge0\), and \(u_0\in C(\overline{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{\xi(0)\})\). The main existence and nonexistence theorems further assume \(u_0\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal{H}})\) and \(u_0\not\equiv0\). The weighted theorem has a separate initial-data condition, to be stated in Chapter 3.

Assumptions · Three classes of initial data
SettingData and role
General formulation\(0\le u_0\in C(\overline{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{\xi(0)\})\). This specifies the datum, not a solvability theorem.
Main results (paper Theorems 1.1–1.3)\(0\le u_0\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal{H}})\), \(u_0\not\equiv0\). Such data are bounded, integrable and square integrable. Nontriviality is essential for the stated nonexistence and strict positivity conclusions.
Weighted result (paper Theorem 1.4)\(0\le u_0\in C(\overline{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{\xi(0)\})\) with a global bound by a positive harmonic reference profile (defined precisely in §3.4). The admissible exponents are developed in Chapters 3 and 9; this class may include singular data and does not require compact support.

The compact-data and weighted-data hypotheses belong to different results. If \(u_0=0\), then \(P_tu_0=0\) and \(A_\xi 0=0\) in the integral equation, for every coupling. The zero solution explains the nonzero-data hypothesis of Theorem 3.2.

Compatibility at the initial time. A classical solution smooth up to the corner \(t=0\) may require \(\partial_\nu u_0=Vu_0\) there. Definition 2.3 below imposes an initial value and continuity away from the graph, but does not prescribe a normal derivative at \(t=0\). In particular, the integral formulation accommodates compact continuous data without a classical corner compatibility condition.

\(C_c(\overline{\mathcal{H}})\) denotes continuous functions with compact support on the closed half-space; they need not vanish on the boundary. The measure \(d\sigma\) is Lebesgue surface measure on the boundary. Continuity on \(D_\xi\) is understood in the relative topology.

Background: Sobolev spaces and boundary traces
\[H^1(\mathcal{H})=\{w\in L^2(\mathcal{H}):\partial_jw\in L^2(\mathcal{H})\ (1\le j\le N)\}.\]

Here the derivatives are weak derivatives. The Sobolev trace \(\operatorname{Tr}:H^1(\mathcal{H})\to L^2(\partial \mathcal{H})\) extends boundary values of smooth functions. The definition of integral solution uses continuous boundary values away from the singularity; identifying these with Sobolev traces requires a separate argument.

For precise definitions, the trace inequality and weak convergence, see Appendix B — Functional analysis primer.

2.2 What do we mean by a solution?

The singular boundary equation is not meaningful pointwise at the pole. Away from it, a classical solution has the indicated derivatives, but the existence theorem uses a formulation that can be justified by positive approximation. Here is the distinction before deriving the integral equation.

NotionRequired conditions
Classical solutionThe necessary derivatives exist, and the equation and boundary conditions hold pointwise.
Weak solutionAn integral identity holds against test functions, with appropriate Sobolev traces.
Integral solutionSatisfies the continuity, initial condition and finite integral equality in Definition 2.3.

Definition 2.3 below will specify the integral class exactly. A Sobolev trace is the boundary value defined by continuous extension from smooth functions in an energy norm; see Appendix B.3. No equivalence is assumed without proof.

2.3 Heat kernel and reflection

The free-space heat kernel has unit mass and is the fundamental solution describing diffusion from initial data concentrated at a point.

Derivation of the free kernel by Fourier transform

For smooth rapidly decreasing initial data on \(\mathbb R^N\), use the unitary convention \(\widehat f(\zeta)=(2\pi)^{-N/2}\int e^{-ix\cdot\zeta}f(x)\,dx\). Taking the spatial transform gives the scalar ODE

\[\partial_t\widehat u=-|\zeta|^2\widehat u,\qquad \widehat u(\zeta,t)=e^{-t|\zeta|^2}\widehat f(\zeta).\]

The Gaussian Fourier integral and the convolution theorem then give \(u=g_t*f\), where \(\widehat g_t=(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-t|\zeta|^2}\). This yields the formula below; direct differentiation verifies it without requiring Fourier theory.

\[g_t(z)=(4\pi t)^{-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{|z|^2}{4t}\right),\qquad t>0.\tag{2.4}\]
Derivation of the free kernel identities

Direct differentiation gives

\[\partial_tg_t=\left(-\frac N{2t}+\frac{|z|^2}{4t^2}\right)g_t=\Delta g_t.\]

Gaussian integration gives \(\int_{\mathbb R^N}g_t=1\). Substitution \(z=\sqrt t\,w\) shows that \(g_t\) concentrates at the origin as \(t\downarrow0\). This is the approximate identity property.

To satisfy the Neumann condition on the half-space, reflect a source \(y=(y',y_N)\) to \(y^*=(y',-y_N)\). Adding the two free-space kernels cancels their normal derivatives at the boundary.

Definition 2.2 · Neumann heat kernel
\[G(x,y,t)=g_t(x-y)+g_t(x-y^*).\tag{2.5}\]

Proof · Derivative at the boundary

Let the source on the half-line be \(a>0\) and let \(g_t^{(1)}\) denote the one-dimensional heat kernel. Using \(\partial_xg_t^{(1)}(x-a)=-(x-a)g_t^{(1)}(x-a)/(2t)\), we obtain

\[\left.\partial_x G(x,a,t)\right|_{x=0}=\frac a{2t}g_t^{(1)}(-a)-\frac a{2t}g_t^{(1)}(a)=0.\]

The final equality follows from evenness of the Gaussian. For the half-space, the same calculation applies in the normal direction at \(x_N=0\).□

2.4 Neumann heat semigroup

Define the operator that evolves initial data \(f\) under free Neumann heat flow.

\[P_tf(x)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t)f(y)\,dy,\qquad P_0f=f.\tag{2.6}\]
Lemma 2.1 · Neumann composition (research paper Lemma 2.1)

For \(a,b>0\),

\[\int_{\mathcal{H}}G(x,z,a)G(z,y,b)\,dz=G(x,y,a+b).\tag{2.7}\]

Consequently \(P_aP_b=P_{a+b}\) for functions for which the integrals are defined.

Why the initial value is recovered

For bounded continuous \(f\) on the closed half-space, extend it evenly across the plane, calling the extension \(\widetilde f\). Then \(P_tf=(g_t*\widetilde f)|_\mathcal{H}\). Fix \(x\) and split the convolution difference into \(|z|<\delta\) and \(|z|\ge\delta\).

\[|P_tf(x)-f(x)|\le \sup_{|z|<\delta}|\widetilde f(x-z)-\widetilde f(x)|+2\|f\|_\infty\int_{|w|\ge\delta/\sqrt t}g_1(w)\,dw.\]

Choose \(\delta\) by continuity and then send \(t\downarrow0\); the Gaussian tail vanishes. In \(L^2\), Minkowski's inequality and continuity of translations in \(L^2(\mathbb R^N)\) yield the same conclusion in norm. The latter translation fact follows by approximating by smooth compactly supported functions.

□

Proof sketch

Reflect the integration variable in the image term of the first kernel. Since the second kernel is even in the normal variable, the two half-space integrals combine into a whole-space Gaussian convolution. The identity \(g_a*g_b=g_{a+b}\) yields the result. The formula also holds for boundary source points, where the two terms coincide.□

The same reflection argument gives \(P_t1=1\). Pointwise initial convergence for bounded continuous data and strong initial convergence for \(L^2\) data follow from the approximate identity property.

Lemma 2.1a · \(L^2\) contraction of the Neumann semigroup

For every \(f\in L^2(\mathcal{H})\) and \(t\ge0\),

\[\|P_tf\|_{L^2(\mathcal{H})}\le\|f\|_{L^2(\mathcal{H})}.\tag{2.6a}\]
Proof of the Neumann semigroup contraction

For \(t>0\), the positive symmetric kernel satisfies \(\int_{\mathcal{H}} G(x,y,t)\,dy=1\) and, by symmetry, \(\int_{\mathcal{H}} G(x,y,t)\,dx=1\). Cauchy–Schwarz with respect to the probability measure \(G(x,y,t)dy\) (equivalently Jensen's inequality) gives

\[|P_tf(x)|^2\le \int_{\mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t)|f(y)|^2\,dy.\]

Integrating in \(x\) and using Tonelli's theorem yields

\[\begin{aligned}\|P_tf\|_2^2&\le\int_{\mathcal{H}} |f(y)|^2\left(\int_{\mathcal{H}} G(x,y,t)\,dx\right)dy\\&=\|f\|_2^2.\end{aligned}\]

The case \(t=0\) is equality. The nonnegative integral on the right is finite for almost every \(x\), so the argument also establishes the bounded operator on \(L^2\).

□

Why we need this. The free heat evolution cannot increase the squared \(L^2\) norm. Combined with positivity, the approximate identity property and the sharp energy estimate, this will help recover the strong initial trace in Chapter 7. See Appendix B (preliminary) for the underlying spaces.

Example 2.1 · Evolution of Gaussian initial data

Let \(f(x)=\exp(-|x|^2/(4b_0))\), \(b_0>0\). Its even extension is \((4\pi b_0)^{N/2}g_{b_0}\), so the semigroup law gives

\[P_tf(x)=\left(\frac{b_0}{b_0+t}\right)^{N/2}e^{-|x|^2/(4(b_0+t))}.\tag{2.8}\]

Figure 1.1 also plots a section of this formula. Since these initial data are not compactly supported, this is an example of free heat evolution rather than an application of the main theorem.

2.5 Boundary integral representation

Instead of treating the singular Robin condition directly, use the Neumann heat kernel to express the boundary contribution as an integral. Fix an interior observation point \(x\in \mathcal{H}\) and time \(t>0\). Assume sufficient smoothness and decay to discard boundary terms at spatial infinity.

Step 1 · Multiply by the backward heat kernel

\[I(s)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t-s)u(y,s)\,dy,\qquad 0<s<t.\]

Using \(\partial_sG=-\Delta_yG\) and \(\partial_su=\Delta u\) produces the difference of two interior integrals.

\[I'(s)=\int_{\mathcal{H}}(G\Delta u-u\Delta_yG)\,dy.\]

Step 2 · Extract the boundary term with Green’s identity

\[I'(s)=\int_{\partial \mathcal{H}}(G\partial_\nu u-u\partial_\nu G)\,d\sigma.\]

The Neumann heat kernel satisfies \(\partial_\nu G=0\). Substitute the Robin condition.

\[I'(s)=\lambda\int_{\partial \mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t-s)\frac{u(y,s)}{|y-\xi(s)|}\,d\sigma(y).\]

Step 3 · Integrate in time and use the initial condition

The limit as \(s\uparrow t\) is \(u(x,t)\) by the approximate identity property; at \(s=0\) the term is \(P_tu_0(x)\). Integrating in time yields the following equation.

Boundary integral equation (research paper (1.3))
\[\begin{aligned}u(x,t)={}&P_tu_0(x)\\&+\lambda\int_0^t\!\int_{\partial \mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t-s)\frac{u(y,s)}{|y-\xi(s)|}\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\end{aligned}\tag{2.9}\]

The boundary operator and positivity

For a nonnegative measurable function \(v\) on the nonsingular cylinder define, allowing infinity,

\[(A_\xi v)(x,t)=\lambda\int_0^t\int_{\partial \mathcal{H}}G(x,y,t-s)\frac{v(y,s)}{|y-\xi(s)|}\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\]

See boundary mass identity (2.10) and Definition 2.3.

We assign the integrand zero on the singular graph; this changes no surface-time integral: each time section is a singleton of surface measure zero. The kernel and coefficient are nonnegative. Thus \(0\le v\le w\) implies \(A_\xi v\le A_\xi w\). The integral equation is \(u=F+A_\xi u\), with \(F=P_\cdot u_0\). For bounded nonnegative coefficients, repeated substitution motivates the positive series \(F+AF+A^2F+\cdots\). In the singular setting the formal iterates can be infinite; existence requires a separate finiteness and regularity argument.

Example · A scalar Volterra equation

As a simplified analogue, solve \(w(t)=1+b\int_0^t w(s)\,ds\) for \(b\ge0\). Starting at \(w_0=1\), the successive iterates are \(w_k(t)=\sum_{j=0}^k(bt)^j/j!\), increasing to \(e^{bt}\). This explicit toy model illustrates positive iteration, not the singular PDE.

Exact scalar formula with \(b=1\); the dashed curve is the limit. Play increases the iteration index \(k\), not physical time. The analogous PDE iterates require separate finiteness and continuity estimates.

The free diffusion term is augmented by the accumulated contributions of past boundary flux to the observation point. Because the unknown boundary value \(u(y,s)\) also appears on the right-hand side, this is not merely an integral of a prescribed source.

Lemma 2.2 · Boundary mass of the heat kernel (research paper (2.1))
\[\int_{\partial \mathcal{H}}G(x,y,h)\,d\sigma(y)=\frac{e^{-x_N^2/(4h)}}{\sqrt{\pi h}},\qquad h>0.\tag{2.10}\]

On the boundary \(y=y^*\), so the kernel is twice the Gaussian. Integration over the \(N-1\) tangential dimensions gives the displayed identity. Integrating the short-time part gives \(\int_0^\eta h^{-1/2}dh=2\sqrt\eta\), which is useful for continuity estimates with bounded boundary densities.

Formal derivation. The preceding Green’s identity calculation is a formal derivation under sufficient regularity, integrability and decay. Those assumptions cannot be imposed without justification near the singularity, so the paper adopts the following definition of integral solution.

2.6 Nonnegative integral solutions and minimality

For a rigorous Green identity calculation one can first use a bounded continuous Robin coefficient and smooth solutions with adequate spatial decay, integrate on a truncated domain and over \(s\in[0,t-\eta]\), then remove the spatial and temporal cutoffs using the stated integrability and approximate identity. In the singular problem, excising a neighborhood of the moving point would introduce additional boundary terms; their disappearance is not automatic. The paper instead constructs bounded approximations and passes to the limit in the integral equation. The definition below does not assume a classical solution at the singularity.

Definition 2.3 · Nonnegative integral solution (research paper Definition 1.1)

A real-valued function \(u\in C(D_\xi)\) is a nonnegative integral solution if it is nonnegative and satisfies the following conditions.

  1. \(u(x,0)=u_0(x)\) for \(x\in\overline{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{\xi(0)\}\).
  2. For every \((x,t)\in \mathcal{H}\times(0,T)\), (2.9) holds with finite right-hand side.

The solution \(u\) is minimal if \(u\le v\) on \(D_\xi\) for every nonnegative integral solution \(v\) with the same initial data.

Continuity is required on \(D_\xi\), the closed half-space cylinder with the singular graph removed. By contrast, the definition requires the integral equality only in the interior \(\mathcal{H}\times(0,T)\). Lemma 2.3 of the paper establishes a supersolution inequality at nonsingular boundary and terminal observations; the solution constructed in research paper [4], Theorem 1.1 satisfies equality there as well.

Example 2.2 · Minimality versus uniqueness

Minimality is an order property: if other nonnegative solutions exist, the minimal solution lies below all of them. It differs from uniqueness, which means that all solutions in the specified class coincide. The latter is established under separate weighted assumptions in this study.

Lemma 2.3 · Supersolution inequality at nonsingular observations (paper Lemma 2.3)

For compact continuous data or nonnegative continuous harmonic-weighted data off the initial pole, with \(0<\alpha<N-2\), any nonnegative integral solution \(v\) satisfies, at every \((x,t)\in D_\xi\) with \(t>0\),

\[v(x,t)\ge P_tu_0(x)+(A_\xi v)(x,t).\tag{2.9a}\]

No weight bound is required of the competing solution. Only the initial datum must belong to one of the two stated classes.

Proof · Extend the inequality by Fatou's lemma

Choose interior observations \((x_j,t_j)\to(x,t)\), with \(0<t_j<T\). The free terms converge by dominated convergence. For compact data this follows from boundedness and compact support. For weighted data, write \(p=\xi(0)\) and use \(u_0(y)\le C|y-p|^{-\alpha}\), by (9.1). On a compact neighborhood of the positive-time observation, all elapsed times are bounded below by \(t/2>0\). Both direct and reflected kernels admit the uniform majorant \(C_t\exp[-((|y-p|-R)_+)^2/(4T)]\) for a sufficiently large \(R\), since reflection preserves distance to the boundary point \(p\). Multiplication by \(|y-p|^{-\alpha}\) gives an integrable volume function: \(\alpha<N\) at the pole and the Gaussian controls infinity. Hence the free term converges also for this noncompact, possibly unbounded datum. The competitor's boundary values need not satisfy any weighted estimate. Extend each nonnegative boundary integrand by zero for \(s\ge t_j\). For almost every boundary space-time point with \(s<t\) and \(y\ne\xi(s)\), the kernel converges to \(G(x,y,t-s)\). Fatou's lemma therefore bounds the limit inferior of the integrals below by \(A_\xi v(x,t)\). Continuity of \(v\) on \(D_\xi\) gives (2.9a). In particular its right-hand side is finite.

□

2.7 Positive iteration and approximation

The scalar example above shows how repeatedly adding a nonnegative contribution can converge. For the actual boundary equation the iterates may instead grow without bound; the next propositions separate order and convergence from the independent question of finiteness.

Proposition 2.1 · Positive iteration and conditional minimality

Set \(F(x,t)=P_tu_0(x)\) and, on \(D_\xi\), let

\[u^{(0)}=F,\qquad u^{(k+1)}=F+A_\xi u^{(k)}.\tag{2.9b}\]

At \(t=0\) take all iterates equal to \(u_0\). The iterates are nonnegative measurable functions, allowed to take the value \(+\infty\) at positive times. They increase pointwise and are dominated by any nonnegative integral solution with the same compact datum.

Proof · Two inductions and the limit

Step 1 (monotonicity). \(u^{(1)}\ge u^{(0)}\) since \(A_\xi\) is positive. If \(u^{(k)}\ge u^{(k-1)}\), applying the order-preserving operator and adding \(F\) gives the next inequality.

\[0\le u^{(0)}\le u^{(1)}\le\cdots.\]

Step 2 (comparison). For any solution \(v\), Lemma 2.3 gives \(v\ge F\), including boundary and terminal observations. If \(u^{(k)}\le v\), then \(u^{(k+1)}\le F+A_\xi v\le v\). This is why one needs the supersolution inequality on the boundary, not just interior equality.

Step 3 (limit). Define \(S=\sup_k u^{(k)}\). Monotone convergence gives \(S=F+A_\xi S\) in the extended nonnegative sense, and the comparison gives \(S\le v\) for every such solution. If \(S\) is finite and continuous on \(D_\xi\) with the prescribed initial trace, it is an integral solution and therefore minimal.

□

Why we need this. The scalar Volterra example illustrates increasing iterates in a case where the limit is explicit. In the singular PDE the limit need not be finite; this conditional proposition is not an existence proof. Chapter 7 establishes the necessary estimates through bounded approximations, and Chapter 8 uses comparison with divergent approximations to exclude finite solutions.

Proposition 2.2 · Unified positive-series principle (supplementary)

Let \(V\ge0\) be Borel and finite almost everywhere on the boundary space-time cylinder, with arbitrary values on null sets. Let \(f\ge0\) be Borel in the volume and suppose \(F=P_\cdot f\) is finite and continuous at all positive-time observations of the closed half-space. Define the series at all positive-time points of the closed half-space, allowing infinity, and use the prescribed initial value at time zero:

\[\begin{aligned}(A_V w)(x,t)&=\int_0^t\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)V(y,s)w(y,s)\,d\sigma ds,\\ S(V,f)&=\sum_{k=0}^\infty A_V^k F.\end{aligned}\tag{2.11}\]

Products of nonnegative extended quantities use the convention \(0\cdot\infty=0\). The following statements hold.

  1. The series is measurable, is a fixed point in the extended nonnegative sense, and is increasing in both \(V\) (almost everywhere) and \(f\) (almost everywhere in volume, for positive time).
  2. Every nonnegative supersolution of the full kernel inequality dominates the series. In the compact or harmonic-weighted data classes of Lemma 2.3, if the series is finite and continuous on \(D_\xi\) with the prescribed initial value, it is the minimal integral solution.
  3. If \(V_j\uparrow V\) almost everywhere, then \(S(V_j,f)\uparrow S(V,f)\) at every positive-time observation. If merely \(V_j\to V\) almost everywhere and \((x_j,t_j)\to(x,t)\) with \(t>0\), then \(\liminf_j S(V_j,f)(x_j,t_j)\ge S(V,f)(x,t)\).
  4. With the additional domination \(V_j\le V\) almost everywhere and a finite continuous limit on \(D_\xi\), convergence is locally uniform there, provided the common free evolution recovers the initial datum continuously off the initial pole. In contrast, divergence at a positive-time observation of the limiting series forces divergence of the approximations without upper domination.
Proof · Measurability, Tonelli, monotone convergence and sequential Fatou

Extend kernels by zero for \(s\ge t\). Parameter integration of nonnegative Borel functions is measurable by Appendix B.9. Each finite partial sum is the corresponding iterate from Proposition 2.1. Positivity and monotone convergence in the sum show \(S=F+A_VS\), even if the value is infinite. If a supersolution dominates \(F\), induction shows that it dominates every finite sum. Lemma 2.3 supplies this inequality at nonsingular boundary and terminal observations for integral competitors; assigning zero on the null graph does not alter an integral. The finite continuous series with initial trace therefore solves the equation and is minimal.

For increasing coefficients, induction and monotone convergence in each integral prove convergence term by term, followed by monotone convergence for counting measure. For general convergence write \(F_{j,k}=A_{V_j}^k F\) and \(F_k=A_V^k F\). At positive times the base case follows from continuity of F. Assume the sequential lower bound holds for term \(k\), in particular at each fixed source point. For almost every \((y,s)\) with \(0<s<t\), the moving observation kernel tends to the limiting kernel and \(V_j(y,s)\to V(y,s)\); the inductive inequality gives the lower bound on the product (including zero coefficients by nonnegativity). Fatou proves the inequality for term \(k+1\). This induction applies also on the boundary, where the kernel is defined at positive elapsed times. Applying Fatou to counting measure proves the assertion for the full series. For nontrivial null exceptional sets of coefficients the same set can be removed at every integration, by Tonelli.

If dominated, termwise order gives \(S_j\le S\). For a convergent sequence of observations with positive limiting time, the lower bound and continuity give convergence to \(S(x,t)\). At a nonsingular time-zero observation, use \(F\le S_j\le S\) and continuity of the initial trace. At exactly zero time all values are the common datum. A failure of uniform convergence on a compact subset would give a sequence of observations with discrepancy bounded below by a positive constant; a convergent subsequence contradicts the sequential conclusion. The divergent case follows immediately from the extended lower bound. Neither finiteness nor continuity of an arbitrary positive series is asserted. □

This proposition unifies the mechanisms used in Chapters 7–10, 12 and 13. See dominated approximation, local uniform convergence and divergence and trajectory lower semicontinuity. The latter applies the assertion at fixed observation sequences because uniform convergence of the trajectories gives coefficient convergence off a countable union of null graphs.

2.8 Why the dimension matters

Optional proof · Why dimension two is obstructed (full treatment in Chapter 12)

Suppose a finite nonnegative integral solution exists with nonzero nonnegative compact continuous initial data. Positivity of the Neumann kernel gives \(F(y,s)=P_su_0(y)>0\) for \(s>0\), and continuity extends \(u\ge F\) to the nonsingular boundary. Fix an interior observation \((x,t)\) and \(0<a<b<t\). On the compact moving boundary neighborhood \(a\le s\le b,\ |y-\xi(s)|\le r\), the continuous product \(G(x,y,t-s)F(y,s)\) has a strictly positive minimum \(c\).

\[ A_\xi u(x,t)\ge c\lambda\int_a^b\int_{0<|y-\xi(s)|<r}\frac{d\sigma(y)\,ds}{|y-\xi(s)|}=+\infty\quad(N=2).\]

Tonelli's theorem justifies this lower bound. It contradicts the finite integral equation. This is the argument of research paper [4], Proposition 6.1, not a claim about every possible generalized solution class.

In polar coordinates, the local boundary integral of the inverse-distance coefficient is, up to a constant factor, \(\int_0^r\rho^{N-3}\,d\rho\). For \(N=2\) this diverges, leading to nonexistence of finite integral solutions for nonzero, nonnegative, compactly supported continuous data (paper Proposition 6.1; see Chapter 12). However, local integrability of the coefficient for \(N\ge3\) does not by itself guarantee existence.

Chapter 12 develops the full obstruction and quantitative divergence. Local integrability alone does not decide solvability in higher dimensions.

Chapter 2 · Takeaways

The Neumann kernel encodes reflection, preserves mass and contracts the \(L^2\) norm. The integral formulation adds the history of boundary interaction. Positivity gives a comparison principle for iterates; existence additionally requires finiteness and continuity away from the graph.
