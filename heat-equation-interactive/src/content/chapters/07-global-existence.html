Part III

7. Proof of global existence

How can bounded approximations yield a finite continuous solution at critical coupling?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Sharp Kato inequality; Appendix B.5–B.8 for weak evolution, Sobolev gain and nonlinear testing; A.5 for compactness.

Bounded problems → uniform \(L^2\) bound → local \(L^\infty\) estimate → continuity off the graph → monotone limit and initial trace → minimality.

We now prove Theorem 3.1 (Theorem 1.1 of the research paper). Fix \(N\ge3\), \(T>0\), \(\xi\in C([0,T];\partial\mathcal H)\), \(0<\lambda\le\lambda_N\), and \(f:=u_0\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\), \(f\ge0\), \(f\not\equiv0\). Here \(f\) is the original initial function, not a boundary profile. All estimates indexed by \(n\) below use the same datum.

At \(\lambda=\lambda_N\), the regularized energy estimate still gives \(\|u_n(t)\|_2\le\|f\|_2\), but its unweighted gradient coefficient is zero. The local bounds on compact subsets of \(D_\xi\) are needed to control nonsingular observations.

Proof roadmap
1 · ConstructBounded positive Volterra series2 · IdentifyWeak evolution and its energy3 · ControlUniform \(L^2\); global gradients only below threshold4 · LocalizeRobin Moser estimate off the graph5 · Recover continuityCutoffs and heat kernel estimates6 · Take the limitFinite identity, initial trace and minimality

The final three steps are needed even when a global \(L^2\) limit is available. No step differentiates the path.

7.1 Bounded Robin approximations and positive iteration

Let \(b\) be a bounded, continuous, nonnegative function on \(\partial\mathcal H\times[0,T]\). Use the real Banach space \(\mathscr B_T=C_b(\overline{\mathcal H}\times[0,T])\), with norm \(\|v\|_{\mathscr B_T}=\sup_{(x,t)}|v(x,t)|\). Completeness follows because a uniformly Cauchy sequence converges uniformly to a bounded continuous function. Define the time-causal operator on this space

\[(A_bv)(x,t)=\int_0^t\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)b(y,s)v(y,s)\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\tag{7.1}\]

At time zero define \(A_bv=0\). All operator norms in (7.3) refer to \(\mathscr B_T\); the time-slice norm in (7.2) is the spatial supremum over \(\overline{\mathcal H}\). From Lemma 2.2, with \(M_b=\|b\|_\infty\),

\[\|A_bv(t)\|_\infty\le \frac{M_b}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^t(t-s)^{-1/2}\|v(s)\|_\infty\,ds.\tag{7.2}\]
Lemma 7.1 · Volterra series

In \(\mathscr B_T\), the unique solution of \(u_b=F+A_bu_b\), \(F(x,t)=P_tf(x)\), is

\[u_b=\sum_{k=0}^\infty A_b^kF,\qquad \|A_b^k\|\le\frac{M_b^kT^{k/2}}{\Gamma(1+k/2)}.\tag{7.3}\]

It is nonnegative, and increases when either the nonnegative datum or the coefficient increases.

Proof

1. Iteration of the time estimate. The stronger bound at time \(t\) replaces \(T\) by \(t\) in (7.3). Suppose it holds for \(k\). Applying (7.2) and substituting \(s=t\theta\) yields the integral \(t^{(k+1)/2}B(1/2,1+k/2)\). The beta identity in Appendix C.4 cancels \(\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(1+k/2)\), giving the next bound. The case \(k=0\) is the identity operator.

2. Convergence and uniqueness. If \(a_k=M_b^kT^{k/2}/\Gamma(1+k/2)\), then \(a_{k+2}/a_k=M_b^2T/(1+k/2)\to0\) when \(M_b>0\); for \(M_b=0\) all terms after the first vanish. Both parity subseries converge. Thus the sum converges uniformly, and applying the bounded operator gives the equation. For a bounded difference \(d=A_bd\), iterating gives \(\|d\|_\infty\le a_k\|d\|_\infty\), hence \(d=0\).

3. Continuity and order. The portion of (7.1) with \(t-s<\delta\) is bounded by \(2M_b\|v\|_\infty\sqrt{\delta/\pi}\). On the complementary time range, for observations in a compact set, Gaussian decay in the boundary variable supplies an integrable majorant; moving the upper integration endpoint does not obstruct dominated convergence. The same short-time estimate handles zero time. This proves that \(A_b\) maps \(\mathscr B_T\) into itself; (7.2) also gives \(\|A_b\|\le2M_b\sqrt{T/\pi}\). The free term belongs to the same space by continuity of the even extension of the compact datum and the approximate identity. Every iterate and the uniform sum therefore belong to \(\mathscr B_T\). Every term is nonnegative for nonnegative \(f\); comparing term by term proves the order property. □

Why the Gamma factor matters. A contraction of \(A_b\) itself is not required. Causality produces a factorial-like denominator in its high powers, so bounded problems can be solved on any finite interval.

Now regularize the actual singular coefficient:

\[b_n(y,t)=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{|y-\xi(t)|^2+n^{-2}}},\qquad u_n=F+A_{b_n}u_n,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots .\tag{7.4}\]

Each coefficient is continuous and bounded by \(\lambda n\); for \(y\ne\xi(t)\) it increases to \(V(y,t)=\lambda/|y-\xi(t)|\). Lemma 7.1 therefore gives \(0\le u_n\le u_{n+1}\) on the closed cylinder. This is pointwise order, not yet an existence proof for the singular equation.

Uniform convergence away from the pole

Write \(r=|y-\xi(t)|\). For \(r\ge d_0>0\), rationalizing the difference gives

\[0\le V-b_n=\frac{\lambda n^{-2}}{r\sqrt{r^2+n^{-2}}\,(r+\sqrt{r^2+n^{-2}})}\le\frac{\lambda}{2n^2d_0^3}.\]

This bound is uniform in both space and time on separated sets. It does not assert uniform convergence on the whole punctured boundary.

7.2 Weak evolution and the energy identity

The integral construction alone does not justify testing the equation with its solution. We identify it with a weak energy evolution. Use \(\mathscr X=H^1(\mathcal H)\) and the Hilbert triple \(\mathscr X\hookrightarrow L^2(\mathcal H)\hookrightarrow\mathscr X^*\). These are dense continuous embeddings; the second sends an \(L^2\) function to its pairing with \(\mathscr X\). See Appendix B.5 and B.6.

\[a_t(v,w)=\int_{\mathcal H}\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\,dx-\int_{\partial\mathcal H}b(y,t)\operatorname{Tr}v\operatorname{Tr}w\,d\sigma.\tag{7.5}\]

The negative sign is the outward-normal convention for positive Robin amplification. By the elementary trace inequality, for \(\delta>0\),

\[a_t(w,w)\ge(1-M_b\delta)\|\nabla w\|_2^2-M_b\delta^{-1}\|w\|_2^2.\]

Choose \(\delta=1/[2(1+M_b)]\). Adding \(c_0\|w\|_2^2\), with \(c_0=M_b\delta^{-1}+1/2\), gives at least \(\tfrac12\|w\|_{H^1}^2\). The ordinary trace bound and Cauchy–Schwarz also bound \(|a_t(v,w)|\) by \(C\|v\|_{H^1}\|w\|_{H^1}\); measurability follows from that of \(b\). Thus the hypotheses of the nonautonomous form theorem are met. It gives a unique weak evolution \(v\) with

\[\begin{gathered}v\in L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\cap C([0,T];L^2(\mathcal H)),\qquad v_t\in L^2(0,T;\mathscr X^*),\\ v(0)=f,\qquad \langle v_t,w\rangle_{\mathscr X^*,\mathscr X}+a_t(v,w)=0\quad(w\in\mathscr X)\quad\text{a.e. }t.\end{gathered}\tag{7.6}\]
Two constructions, one solution
Positive seriesContinuous bounded values and comparisonWeak evolutionSobolev regularity and legal energy testingVolume identityBoundary flux → bulk via a time-integrable normTrace identityRecover boundary values in space-time \(L^2\)

The weak solution's volume representative alone does not identify its flux. We first recover the boundary Volterra equation, then compare its convergent series with the continuous construction.

Two kernel operators
\[\begin{aligned}S_h &:L^2(\partial\mathcal H)\longrightarrow L^2(\mathcal H),&\quad\|S_h\|&\le Ch^{-1/4},\\ R_h &:L^2(\partial\mathcal H)\longrightarrow L^2(\partial\mathcal H),&\quad\|R_h\|&\le(\pi h)^{-1/2}.\end{aligned}\]

Here \(R_h\psi=(S_h\psi)(\cdot,0)\) is defined by the explicit kernel formula (7.7), not by assuming Sobolev regularity. The first estimate allows Cauchy–Schwarz in time at a fixed observation time; the second uses an integrable time kernel in the boundary space-time norm. The proof below identifies the latter with the Sobolev trace of the weak evolution.

Proof of the identification: weak evolution = bounded integral solution

Step 1 · Boundary-to-volume estimates

Write \(S_h\psi(x)=\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,h)\psi(y)\,d\sigma(y)\). For boundary sources, the reflection formula factors as

\[S_h\psi(x',z)=\frac{e^{-z^2/(4h)}}{\sqrt{\pi h}}\,(g_h^{(N-1)}*\psi)(x'),\qquad z\ge0.\tag{7.7}\]

The superscript denotes dimension of the Gaussian. Tangential convolution is an \(L^2\) contraction. Taking the normal squared integral gives \(\|S_h\|_{L^2(\partial\mathcal H)\to L^2(\mathcal H)}\le C h^{-1/4}\), since \(\int_0^\infty e^{-z^2/(2h)}dz=C\sqrt h\). At height zero, the explicit operator \(R_h\) is tangential convolution multiplied by \((\pi h)^{-1/2}\); its operator norm is at most \((\pi h)^{-1/2}\). By symmetry and duality, \(\|\operatorname{Tr}P_hf\|_{L^2(\partial\mathcal H)}\le Ch^{-1/4}\|f\|_2\). This free trace is square integrable in time.

Step 2 · Backward testing

For any \(g\in C_c^\infty(\mathcal H)\) and fixed \(t>0\), use \(P_{t-s}g\) as a time-dependent test in (7.6). The even extension of \(g\) is smooth, so this test satisfies the homogeneous Neumann heat equation backward in time and has the requisite energy regularity through \(s=t\). The dual pairing rule cancels the two bulk gradient terms. Integration from zero to \(t\) gives

\[(v(t),g)_2=(P_tf,g)_2+\int_0^t\big(b(\cdot,s)\operatorname{Tr}v(s),\operatorname{Tr}P_{t-s}g\big)_{L^2(\partial\mathcal H)}ds.\]

Because \(\psi=b\operatorname{Tr}v\in L^2(0,T;L^2(\partial\mathcal H))\) and \(h^{-1/4}\in L^2(0,T)\), the integral \(\int_0^t S_{t-s}\psi(s)ds\) exists in \(L^2(\mathcal H)\) for every \(t\). Density of interior tests identifies

\[v(t)=P_tf+\int_0^t S_{t-s}\psi(s)ds\quad\text{in }L^2(\mathcal H).\tag{7.8}\]

Step 3 · Recover the trace, not just the volume representative

Set \(Z=L^2(0,T;L^2(\partial\mathcal H))\). At height \(z\), (7.7) introduces a factor \(e^{-z^2/(4h)}\) in the time convolution. As \(z\downarrow0\), the difference from the boundary operator has time-kernel majorant \((\pi h)^{-1/2}|1-e^{-z^2/(4h)}|\), whose \(L^1(0,T)\) norm tends to zero by dominated convergence. Young's convolution inequality in time gives convergence in \(Z\). The free term converges in the same space: normal Gaussian integration gives a height-uniform bound \(Ch^{-1/4}\|f\|_2\), and at each positive time its slices converge in tangential \(L^2\) by Gaussian continuity. For almost every height (7.8) agrees with the Sobolev slice by Fubini. Moreover \(\|v(\cdot,z,\cdot)-\operatorname{Tr}v\|_Z\le\sqrt z\|\partial_Nv\|_{L^2(\mathcal H\times(0,T))}\) along such slices, by the fundamental theorem on normal lines. Therefore, writing \(\tau=\operatorname{Tr}v\), the equality in \(Z\) is

\[\tau(t)= (P_tf)(\cdot,0)+\int_0^t R_{t-s}[b(\cdot,s)\tau(s)]\,ds.\]

The equality holds for almost every time as an equality of boundary \(L^2\) functions. The boundary value of the free term is given by its Gaussian kernel at positive times.

Step 4 · Identify both constructions

Let the boundary operator on \(Z\) be the right-hand integral of (7.1) restricted to \(x\in\partial\mathcal H\). Its \(k\)-fold norm is bounded by the same Gamma expression as (7.3): iterating the positive time majorant gives a convolution kernel \(M_b^kh^{k/2-1}/\Gamma(k/2)\) for \(k\ge1\), whose \(L^1(0,T)\) norm is the bound. Hence the trace equation has a unique solution in \(Z\), obtained by its series with free trace \(\operatorname{Tr}P_\cdot f\). The same partial sums converge uniformly to the continuous boundary values of \(u_b\). On each compact boundary-time patch both limits agree almost everywhere (convergence in measure is unique). Substituting the identified flux into (7.8) shows that \(u_b(t)=v(t)\) as volume functions for every time, and that their traces agree almost everywhere in boundary space-time. Thus the bounded continuous representative inherits the weak energy regularity. □

The Hilbert-triple chain rule from Appendix B.6 justifies testing with \(v(t)\): the derivative of \(\tfrac12\|v(t)\|_2^2\) is \(\langle v_t,v\rangle\) almost everywhere. Integrating and using the identification gives, for every \(0\le t\le T\),

\[\frac12\|u_b(t)\|_2^2+\int_0^t\|\nabla u_b(s)\|_2^2ds=\frac12\|f\|_2^2+\int_0^t\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H} b\,|\operatorname{Tr}u_b|^2d\sigma ds.\tag{7.9}\]

7.3 Uniform energy control at and below criticality

Apply (7.9) with \(b=b_n\). At each time the translated sharp Kato inequality controls the singular weight, and \(b_n\le V\) almost everywhere on the boundary. This yields the estimate

Proposition 7.2 · Uniform approximation estimate
\[\boxed{\|u_n(t)\|_2^2+2\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_N}\right)\int_0^t\|\nabla u_n(s)\|_2^2ds\le\|f\|_2^2.}\tag{7.10}\]

It holds for every \(n\) and every \(t\in[0,T]\); no derivative of the moving center enters the inequality.

Subcritical. The positive coefficient of the gradient gives a uniform bound in \(L^2(0,T;H^1(\mathcal H))\). Critical. That coefficient is zero, so estimate (7.10) supplies a uniform volume norm; it does not imply that the limit belongs to the global energy space.

Retained control at criticality

At \(\lambda=\lambda_N\), the exact bounded energy identity and \(b_n\le\lambda_N/|y-\xi(s)|\) give

\[\boxed{\|u_n(t)\|_2^2+2\int_0^t Q_{\lambda_N,\xi(s)}[u_n(s)]\,ds\le\|f\|_2^2.}\]

Indeed the regularized form equals the singular critical form plus \(\int_{\partial\mathcal H}(V-b_n)|\operatorname{Tr}u_n|^2\,d\sigma\), a nonnegative correction. By Chapter 6 the critical form is itself nonnegative. This controls its time integral uniformly in \(n\), but does not control the two large terms \(\mathcal E[u_n]\) and \(\lambda_N B_{\xi(s)}[u_n]\) separately. We do not claim that the limiting solution is in global \(H^1\) or pass this form to the limit without further analysis.

Set \(u=\sup_nu_n\) initially with extended values. At each fixed \(t\), monotone convergence applied to \(u_n^2\) shows that \(\|u(t)\|_2\le\|f\|_2\) and \(u(t)\) is finite almost everywhere in the volume. Because \(0\le u-u_n\le u\) almost everywhere, dominated convergence gives strong \(L^2\) convergence at each time, and, by the common norm bound, also in \(L^2(0,T;L^2(\mathcal H))\). For strict coupling, a weakly convergent subsequence in the global energy space has this same limit. Restricting weak convergence to \((0,t)\) and applying lower semicontinuity proves the gradient estimate for the limit at every \(t\).

Example 7.1 · Why the endpoint is genuinely different

Let \(0<\rho<1/4\) and choose a radial cutoff \(\chi_\rho\) equal to one on \(2\rho\le|x|\le1\), zero for \(|x|\le\rho\) or \(|x|\ge2\), with \(|\nabla\chi_\rho|\le C/|x|\). Put \(w_\rho=\Phi_*\chi_\rho\). Homogeneity gives \(\|w_\rho\|_2^2\le C\int_0^2r\,dr\), while \(B_0[w_\rho]\ge |\mathbb S^{N-2}|\log(1/(2\rho))\). The critical ground-state identity bounds \(Q_{\lambda_N,0}[w_\rho]\) by a constant because each cutoff transition has a fixed logarithmic width. Therefore \(\mathcal E[w_\rho]=Q_{\lambda_N,0}[w_\rho]+\lambda_N B_0[w_\rho]\to\infty\). Even bounded critical form energy and volume norm cannot control the full gradient. These test functions are not actual heat solutions.

Why we need a local argument. The preceding convergence concerns volume equivalence classes. It does not recover a boundary value, pointwise finiteness or continuity. The next two sections supply precisely those missing properties.

7.4 Local boundedness by boundary Moser iteration

A uniform \(L^2\) estimate controls an integral, not the maximum at any individual point. Even smooth functions can form high, narrow peaks with bounded \(L^2\) norm. We therefore need a local estimate in a cylinder separated from the pole.

We work on a backward parabolic cylinder \(Q_R=(B_R(x_0)\cap\mathcal H)\times(t_0-R^2,t_0]\), with \(x_0\in\overline{\mathcal H}\). A cylinder can meet the flat boundary; its test functions need not vanish there. The key is that on a neighborhood separated from the moving pole, the coefficients \(b_n\) admit a bound independent of \(n\).

Lemma 7.3 · Local boundedness (paper Lemma 3.1)

Suppose \(w\ge0\) is bounded and belongs locally to \(L^2\) in time with spatial \(H^1\) regularity up to the flat boundary, on smaller spatial balls and compact time intervals strictly inside \((t_0-R^2,t_0)\). Assume for all nonnegative smooth tests \(\varphi\) supported inside the ball and the open time interval (restricted to the closed half-space),

\[\iint_{Q_R}(-w\varphi_t+\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi)\,dx\,dt\le B\int_{t_0-R^2}^{t_0}\!\int_{B_R(x_0)\cap\partial\mathcal H}w\varphi\,d\sigma\,dt,\qquad B\ge0.\tag{7.11}\]

For \(0<r<R\),

\[\|w\|_{L^\infty(Q_r)}\le C(N,B,R,r)\|w\|_{L^2(Q_R)}.\tag{7.12}\]

The constant does not depend on the individual bound of \(w\).

Step 1 · Power testing

Take \(p\ge2\), and a cutoff \(\theta\) equal to one on \(Q_r\), supported spatially strictly inside \(B_R(x_0)\), and zero close to the initial cylinder time. Choose \(|\nabla\theta|\le C/(R-r)\) and \(|\theta_t|\le C/(R-r)^2\); such cutoffs follow from the spatial gap and \(R^2-r^2\ge(R-r)^2\). Write \(v=w^{p/2}\) and use \(\theta^2w^{p-1}\) in (7.11).

Justification of the nonlinear test

See Appendix B.8 for the time-averaging and approximation lemmas. Average the subsolution inequality over a short forward time interval of length \(\ell\), using only times strictly inside the cylinder. The average \(w_\ell\) is nonnegative and bounded by the same individual bound as \(w\), has a local \(L^2\) time derivative, and satisfies the same inequality with boundary upper term \(B\operatorname{Tr}w_\ell\). Sobolev approximation and a cutoff at the upper time allow the test \(\theta^2w_\ell^{p-1}\). For \(F(s)=s^{p-1}\) the function and derivative are bounded on the actual range (for \(p\ge2\)); thus the spatial chain rule is valid. Time averages converge strongly in local \(L^2(H^1)\) and their traces converge in local space-time \(L^2\). Along a subsequence, the difference of gradients of the powers equals \(F'(w_\ell)\nabla(w_\ell-w)+(F'(w_\ell)-F'(w))\nabla w\); the first term tends to zero in \(L^2\) and the second by dominated convergence. The upper-time primitives converge for almost every upper time. Taking the essential supremum and exhausting the open interval justifies the integrated power estimate up to the cylinder top. The resulting constants are independent of the individual bound.

Before multiplication by \(p\), the time contribution is \(p^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\int\theta^2v^2-2p^{-1}\int\theta\theta_t v^2\). The gradient contribution is

\[\frac{4(p-1)}{p^2}\int\theta^2|\nabla v|^2+\frac4p\int\theta v\nabla v\cdot\nabla\theta.\]

Young's inequality absorbs the mixed term into half the principal term plus a cutoff cost. Multiplying by \(p\), using \(p\ge2\), and estimating \(|\nabla(\theta v)|^2\) by twice each square yields absolute constants \(c,C>0\) such that

\[\frac{d}{dt}\int(\theta v)^2+c\int|\nabla(\theta v)|^2\le C\int(|\nabla\theta|^2+|\theta\theta_t|)v^2+pB\int_{\partial\mathcal H}(\theta v)^2.\tag{7.13}\]

Step 2 · Absorb the Robin contribution

Apply (B.1) to \(\theta v\), extended by zero beyond its spatial support, with \(\delta=c/[2(1+pB)]\). Then \(pB\delta\le c/2\), while \(pB\delta^{-1}\le C(1+p^2B^2)\). Integrate from the lower cylinder time, where the cutoff vanishes, and take the essential supremum of the first term. We obtain

\[\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{s}\|\theta v(s)\|_2^2+\iint_{Q_R}|\nabla(\theta v)|^2\le C\big[1+(R-r)^{-2}+p^2B^2\big]\iint_{Q_R}w^p.\tag{7.14}\]

Step 3 · Gain an integrability exponent

Put \(\kappa=1+2/N\). For \(g=\theta v\), even reflection and the Euclidean Sobolev inequality (see B.7) imply \(\|g\|_{2N/(N-2)}\le C_N\|\nabla g\|_2\). Hölder interpolation at each time gives \(\|g\|_{2\kappa}^{2\kappa}\le\|g\|_2^{4/N}\|g\|_{2N/(N-2)}^2\). Integrating in time and using (7.14), then taking the \(p\kappa\)-th root, yields

\[\|w\|_{L^{p\kappa}(Q_r)}\le\big[C_N(1+(R-r)^{-2}+p^2B^2)\big]^{1/p}\|w\|_{L^p(Q_R)}.\tag{7.15}\]

Step 4 · Iterate to the essential supremum

Use \(p_j=2\kappa^j\), \(R_j=r+(R-r)2^{-j}\). Apply (7.15) from \(Q_{R_j}\) to \(Q_{R_{j+1}}\). Because \(R_j-R_{j+1}=(R-r)2^{-j-1}\), the logarithm of the product of all multiplicative costs is bounded by \(C(N,B,R,r)\sum_{j\ge0}(1+j)/p_j<\infty\). To see the mechanism, let \(q_\kappa=1/\kappa\). The exact summability factor is \(\sum_{j\ge0}(j+1)/p_j=1/[2(1-q_\kappa)^2]\). In a simple model with \(\kappa=2\), factors \(4^{(j+1)/p_j}\) have product \(4^2=16\), even though the unpowered costs diverge. In the actual iteration, \(p_j^2B^2\) and the squared inverse radius gap both grow at most geometrically, so taking logarithms gives a linear-in-\(j\) bound divided by \(p_j\). The exact numerical value of the PDE constant is not asserted. Every norm on \(Q_r\) is bounded by the same product times \(\|w\|_{L^2(Q_R)}\). Since the cylinder has finite measure, the limit of its \(L^{p_j}\) norms is its essential supremum. This proves (7.12). □

7.5 Applying the local estimate, including time zero

On a compact set \(K\subset D_\xi\), continuity of the trajectory implies

\[d_K=\min_{(x,t)\in K}|x-\xi(t)|>0.\]

Choose \(\rho>0\) so that \(2\rho<d_K/4\) and \(|\xi(s)-\xi(t)|<d_K/4\) for \(|s-t|\le4\rho^2\) in \([0,T]\). Uniform continuity on the compact time interval guarantees such a radius. The triangle inequality gives \(|y-\xi(s)|\ge d_K/2\) whenever \(|y-x|\le2\rho\) and the stated time constraint holds. Hence all \(b_n\) are bounded there by \(B=2\lambda/d_K\).

For a positive-time observation choose the backward cylinder of radius \(\rho\) centered at \(x\) with top time \(\min(t+\rho^2/8,T)\). Its cylinder of radius \(\rho/2\) contains the observation in the relative closed space-time region. If this cylinder reaches negative times, extend the subsolution as follows.

Initial-time extension
\[M_0=\|f\|_\infty,\qquad w_n(x,s)=\begin{cases}\max\{u_n(x,s),M_0\},&s\ge0,\\ M_0,&s<0.\end{cases}\tag{7.16}\]

In any such cylinder with positive-time boundary bound \(b_n\le B\), the extended function satisfies (7.11).

Proof of the extension

Approximate \(r\mapsto\max(r,M_0)\) by smooth convex functions \(F_\delta\) lying above \(r\) for \(r\ge0\), with \(0\le F_\delta'\le1\), derivative zero below \(M_0\), and uniform convergence on bounded intervals. For example, integrate a smooth increasing transition from zero to one over \([M_0,M_0+\delta]\), starting with constant \(M_0+\delta\). Use the time-averaged weak equation and the nonnegative test \(F_\delta'(u_{n,\ell})\varphi\). The same strong local convergence of averages and traces as in the nonlinear-test justification permits passage \(\ell\downarrow0\) for fixed \(n,\delta\); the averaged Robin flux converges strongly in local \(L^2\) since \(b_n\) is bounded. The convexity contribution \(-F_\delta''(u_n)|\nabla u_n|^2\le0\) can be discarded. On the plane the flux is at most \(B F_\delta(u_n)\), since \(F_\delta'(u_n) b_nu_n\le B F_\delta(u_n)\). Passing \(\delta\downarrow0\) using bounded convergence and the Sobolev chain rule gives the subsolution for \(\max(u_n,M_0)\). The latter tends to \(M_0\) locally in \(L^2\) as \(s\downarrow0\), because \(u_n(s)\to f\) strongly and \(f\le M_0\). Let \(z_n=\max(u_n,M_0)\) on positive times and let \(\varphi\ge0\) be supported inside the extended cylinder, vanishing near its time endpoints. Integrate the positive-time inequality first with a cutoff which rises from zero to one just after time zero. Taking that cutoff to the initial face using the local strong trace gives

\[\int_{s>0}(-z_n\varphi_s+\nabla z_n\cdot\nabla\varphi)\,dx\,ds\le B\int_{s>0,\partial\mathcal H}z_n\varphi\,d\sigma\,ds+\int_{\mathcal H}M_0\varphi(x,0)\,dx.\]

On the negative-time piece, the gradient is zero and \(\int_{s<0}-M_0\varphi_s\,dx\,ds=-\int M_0\varphi(x,0)dx\). Adding cancels both interface terms exactly. The negative-time Robin upper bound is also valid because its right-hand side is nonnegative. The extended function therefore obeys the same subsolution inequality throughout the cylinder. This is a proof device, not an extension of the original physical evolution. □

The uniform volume estimate (7.10) controls the space-time \(L^2\) norm on the positive-time part of any chosen cylinder; truncation adds at most the constant contribution \(M_0^2\) times its finite measure, and the negative-time part is also bounded in that way. Lemma 7.3 gives a pointwise bound on the smaller cylinder after using continuity of each \(u_n\) to pass from essential to ordinary values, including its boundary and terminal face. The constants can be chosen using \(d_K,\rho,N,T,f\), not \(n\). Thus

\[\sup_n\sup_{(x,t)\in K}u_n(x,t)<\infty\qquad(K\subset D_\xi\text{ compact}).\tag{7.17}\]

The limit is now finite at every nonsingular observation, not merely almost everywhere. Condition (M) is unnecessary: the only motion estimate used was uniform continuity.

7.6 Continuity off the graph and local uniform convergence

A locally bounded monotone limit need not be continuous. Uniform local continuity supplies the extra compactness needed to identify the integral solution, including boundary observations.

Local boundedness does not imply continuity of a pointwise increasing limit. We prove equicontinuity of \(u_n\) by localized kernel representations, rather than differentiating the moving coefficient.

Step 1 · Localize in space

Near a fixed nonsingular observation, take nested spatial neighborhoods and a time interval \([a,b]\) with closures away from the graph and bounds from (7.17). Choose \(a=0\) for an observation at zero, and \(a<t\) for positive-time observations. A smooth spatial cutoff \(\chi\) is one around the smaller neighborhood and supported in the larger, with \(\partial_\nu\chi=0\). For a boundary observation take radial cutoffs centered on the plane; for an interior one use balls lying strictly inside the half-space. Set \(v_n=\chi u_n\). In the weak equation, the product rule gives

\[(\partial_t-\Delta)v_n=-2\operatorname{div}(u_n\nabla\chi)+u_n\Delta\chi,\qquad\partial_\nu v_n=\chi b_nu_n.\tag{7.18}\]

The volume divergence source has zero normal component on the plane. Moving its derivative onto the kernel avoids needing uniform bounds on \(\nabla u_n\) for this continuity argument.

Step 2 · Write the representation

\[\begin{aligned}v_n(x,t)={}&P_{t-a}v_n(a)(x)\\ &+\int_a^t\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)\chi(y)b_n(y,s)u_n(y,s)\,d\sigma ds\\ &+\int_a^t\!\int_{\mathcal H}\big[2\nabla_yG(x,y,t-s)\cdot\nabla\chi(y)\\ &\hspace{95pt}+G(x,y,t-s)\Delta\chi(y)\big]u_n(y,s)\,dy ds.\end{aligned}\tag{7.19}\]

Test first against smooth compactly supported interior functions by the backward Neumann method of §7.2. The derivative term is integrated by parts against the kernel with no boundary term. This identifies (7.19) in duality. On the smaller neighborhood, all sources have uniformly bounded densities on fixed compact spatial supports, and the derivative sources are spatially separated from observations. The estimates below yield a continuous representative of this formula; its volume equality with \(v_n\) then extends to the plane by continuity.

Step 3 · Split elapsed time

Let \(h=t-s\). For recent times \(0<h<\delta\), the boundary term is bounded in absolute value by \(C\sqrt\delta\), using (2.10). For the volume term differentiate the Gaussian directly:

\[|\nabla g_h(z)|=\frac{|z|}{2h}g_h(z)\le C_Nh^{-(N+1)/2}e^{-|z|^2/(8h)}.\]

Positive distance from the cutoff derivative supports, also for their reflected images, gives the uniform bound

\[C\int_0^\delta\left(h^{-N/2}+h^{-(N+1)/2}\right)e^{-c/h}\,dh\longrightarrow0\qquad(\delta\downarrow0),\tag{7.20}\]

where \(c>0\) depends on spatial separation. The substitution \(z=c/h\) converts each integral to an exponentially decaying tail. No uncontrolled gradient appears. For elapsed times \(h\ge\delta\), the kernels and their derivatives are uniformly continuous in observations on the fixed compact ranges and uniformly bounded. In comparing observations, the change of time indicator affects an interval of length at most \(|t-t'|\) and contributes at most \(C_\delta|t-t'|\). Thus fixing \(\delta\) first and then making observation differences small controls the separated-time terms. Finally take \(\delta\) small to make the recent portions uniformly negligible.

Step 4 · Free term and the limit

For \(t\ge a+\epsilon_0\) the free terms are equicontinuous by the kernel's smoothness and the uniform compactly supported bound on \(v_n(a)\). If \(a=0\), the free term is independent of \(n\): \(P_t(\chi f)\) is continuous up to time zero by the approximate identity (even reflection). This proves equicontinuity locally at all nonsingular observations, including \(t=0,T\). The finite pointwise limit is continuous: the common local modulus passes to the limit. On any compact \(K\subset D_\xi\), Dini's theorem gives uniform convergence: for any tolerance, the relatively open sets on which the continuous error is smaller than that tolerance form an increasing cover of the compact set, hence a finite subcover and then a single index suffice. □

7.7 Strong initial trace

Two different traces. Continuity on the nonsingular closed cylinder recovers the pointwise datum including nonsingular boundary points; strong convergence in volume \(L^2\) is a separate statement and does not assign values on the plane.

At \(t=0\), every bounded approximation equals \(f\), so local uniform convergence gives the correct pointwise initial values off \(\xi(0)\). For the global norm we need a different argument: \(u\ge P_tf\ge0\) by the positive series, and \(\|u(t)\|_2\le\|f\|_2\). Hence

\[\begin{aligned}\|u(t)-P_tf\|_2^2&=\|u(t)\|_2^2+\|P_tf\|_2^2-2(u(t),P_tf)_2\\&\le\|f\|_2^2-\|P_tf\|_2^2\longrightarrow0.\end{aligned}\tag{7.21}\]

The inequality uses \((u(t),P_tf)_2\ge\|P_tf\|_2^2\). The last limit follows because even extension to the full space and Gaussian approximation give strong continuity \(P_tf\to f\) in \(L^2\) (first for smooth compact data, then by density and contraction). Adding \(\|P_tf-f\|_2\) proves \(\lim_{t\downarrow0}\|u(t)-f\|_2=0\).

Example 7.2 · Why an \(L^2\) limit is not a boundary limit

Choose a smooth compactly supported function \(\psi\) on the closed half-space with \(\psi(0)=1\). Then \(\psi_j(x)=\psi(jx)\) has \(\|\psi_j\|_2=j^{-N/2}\|\psi\|_2\to0\), but \(\psi_j(0)=1\) at every index. A volume norm cannot determine behavior on a set of zero volume measure. This is why §7.6 is indispensable even after the norm convergence in §7.3.

7.8 Passing to the limit and proving minimality

Step 1 · Finite integral identity

For fixed \((x,t)\in D_\xi\), \(t>0\), define boundary integrands arbitrarily as zero on the singular graph for integration. The graph is closed relative to the cylinder by continuity of the trajectory, hence measurable. It has boundary space-time measure zero by Tonelli's theorem: at each time the exceptional boundary set is a singleton. Away from it, \(b_nu_n\uparrow Vu\), so monotone convergence applies directly to the nonnegative integrands in (7.4). Since the left-hand sides converge to a finite value,

\[u(x,t)=P_tf(x)+\lambda\int_0^t\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-s)\frac{u(y,s)}{|y-\xi(s)|}\,d\sigma ds<\infty.\tag{7.22}\]

In particular equality holds also at nonsingular boundary points and at terminal time, stronger than the interior equality in Definition 2.3. The limiting boundary density is locally integrable even across the graph for times strictly below \(T\): on a compact boundary-time source set with times at most \(b<T\), evaluate (7.22) at an interior point and a later time \(t>b\). For a compact source set \(K\) of times at most \(b<t\), put \(g_*=\min_{(y,s)\in K}G(x,y,t-s)>0\). Then \(\int_K Vu\,d\sigma ds\le g_*^{-1}(u(x,t)-P_tf(x))<\infty\).

Step 2 · Recover the local weak equation

For compact cylinders off the graph, the \(p=2\) version of (7.14) bounds the gradients on smaller cylinders independently of \(n\). With the local uniform convergence, any weak limit of these gradients is the distributional gradient of \(u\) (test against compact smooth functions and integrate by parts). Thus \(u\) has local energy regularity there. Weak continuity of the trace map and uniform convergence of the boundary values identify its trace with the continuous boundary values. Also \(b_n\to V\) uniformly on compact boundary patches separated from the pole, so \(b_nu_n\to Vu\) uniformly there. Passing to the limit in the weak equation gives the local Robin identity away from the graph, for tests with positive interior times. This does not assert a classical solution at the singularity.

Step 3 · Compare with every competitor

Let \(v\) be any finite nonnegative integral solution for the same datum. The boundary and terminal supersolution inequality (Lemma 2.3) and \(b_n\le V\) imply \(v\ge F+A_{b_n}v\) on nonsingular positive-time observations. By positivity, induction gives

\[v\ge\sum_{k=0}^j A_{b_n}^kF\quad\text{for every }j,n.\]

Taking \(j\to\infty\) gives \(v\ge u_n\) by the bounded series construction; then \(n\to\infty\) gives \(v\ge u\). At zero time the initial values agree. This proves minimality. Since \(f\ge0\) is continuous and nonzero, it is positive on a volume subset of positive measure, hence \(u\ge P_tf>0\) for all nonsingular positive-time observations.

Conclusion

The finite integral identity, continuity, initial values and minimality establish the first assertion of Theorem 3.1. The global norm estimate and strong initial trace give the second. Strict subcriticality gives the gradient estimate by the weak compactness argument of §7.3. At criticality no global gradient bound or unrestricted uniqueness has been proved. Since \(T\) is arbitrary, the result holds on every finite time interval of a continuous trajectory; minimal solutions for successive horizons agree by the same approximation construction. □

7.9 The singular energy solution and critical weighted energy

Theorem 7.4 · Singular weak/integral identification (supplementary)

Let \(N\ge3\), \(T>0\), \(0\le\lambda<\lambda_N\), \(\xi\in C([0,T];\partial\mathcal H)\), and \(0\le f\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\). Set \(\mathscr X=H^1(\mathcal H)\), \(Y=L^2(\mathcal H)\) and \(\mathcal W=L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\cap H^1(0,T;\mathscr X^*)\), using the pivot inclusion \(Y\hookrightarrow\mathscr X^*\). The minimal integral solution is the continuous off-graph representative of the unique weak energy solution in \(\mathcal W\), with initial value \(f\) in \(Y\). Its boundary values agree with the Sobolev trace almost everywhere. This does not assert that every integral competitor belongs to \(\mathcal W\).

\[\langle u_t,w\rangle_{\mathscr X^*,\mathscr X}+\int_{\mathcal H}\nabla u\cdot\nabla w\,dx-\int_{\partial\mathcal H}V\operatorname{Tr}u\operatorname{Tr}w\,d\sigma=0\tag{7.23}\]

The identity holds in \(\mathscr X^*\) for almost every time.

Proof · Uniform dual estimates and weak passage to the singular form

Put \(c=1-\lambda/\lambda_N>0\). For each \(t\) the singular bilinear form \(a_t(v,w)=\int\nabla v\cdot\nabla w-\int V\operatorname{Tr}v\operatorname{Tr}w\) is bounded on \(\mathscr X\) by translated Kato and weighted Cauchy–Schwarz. The same bound applies to \(a_{n,t}\) with the bounded coefficients of §7.1. Measurability on fixed pairs follows by truncating \(V\), since the absolute boundary integral is bounded by \((\lambda/\lambda_N)\|\nabla v\|_2\|\nabla w\|_2\). Furthermore \(a_t(w,w)+c\|w\|_2^2\ge c\|w\|_{\mathscr X}^2\). Appendix B.6 therefore supplies a unique weak evolution with datum \(f\).

The approximants \(u_n\) belong to \(\mathcal W\) by §7.2; §7.3 bounds them uniformly in \(L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\), and their equations give \(\|(u_n)_t\|_{L^2\mathscr X^*}\le(1+\lambda/\lambda_N)\|u_n\|_{L^2\mathscr X}\). They converge strongly in \(L^2(0,T;Y)\) by monotone and dominated convergence, so a weakly convergent subsequence in \(\mathcal W\) has limit \(u\). The continuous evaluation map \(\mathcal W\to Y\) at time zero is bounded by the Hilbert-triple estimate; hence \(u\) has trace \(f\).

For a test \(w\in L^2(0,T;\mathscr X)\), write the boundary difference as the fixed singular form acting on \(u_n-u\), plus the error with coefficient \(b_n-V\) acting on \(u_n,w\). The former tends to zero by weak convergence and the uniform form bound. The absolute value of the latter is at most

\[\left(\int_0^T\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}V|\operatorname{Tr}u_n|^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_0^T\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}(V-b_n)|\operatorname{Tr}w|^2\right)^{1/2}.\]

Translated Kato uniformly bounds the first factor by \((\lambda/\lambda_N)^{1/2}\|\nabla u_n\|_{L^2((0,T)\times\mathcal H)}\). In the second factor, the integrand \((V-b_n)|\operatorname{Tr}w|^2\) tends to zero almost everywhere and is bounded by \(V|\operatorname{Tr}w|^2\), whose integral is at most \((\lambda/\lambda_N)\|\nabla w\|_{L^2((0,T)\times\mathcal H)}^2\). Dominated convergence then gives boundary convergence. The weak derivative and bulk terms pass by weak convergence. This proves (7.23); uniqueness follows from the Hilbert-triple chain rule applied to the difference and nonnegativity of the form. At each fixed time the approximants converge strongly in \(Y\) as in §7.3; weak evaluation of the subsequence in \(\mathcal W\) identifies the continuous \(Y\) representative at every time, not merely almost everywhere in time. On compact nonsingular boundary patches, the continuous representative and the Sobolev trace agree almost everywhere by the slice estimate of §2.6 (for almost every time), using local continuity up to the plane. For \(\lambda=0\) the free Neumann solution gives the same conclusion. □

Theorem 7.5 · Limiting critical weighted-energy estimate (supplementary)

Under the compact-data hypotheses of Theorem 3.1 at \(\lambda=\lambda_N\), set \(\Psi_s(x)=\Phi_{\alpha_*}(x-\xi(s))\). Then for every \(t\in[0,T]\),

\[\boxed{\|u(t)\|_2^2+2\int_0^t\!\int_{\mathcal H}\Psi_s^2\left|\nabla_x\left(u(x,s)/\Psi_s(x)\right)\right|^2dx\,ds\le\|f\|_2^2.}\tag{7.24}\]

The gradient is the local weak spatial gradient in the open half-space, defined for almost every time. No time derivative of \(\Psi_s\) or of \(\xi\) is required. The integral is not defined as a difference of potentially infinite ordinary energies.

Proof · Ground-state inequality, local weak compactness and exhaustion

For fixed \(s\) and any \(w\in H^1(\mathcal H)\), the translated form of Proposition 6.3's approximation argument gives

\[\int_{\mathcal H}\Psi_s^2|\nabla(w/\Psi_s)|^2\,dx\le Q_{\lambda_N,\xi(s)}[w].\]

Indeed, approximate \(w\) by smooth compactly supported functions avoiding the boundary pole (the cutoff cost for smooth tests tends to zero in dimension \(N\ge 3\)). Kato applied to the differences gives convergence of weighted traces in their weighted \(L^2\) norm. The ordinary gradients converge in \(L^2\), so the form values converge. On every compact interior region the reciprocal profile is smooth with bounded derivatives, and the transformed gradients converge locally in \(L^2\). Exhaustion proves the inequality. Translation is an isometry and no uniform-in-\(s\) approximation choice is needed.

For the bounded approximations the retained critical estimate in §7.3 thus gives \(\|u_n(t)\|_2^2+2\int_0^t\int\Psi_s^2|\nabla(u_n/\Psi_s)|^2\le\|f\|_2^2\). The profile is jointly continuous in \(x,s\) in the open half-space, with continuous spatial derivatives there, since the path is continuous. On each bounded interior subdomain \(K\Subset\mathcal H\), \(\Psi_s\) and its reciprocal are bounded uniformly for all \(s\), as are their spatial derivatives. The global strong \(L^2(0,T;Y)\) convergence established in §7.3 implies strong convergence of \(u_n/\Psi_s\) in \(L^2(K\times(0,t))\). The estimate supplies a bounded sequence of spatial gradients there. Weak compactness identifies any weak gradient limit with the distributional spatial gradient of \(u/\Psi_s\), by testing against smooth compactly supported space-time functions and integrating only in space. Multiplication by the bounded \(\Psi_s\) preserves weak convergence, and weak lower semicontinuity gives the desired local bound.

For every fixed \(t\), take the lower limit of the combined inequality using strong convergence \(u_n(t)\to u(t)\) in \(Y\). Exhaust \(K\) by the sets \(\{|x|<j,\ x_N>1/j\}\) (or a nested interior exhaustion); local distributional gradients agree on overlaps. Monotone convergence for the nonnegative squared weighted gradient yields (7.24). The argument works for each \(t\) independently, and the gradient on the full cylinder restricts to each shorter cylinder. At \(t=0\) the claim is the initial equality. □

This is a bound in a weighted gradient space, not a proof of global unweighted \(H^1\) regularity. For its implications and limitations regarding logarithmic profiles see Proposition 13.7 and Open question 13.2. Weak energy-class uniqueness at criticality is conditional on membership in that class; see Proposition 13.9.

Chapter 7 · Takeaways

The positive series supplies bounded solutions. Identification with weak evolution permits energy testing. The exact translated Kato inequality controls every regularization at the endpoint; Moser iteration and localized heat kernel estimates recover local boundedness and continuity without a global gradient bound. Positivity then makes monotone convergence and minimality possible.
