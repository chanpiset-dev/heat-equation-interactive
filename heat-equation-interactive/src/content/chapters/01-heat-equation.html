Part I

1. Heat equation and boundary interaction

How does a positive boundary input coexist with local cooling?

Prerequisites and proof outline

The governing equation; elementary differentiation and integration.

Derive the heat equation from flux → compare boundary conditions → inspect a regularized moving coefficient.

1.1 Heat diffusion

The governing equation in §0.1 uses diffusivity one. To see why, start from conservation of heat and the direction in which heat flows. The whole-space Gaussian below isolates interior diffusion before we return to the boundary.

Let \(u(x,t)\) denote temperature and \(J(x,t)\) the heat flux. With constant thermal conductivity, Fourier’s law states that heat flows opposite to the temperature gradient. For volumetric heat capacity \(c>0\) and conductivity \(k>0\), the heat balance takes the following form.

\[J=-k\nabla u,\qquad c\,\partial_t u=-\operatorname{div}J=k\Delta u.\tag{1.1}\]

Normalizing the diffusivity \(k/c\) to one gives the heat equation used in this study. The Laplacian \(\Delta u=\sum_{j=1}^N\partial_{x_j}^2u\) is the sum of second directional derivatives in the coordinate directions. At a smooth interior local maximum, \(\Delta u\le0\), so diffusion tends to flatten peaks.

\[\partial_tu=\Delta u.\tag{1.2}\]
Example · Local diffusion

In one dimension, at an interior point with \(u(x)=1-x^2\) locally, \(u_{xx}=-2\), so the instantaneous time derivative is negative. For \(u(x)=1+x^2\), it is positive. In several dimensions the second derivatives are added: a saddle point need not have zero Laplacian. These are local calculations, not global solutions.

Example 1.1 · Gaussian initial data

In the whole space, take \(u(x,0)=\exp(-|x|^2/(4b_0))\), \(b_0>0\). The solution is

\[u(x,t)=\left(\frac{b_0}{b_0+t}\right)^{N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^2}{4(b_0+t)}\right).\]

Its central value decreases, while its integral over the whole space stays constant. Chapter 2 derives this using the heat kernel.

1.2 Boundary conditions and heat balance

Imagine the same initial heat distribution in three identical intervals: a boundary held at zero temperature, an insulated boundary, and a boundary supplying heat proportionally to its temperature. These are Dirichlet, Neumann and positive Robin conditions, respectively. The outward-flux sign will distinguish them.

Let \(\nu\) be the outward unit normal of a domain \(\Omega\). The normal derivative is \(\partial_\nu u=\nabla u\cdot\nu\). In normalized units the outward component of the heat flux is \(J\cdot\nu=-\partial_\nu u\), so \(\partial_\nu u>0\) corresponds to heat entering the domain.

Boundary conditionFormulaInterpretation
Dirichlet\(u=g\)Prescribes the boundary temperature.
Neumann\(\partial_\nu u=q\)Prescribes the normal heat flux. \(q=0\) means insulation.
Robin\(\partial_\nu u=bu\)Flux proportional to the boundary temperature. Under our sign convention, \(b>0\) gives amplification.

For a smooth solution on a bounded smooth domain, assuming that differentiation under the integral is justified and the boundary flux is integrable, the divergence theorem gives

\[\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega u\,dx=\int_\Omega\Delta u\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_\nu u\,d\sigma.\tag{1.3}\]

In the half-space, one can apply the balance on truncated half-balls and pass to infinity only if the omitted spherical flux tends to zero and the volume and planar integrals converge. In particular, neither finite \(L^2\) norm nor continuity away from the singular point alone guarantees this identity. We use it here for smooth bounded-coefficient models, not as an assertion about all singular integral solutions.

Example · Prescribed flux versus feedback

If \(\partial_\nu u=q(x,t)\), the incoming flux is specified independently of the unknown. For Robin data \(\partial_\nu u=Vu\), two points with the same coefficient but temperatures 1 and 2 have fluxes \(V\) and \(2V\). This is a linear feedback law. It is not a heater that injects a fixed amount of energy regardless of temperature.

Thus, when boundary terms at infinity vanish in the half-space, a nonnegative temperature and a positive Robin coefficient contribute to increasing total heat. This does not imply that the temperature at every point must increase. In a finite domain, artificial Dirichlet boundaries may also allow heat loss.

1.3 Moving boundary coefficient

The model introduces a coefficient inversely proportional to the distance from a boundary point \(\xi(t)\). The singularity means that the coefficient diverges; the moving point is not a heat particle being transported. There is no transport term in the interior equation.

\[\partial_\nu u(x,t)=V(x,t)u(x,t),\qquad V(x,t)=\frac{\lambda}{|x-\xi(t)|}.\tag{1.4}\]

The three quantities \(V\), \(u\) and \(Vu\) must be distinguished. The coefficient is singular at the boundary point, but this does not prescribe the value of the solution there. Nor does the trajectory describe a moving material particle: the interior PDE contains no advection term. For the half-space, \(\nu=-e_N\), so the boundary condition is \(-\partial_{x_N}u=Vu\). With positive temperature this represents inward flux in normalized units.

\(\lambda>0\) is the coupling constant. The coefficient is finite for \(x\ne\xi(t)\), but grows as the distance approaches zero. In numerical experiments, one may instead use the following bounded coefficient.

\[V_\varepsilon(x,t)=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{|x-\xi(t)|^2+\varepsilon^2}},\qquad \varepsilon>0.\tag{1.5}\]

1.4 Stationary and moving singularities

The next calculation is a regularized three-dimensional finite-box experiment, not the singular half-space theorem. The initial bump is shared between both runs, the sides and top have zero Dirichlet values, and the bottom has a bounded Robin coefficient. The introductory preset (\(\lambda = 0.85\), \(\varepsilon = 0.20\), sinusoidal motion) is selected for visible contrast; the revised thesis preset (\(\lambda = 0.55\), \(\varepsilon = 0.20\), linear motion) is available in the experiment settings. Chapter 11 gives the scheme and its limitations.

Use the same initial temperature and compare a fixed center with a moving center. Past boundary amplification also affects diffusion, so the present position of the center alone does not determine the temperature field.

Scope of the physical interpretation. A positive Robin coefficient represents boundary amplification proportional to temperature. This models temperature-dependent boundary input, not a prescribed external heat source.

Chapter 1 · Takeaways

Interior diffusion redistributes temperature. The positive Robin condition supplies an inward flux proportional to the boundary value; the moving center changes the coefficient, not the transport law. The figures solve bounded regularizations on a finite box.

Check your understanding

Does \(V>0\) force every temperature value to rise? What changes when the center moves?

Answers: no, diffusion and outer-boundary losses may dominate locally; the spatial and temporal boundary coefficient changes, while the interior PDE is still the heat equation.
