Part III

8. Proof of supercritical nonexistence

Why does a negative form value lead to complete divergence, rather than just some growth?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Sharpness, Gaussian comparison, and Appendix D.1–D.3 for spectral measures, forms and positive semigroups.

Negative test energy → concentration → spectral Jensen → propagation by positive kernels → moving-frame comparison → contradiction.

We prove Theorem 3.2 (research paper Theorem 1.2). Let \(N\ge3\), \(T>0\), \(u_0\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\), \(u_0\ge0\), \(u_0\not\equiv0\), and \(\xi\in C([0,T];\partial\mathcal H)\). Assume \(\lambda>\lambda_N\) and the uniform motion condition (M), namely \(\omega(h)\to0\) as \(h\downarrow0\). We will rule out a finite nonnegative integral solution on every \([0,S]\), \(0<S\le T\).

In Chapter 7 the form inequality gave an upper estimate for bounded approximations. Here the sign reverses for suitable test functions. This does not yet prove nonexistence: a test function with negative energy need not describe the temperature of a particular solution. The missing link is a positive semigroup argument that turns concentration into divergence for every nonzero nonnegative datum and every interior observation.

8.1 Negative quadratic forms

At the origin, write the form of Theorem 4.1 as

\[Q_{\mu,0}[w]=\mathcal E[w]-\mu B_0[w],\qquad B_0[w]=\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|\operatorname{Tr}w(y)|^2}{|y|}\,d\sigma(y).\tag{8.1}\]

Here \(\mu\) denotes a stationary coupling, which will later be obtained from the moving coupling \(\lambda\). The form is finite on \(H^1(\mathcal H)\) by the Kato inequality. At or below the threshold it is nonnegative. Above the threshold, the optimality proved in §6.7 ensures a real smooth compactly supported function on the closed half-space, with positive boundary mass, whose quotient \(\mathcal E[w]/B_0[w]\) is less than \(\mu\). Thus its form is negative.

Proposition 8.1 · Negative test function

If \(\mu>\lambda_N\), there exist \(a>0\) and a nonnegative, continuous, bounded, compactly supported \(\varphi\in H^1(\mathcal H)\) with \(\|\varphi\|_2=1\) and \(Q_{\mu,0}[\varphi]=-a\).

Proof. Take a real test function supplied by sharpness, replace it by its absolute value, and divide by its positive volume norm. The absolute value preserves the squared gradient integral for real Sobolev functions (the gradient is zero almost everywhere on the zero level set), the trace is the absolute value of the trace, and the boundary mass and volume norm are unchanged. See Appendix B.8. This gives the stated properties without requiring the absolute value to be smooth. □
Negative energy is not an eigenvalue

For a lower-bounded self-adjoint operator associated with a closed form, a negative form value for a unit vector implies that the spectrum meets the negative real axis: if the spectrum were nonnegative then every spectral first moment would be nonnegative. It does not imply the existence of an eigenvector. A continuous-spectrum example is given in Appendix D.1. The singular form above is unbounded below after dilation; we do not construct a self-adjoint singular generator or assert any spectral type for it. The actual proof uses only the bounded-coefficient operators.

8.2 Concentration by scaling

Compress this test function about the origin but preserve its \(L^2\) norm. For \(0<\rho\le1\), define

\[\varphi_\rho(x)=\rho^{-N/2}\varphi(x/\rho).\tag{8.2}\]
Scaling calculation. The half-space is invariant under dilation. With \(x=\rho z\), the volume Jacobian is \(\rho^N\). With \(y=\rho z\) on the plane the boundary Jacobian is \(\rho^{N-1}\), while the inverse-distance coefficient contributes \(\rho^{-1}\). Hence
\[\begin{aligned}\|\varphi_\rho\|_2^2&=\|\varphi\|_2^2=1,\\ \mathcal E[\varphi_\rho]&=\rho^{-2}\mathcal E[\varphi],\\ B_0[\varphi_\rho]&=\rho^{-2}B_0[\varphi],\\ Q_{\mu,0}[\varphi_\rho]&=-a\rho^{-2}.\end{aligned}\tag{8.3}\]
The singular form is therefore unbounded below on the unit sphere of \(L^2\). In particular one cannot define a semibounded operator for the entire singular form by the bounded-coefficient construction of Chapter 7. This conclusion by itself is not a proof about integral solutions.
Example 8.1 · An explicit concentrating family (supplementary)

In dimension four let \(w(x)=(1-|x|)_+\) on \(\overline{\mathcal H}\). The hemisphere area is \(\pi^2\), and the unit sphere in the boundary has area \(4\pi\). Radial integration gives

\[\|w\|_2^2=\pi^2\int_0^1 r^3(1-r)^2\,dr=\frac{\pi^2}{60},\quad \mathcal E[w]=\frac{\pi^2}{4},\quad B_0[w]=\frac{\pi}{3}.\]

For \(\varphi=\sqrt{60}\,w/\pi\), the normalized energies are \(15\) and \(20/\pi\). At \(\mu=3\), the form value is \(15-60/\pi<0\). This simple tent is not a minimizing sequence and only gives a negative value for \(\mu>3\pi/4\); Proposition 8.1 covers every supercritical coupling using sharpness instead.

We must work with bounded coefficients before using spectral theory. For an integer \(m\ge1\), put

\[V_m(y)=\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{|y|^2+m^{-2}}},\qquad q_m[w]=\mathcal E[w]-\int_{\partial\mathcal H}V_m|\operatorname{Tr}w|^2\,d\sigma.\tag{8.4}\]

For each fixed \(\rho\), monotone convergence of the nonnegative boundary integrands gives \(q_m[\varphi_\rho]\downarrow Q_{\mu,0}[\varphi_\rho]\). The limiting boundary integral is finite by Kato. Consequently, there exists \(m_\rho\) such that

\[q_m[\varphi_\rho]\le-\frac{a}{2\rho^2},\qquad m\ge m_\rho.\tag{8.5}\]

The cutoff index depends on the compression scale. We do not let \(\rho\) tend to zero at a fixed \(m\).

Proposition 8.1a · Two independent length scales (supplementary)

For the unit test function in Proposition 8.1 and \(\rho>0\), the regularized form obeys

\[q_m[\varphi_\rho]=\rho^{-2}\left[\mathcal E[\varphi]-\mu\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{|\operatorname{Tr}\varphi(y)|^2}{\sqrt{|y|^2+(m\rho)^{-2}}}\,d\sigma(y)\right].\tag{8.5a}\]

In particular, as \(m\rho\to\infty\), the expression in brackets tends to \(-a\). At fixed \(m\), however, \(q_m[\varphi_\rho]\to+\infty\) as \(\rho\downarrow0\).

Proof. In the boundary term substitute \(y=\rho z\). The trace square contributes \(\rho^{-N}\), the surface element contributes \(\rho^{N-1}\), and the denominator factors as \(\rho\sqrt{|z|^2+(m\rho)^{-2}}\). This yields (8.5a) with the bulk scaling from (8.3). The bracket converges to \(Q_{\mu,0}[\varphi]=-a\) by dominated convergence, with majorant \(\mu |\operatorname{Tr}\varphi(z)|^2/|z|\), integrable by Kato. It follows that some finite \(K_*\) has the property that \(m\rho\ge K_*\) implies (8.5). Conversely, the regularized coefficient is bounded by \(\mu m\). The trace square integral scales like \(\rho^{-1}\), so

\[q_m[\varphi_\rho]\ge\rho^{-2}\mathcal E[\varphi]-\mu m\rho^{-1}\|\operatorname{Tr}\varphi\|_2^2\longrightarrow+\infty\quad(\rho\downarrow0,\ m\text{ fixed}).\tag{8.5b}\]

Here \(\mathcal E[\varphi]>0\): a zero-gradient function is constant on the connected half-space, and a nonzero constant is not in \(L^2\). Thus the quadratic inverse-length term dominates the linear one. □

Interpretation. The concentration length \(\rho\) and the regularization length \(m^{-1}\) must be compared. If the regularization length is much smaller than the test-function support, the rescaled potential is close to the singular one. If the test function is compressed inside a fixed regularization length, the potential looks bounded and cannot offset the increasing gradient energy. This is why the proof chooses \(\rho\) first and only then \(m\) large enough; a single joint path with \(m\rho\to\infty\) also suffices for the negative-form estimate, but is not needed for the quantified divergence proof.

8.3 Spectral amplification for bounded Robin problems

A negative form value alone does not prove nonexistence. Think first of a scalar eigenmode with generator −A: an eigenvalue −a of A produces exp(at). The general proof uses an average over spectral values rather than assuming an eigenfunction exists.

For any bounded continuous stationary \(V\ge0\) on the boundary, use the real symmetric form \(q_V\) of (8.4) with \(V\) in place of \(V_m\). The trace estimate of §7.2, with a sufficiently small trace tolerance, proves

\[c\|w\|_{H^1}^2\le q_V[w]+C\|w\|_2^2\le C'\|w\|_{H^1}^2\tag{8.6}\]

for suitable positive constants, possibly depending on \(V\). Thus the form is dense, closed and bounded below. The representation theorem (proved from the bounded spectral theorem in Appendix D.2) yields a self-adjoint operator \(A_V\) with

\[\begin{aligned}D(A_V)&=\{w\in H^1(\mathcal H):\exists g\in L^2(\mathcal H),\\ &\qquad q_V(w,v)=\langle g,v\rangle\ \forall v\in H^1(\mathcal H)\},\\ A_Vw&=g.\end{aligned}\tag{8.7}\]

The form is polarized in the usual way. Integration by parts on smooth domain elements gives \(A_V=-\Delta\) and \(\partial_\nu w=Vw\); the sign of the boundary term is essential. Define the heat semigroup \(S_V(t)=e^{-tA_V}\) through spectral calculus. Its weak evolution equals that of Chapter 7, as shown in Appendix D.3. In particular it gives the bounded integral solution for compact continuous data. Positivity and free-flow domination, \(S_V(t)g\ge P_tg\) for \(g\ge0\), extend to all nonnegative \(L^2\) data by approximation in \(L^2\). We use this order in the next section.

Lemma 8.1 · Spectral Jensen inequality

For a unit vector \(v\) in the form domain of a lower-bounded self-adjoint operator \(A\), with quadratic form \(q\), and \(h>0\),

\[\langle v,e^{-hA}v\rangle\ge \exp(-h q[v]).\tag{8.8}\]

Proof. The spectral projection measure \(E_A\) determines a probability measure \(\mathfrak m_v(J)=\|E_A(J)v\|_2^2\) for Borel sets \(J\subset\mathbb R\). Because \(\|v\|_2=1\), its total mass is one. Choose a shift \(C\) so that \(A+C\ge1\). The form-domain identity from Appendix D.2 gives

\[\int (\ell+C)\,d\mathfrak m_v(\ell)=\|(A+C)^{1/2}v\|_2^2=q[v]+C.\]

In particular \(\ell\) is integrable since it is bounded below and has finite shifted first moment. Spectral calculus yields \(\langle v,e^{-hA}v\rangle=\int e^{-h\ell}d\mathfrak m_v\), finite since the spectrum is bounded below. Jensen's inequality applied to the convex exponential gives (8.8). This uses only membership in the form domain, not \(D(A)\), and does not assume a discrete spectrum. □

Apply the lemma to \(A_{V_m}\) and \(v=\varphi_\rho\). Equation (8.5) gives, for every fixed \(\rho\) and sufficiently large \(m\),

\[\langle\varphi_\rho,S_{V_m}(h)\varphi_\rho\rangle\ge\exp\left(\frac{ha}{2\rho^2}\right).\tag{8.9}\]

This is a large pairing for specially chosen input and output. How does it force growth for the prescribed datum and at any interior point? The positivity of heat flow supplies both connections.

8.4 Complete stationary divergence

Amplification of a localized test function must be transported to the observation point. The positive kernel spreads the initial heat toward the test region, permits the spectral estimate, and spreads the result outward.

Proposition 8.2 · Stationary bounded-approximation divergence

Let \(\mu>\lambda_N\), \(f_0\in C_c(\overline{\mathcal H})\), \(f_0\ge0\), \(f_0\not\equiv0\). For \(V_m\) in (8.4), let \(w_m(x,t)=S_{V_m}(t)f_0(x)\) denote its continuous bounded integral representative. Then for every \(x\in\mathcal H\) and \(t>0\),

\[w_m(x,t)\uparrow+\infty\quad(m\to\infty).\tag{8.10}\]
Three equal time intervals — spread, amplify, propagate
First · \(t/3\)Free heat gives a positive lower bound on the test support.Middle · \(t/3\)Spectral Jensen gives exponential amplification of a pairing.Last · \(t/3\)A positive kernel transports the lower bound to \(x\).

Schematic only: the three actual operators are bounded Robin semigroups. Replacing the first and last by free heat provides lower bounds, not an equality. The middle panel illustrates amplification of a pairing, not a temperature profile or pointwise estimate.

Proof. Fix \(x\in\mathcal H\), \(t>0\), and write \(h=t/3\). Let \(K\) be a closed half-ball containing the supports of all \(\varphi_\rho\), \(0<\rho\le1\), and put \(M_\varphi=\|\varphi\|_\infty>0\).

Step 1 · Spread the datum

By strict positivity of the Neumann kernel and nontriviality of the datum, \(P_hf_0>0\) throughout the closed half-space. Its minimum \(c\) on \(K\) is positive. Since \(\varphi_\rho\le\rho^{-N/2}M_\varphi\), positivity and free-flow domination imply

\[S_{V_m}(h)f_0\ge P_hf_0\ge \frac{c\rho^{N/2}}{M_\varphi}\varphi_\rho.\tag{8.11}\]

Step 2 · Amplify and propagate

The semigroup identity and preservation of order give an \(L^2\) inequality

\[S_{V_m}(t)f_0\ge\frac{c\rho^{N/2}}{M_\varphi}P_h S_{V_m}(h)\varphi_\rho.\]

The right side has a continuous representative because \(P_h\) at positive time maps \(L^2\) to continuous functions (Cauchy–Schwarz and \(L^2\) continuity of translated Gaussians). The left has the continuous bounded representative from Chapter 7. Thus the a.e. inequality holds pointwise in the interior. For fixed \(x,h\), the kernel \(G(x,y,h)\) has a strictly positive minimum \(c_x\) on \(K\). Since \(S_{V_m}(h)\varphi_\rho\ge0\),

\[\begin{aligned}w_m(x,t)&\ge\frac{cc_x\rho^{N/2}}{M_\varphi}\int_K S_{V_m}(h)\varphi_\rho(y)\,dy\\&\ge\frac{cc_x\rho^N}{M_\varphi^2}\langle\varphi_\rho,S_{V_m}(h)\varphi_\rho\rangle\\&\ge\frac{cc_x}{M_\varphi^2}\rho^N e^{ha/(2\rho^2)}.\end{aligned}\tag{8.12}\]

The middle inequality uses the pointwise bound on \(\varphi_\rho\) and its support in \(K\); the last uses (8.9), for \(m\ge m_\rho\). The constants \(c,c_x,M_\varphi\) are independent of both indices.

Step 3 · Take limits in the correct order

Increasing \(m\) increases the coefficient and hence the solution by positive iteration (Lemma 7.1). For each fixed \(\rho\), (8.12) holds for every sufficiently large \(m\). Now \(N\log\rho+ha/(2\rho^2)\to+\infty\), since \(\rho^2|\log\rho|\to0\). Given any finite target, first choose \(\rho\) to make the right side exceed it, then choose \(m\ge m_\rho\). This proves (8.10). □

What has been proved? Every bounded Robin problem separately has a finite solution. Their increasing sequence diverges at each positive time and every interior point; no singular limit is assumed to be continuous or to satisfy a PDE. The original stationary result of Ishige and Ishiwata uses different truncations and logarithmic testing; the spectral proof here follows the revised research paper and thesis for the full half-space approximations. See Appendix D.4 for the logarithmic identity.

8.5 Lower Gaussian comparison in moving coordinates

Return to the moving problem with coupling \(\lambda\). If a finite integral solution exists on \([0,S]\), write \(U(x,t)=u(x+\xi(t),t)\) and \(f_0(x)=u_0(x+\xi(0))\). The moving integral equation was derived in (5.4a). For \(0<\tau\le S\), set \(M=\omega(\tau)\). This bound controls every pair of times in the comparison window, not just the displacement from time zero.

Lemma 8.2 · Lower kernel comparison

Assume \(M<\infty\). For any \(\delta>0\), let

\[D_-=(1+\delta)^{-1},\qquad c_-=(1+\delta)^{-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{(1+\delta)M^2}{4\delta}\right).\tag{8.13}\]

For \(x,y\in\overline{\mathcal H}\), \(0\le s<t\le\tau\),

\[K_\xi(x,y;t,s)\ge c_-G(x,y,D_-(t-s)).\tag{8.14}\]

Proof. Put \(h=t-s\), \(d_\xi=\xi(t)-\xi(s)\), and \(z=x-y\). Expanding the square and using \(2z\cdot d_\xi\le\delta|z|^2+\delta^{-1}|d_\xi|^2\) yields

\[|z+d_\xi|^2\le(1+\delta)|z|^2+(1+\delta^{-1})|d_\xi|^2.\]

Divide by \(4h\), use \(|d_\xi|^2/h\le M^2\), and exponentiate with a negative sign. Comparing the normalizations of \(g_h\) and \(g_{D_-h}\) supplies \(D_-^{N/2}\). The same computation holds for \(z=x-y^*\) since tangential translation commutes with reflection. Summing both lower bounds proves (8.14). Unlike the upper comparison, this direction permits every \(\delta>0\). □

Figure 5.3 already shows the Gaussian comparison itself. Here the narrower kernel runs at a slower time \(D_-t\), with a multiplicative loss \(c_-\). Both losses matter in the boundary integral.

8.6 Keeping the effective coupling supercritical

Substitute \(r=D_-t\), \(q=D_-s\) in a stationary boundary integral: \(ds=dq/D_-\). The coefficient after comparison is therefore

\[\mu_-:=\frac{c_-\lambda}{D_-}=\lambda(1+\delta)^{1-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{(1+\delta)M^2}{4\delta}\right).\tag{8.15}\]

For fixed \(\delta>0\), condition (M) implies \(\mu_-\to\lambda(1+\delta)^{1-N/2}\) as \(\tau\downarrow0\). Since \(\lambda>\lambda_N\), choose \(\delta\) first so the latter is strictly larger than \(\lambda_N\); then choose \(\tau\le S\) small enough that \(\mu_->\lambda_N\). Equivalently, with \(R=\lambda/\lambda_N>1\), choose \(0<\delta<R^{1/(N/2-1)}-1\) and require

\[M^2<\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}\left[\log R-(N/2-1)\log(1+\delta)\right].\tag{8.16}\]

The right side is positive by the first choice; the motion condition ensures the second. This sequential choice is important. Choosing \(\delta\) too small at a fixed nonzero \(M\) would increase the exponential penalty instead.

Example 8.2 · A constant-velocity trajectory (supplementary)

For \(N=4\), \(\lambda=2\lambda_4\), \(\xi(t)=vte_1\), \(|v|=1\), take \(\delta=1/4\). With \(\tau\le T\), \(M^2=\tau\); hence \(\mu_-/\lambda_4=1.6\,e^{-1.25\tau}\). Any \(0<\tau<\log(1.6)/1.25\approx0.376\) satisfies the strict comparison, for instance \(\tau=0.1\) if the interval permits. The argument works on arbitrarily short positive intervals.

Sequential choice of parameters

Write \(R=\lambda/\lambda_N\). Choose \(0<\delta<R^{1/(N/2-1)}-1\) while ignoring motion; this leaves a positive logarithmic margin. Then shrink \(\tau\) until (8.16) holds for the actual \(M=\omega(\tau)\). For constant velocity, this is an explicit upper bound on the window length. For a general trajectory satisfying (M), the limit supplies such a window but need not give a computable rate.

8.7 Positive iteration and contradiction

Fix the short window just chosen, abbreviate \(c=c_-\), \(D=D_-\), \(\mu=\mu_->\lambda_N\), and suppose a finite nonnegative integral solution exists. Define for \(0<t\le\tau\) and \(x\ne0\) on the closed half-space

\[\begin{aligned}F_\xi(x,t)&=\int_{\mathcal H}K_\xi(x,y;t,0)f_0(y)\,dy,\\ (A^{\rm mov}W)(x,t)&=\lambda\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}K_\xi(x,y;t,s)\frac{W(y,s)}{|y|}\,d\sigma\,ds.\end{aligned}\tag{8.17}\]

At time zero use \(F_\xi(x,0)=f_0(x)\). At the pole we may assign arbitrary nonnegative values to integrands, since its space-time section has measure zero. From the integral equation and Lemma 2.3, \(U\ge F_\xi+A^{\rm mov}U\) at every nonsingular positive-time observation, including the plane and terminal time. This boundary inequality is required because the iteration uses boundary values of the previous iterate.

Proof of Theorem 3.2.

Step 1 · Moving iterates remain below the putative solution

\[U^{(0)}=F_\xi,\qquad U^{(j+1)}=F_\xi+A^{\rm mov}U^{(j)}.\]

Positivity and the preceding supersolution inequality imply inductively \(0\le U^{(j)}\le U\) off the pole; consequently each iterate is finite there. Measurability follows from integration of nonnegative measurable kernels. At time zero the iterates agree with the datum.

Step 2 · Compare to stationary iterates

For \(0\le r\le D\tau\) set \(v^{(0)}(x,r)=P_rf_0(x)\) and, initially allowing extended nonnegative values,

\[v^{(j+1)}(x,r)=P_rf_0(x)+\mu\int_0^r\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,r-q)\frac{v^{(j)}(y,q)}{|y|}\,d\sigma\,dq.\tag{8.18}\]

The lower kernel bound gives \(U^{(0)}(x,t)\ge c v^{(0)}(x,Dt)\). If the inequality holds for \(j\), positivity gives

\[\begin{aligned}U^{(j+1)}(x,t)&\ge cP_{Dt}f_0(x)+c^2\lambda\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,D(t-s))\frac{v^{(j)}(y,Ds)}{|y|}\,d\sigma\,ds\\&=c\left[P_{Dt}f_0(x)+\frac{c\lambda}{D}\int_0^{Dt}\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,Dt-q)\frac{v^{(j)}(y,q)}{|y|}\,d\sigma\,dq\right]\\&=c v^{(j+1)}(x,Dt).\end{aligned}\tag{8.19}\]

Here the second line uses \(q=Ds\). The comparison holds for all \(j\) and all nonsingular observations, and in particular proves finiteness of the stationary iterates off zero. No stationary singular solution has been constructed.

Step 3 · Compare with each bounded approximation

Fix \(m\). Since \(V_m\le\mu/|y|\) off the pole, the bounded iterates \(\sum_{k=0}^j A_{V_m}^k(P_\cdot f_0)\) lie below \(v^{(j)}\) by induction. For fixed \(m\) the bounded Volterra series converges by Lemma 7.1. Thus letting \(j\to\infty\) gives

\[U(x,t)\ge c\,w_m(x,Dt)\qquad\text{for every }m.\tag{8.20}\]

Fix an interior \(x\) and \(0<t<\tau\). Proposition 8.2 applies at time \(Dt>0\), because \(\mu>\lambda_N\) and the translated datum is compact continuous, nonnegative and nonzero. The right side tends to infinity as \(m\) increases, while the left side is finite. This contradiction rules out the assumed solution on every positive initial interval. □

Why each hypothesis matters

Sharpness produces a negative form precisely above the threshold. Nonnegative nonzero data allow positive heat propagation, and nonnegative solutions allow order comparison. Condition (M) guarantees a short interval with surviving supercritical coupling. This proof does not settle all rough trajectories, does not establish sign-changing nonexistence, and does not imply that bounded regularized problems become infinite at finite m. At criticality the comparison has no strict margin; existence was proved independently in Chapter 7.

Stationary divergence and the final moving comparison are applications of the extended nonnegative positive-series principle; no finite singular stationary semigroup is presupposed.

Chapter 8 · Takeaways

Negative unit-mass form values scale like \(-\rho^{-2}\). Bounded Robin semigroups convert these values into exponential lower bounds. Positivity transports the amplification from arbitrary nontrivial nonnegative data to every interior point. A lower Gaussian comparison and a rescaled stationary clock preserve supercriticality for sufficiently short windows, and positive iteration transfers the stationary obstruction to the moving problem.
