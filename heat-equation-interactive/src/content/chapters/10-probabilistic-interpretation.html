Part IV

10. Probabilistic interpretation

Why do reflected random paths and boundary weights represent the same diffusion?

Prerequisites and proof outline

Neumann kernel; Appendix E.1–E.4 for conditioning, Itô calculus, reflection and sampling.

Brownian diffusion → reflection → regulator → boundary occupation → backward-time weight → exact expectation → regularized experiments and singular limit.

The same boundary interaction has three mathematical descriptions. In the PDE, positive outward normal derivative corresponds to inward heat flux. In the energy method, boundary amplification competes with the Dirichlet energy. In the probabilistic picture, a reflected diffusing path acquires a multiplicative weight when it contacts the plane. These are descriptions of the same linear evolution, not a model of literal heat particles reproducing.

10.1 Brownian motion and the heat generator

We use the probability notation in Appendix E. On a filtered probability space \((\Omega_{\rm prob},\mathcal F,(\mathcal F_s)_{s\ge0},\mathbb P)\), an \(N\)-dimensional standard Brownian motion \(W\) starts at zero, has continuous paths and independent increments with law \(W_{s+h}-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,hI_N)\). Each coordinate has variance \(h\), not \(2h\). The diffusion associated with our heat equation is

\[Y_s=x+\sqrt2 W_s,\qquad \mathbb E_x f(Y_s)=\int_{\mathbb R^N}g_s(x-y)f(y)\,dy.\tag{10.1}\]

For \(f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^N)\), a second-order Taylor expansion has zero expected linear term and second-order expectation \(h\Delta f(x)\), because the covariance of \(\sqrt2W_h\) is \(2h I_N\). Bounded third derivatives give a remainder of order \(h^{3/2}\) in expectation. Dividing by \(h\) proves that the infinitesimal generator on these tests is \(\Delta\). The law of \(a^{-1/2}W_{as}\) equals that of \(W\) by the increment and continuity properties. Consequently the root-mean-square displacement of \(Y\) over time \(h\) is \(\sqrt{2Nh}\), the scale in Chapter 5.

10.2 Reflection and the Skorokhod map

Let \(x\in\overline{\mathcal H}\). Tangential coordinates are unchanged by reflection. For the normal driving path \(Z_s=x_N+\sqrt2W_s^N\), define

\[L_s=\max\{0,-\min_{0\le r\le s}Z_r\},\quad X_s^N=Z_s+L_s,\quad X_s'=x'+\sqrt2W_s'.\tag{10.2}\]

The continuous regulator \(L\) is nondecreasing, starts at zero and satisfies \(\int_0^s\mathbf1_{\{X_r^N>0\}}\,dL_r=0\). Indeed a new negative running minimum can occur only when the corrected path is zero. Conversely any continuous nondecreasing correction \(K\) with these properties must satisfy \(K_s\ge L_s\): nonnegativity gives \(K_r\ge -Z_r\) and monotonicity gives the bound at \(s\). If \(K\) exceeded \(L\), on the open set where \(K>L\) the corrected path would be strictly positive, so \(K\) could not increase there; continuity and monotonicity of \(L\) preclude such an excursion. Thus (10.2) is the unique Skorokhod reflection map.

The map restarts at each time: after \(s\), the new regulator increments are obtained by reflecting the path starting at \(X_s^N\) driven by \(\sqrt2(W^N_{s+r}-W^N_s)\). By independence of Brownian increments (and the strong Markov property at stopping times), the reflected process is Markov, with the same restart law also at finite stopping times.

Example 10.1 · Reflection without randomness

For \(Z_s=1-2s\) on [0,1], the regulator is \(L_s=(2s-1)_+\) and the reflected path is \(X_s^N=(1-2s)_+\). Once the driver passes below the plane, the regulator compensates exactly. This deterministic example illustrates the map; it is not Brownian motion.

Recovering the Neumann kernel

Write \(g_s^{(1)}(b)=(4\pi s)^{-1/2}e^{-b^2/(4s)}\). For a normal start \(a\ge 0\) the reflection principle gives the joint endpoint and crossing law: for \(m\le\min(a,b)\),

\[\mathbb P(\min_{r\le s}Z_r\le m,\ Z_s\in db)=g_s^{(1)}(a+b-2m)\,db.\]
Derive the reflected density from this law

The no-crossing part at a positive height \(b\) has density \(g_s^{(1)}(b-a)-g_s^{(1)}(b+a)\). On paths with minimum \(m<0\) the reflected height is \(z=b-m\). Differentiate the crossing formula in \(m\) at fixed \(b\), then substitute \(b=z+m\) (Jacobian one). The crossing contribution at \(z>0\) is \(\int_{-\infty}^0 -2(g_s^{(1)})'(a+z-m)\,dm=2g_s^{(1)}(a+z)\). The two densities sum to \(g_s^{(1)}(z-a)+g_s^{(1)}(z+a)\), which integrates to one on \(z\ge 0\). The density of the pair and the no-crossing contribution account for all mass; the endpoint at zero has probability zero.

Combining the normal law with the independent tangential Gaussian gives exactly the method-of-images kernel \(G\) of Chapter 2. In particular

\[\mathbb E_x[f(X_s)]=P_sf(x)=\int_{\mathcal H}G(x,y,s)f(y)\,dy.\tag{10.3}\]

The notation \(|x_N+\sqrt2B_s|\) is another realization in law of the reflected normal process (its folded Gaussian transition law follows from symmetry and independence of increments); it is not the same pathwise driver \(W\) as in (10.2). We use (10.2) to track the regulator.

10.3 Boundary local time and the occupation identity

Counting sampled contacts with a boundary would depend on the time grid. Instead the regulator accumulates the normal push needed to keep a Brownian path in the half-space. Its normalization is what makes the occupation identity match the heat kernel.

In this chapter boundary local time means the regulator \(L\) of (10.2). This fixes a normalization that varies across references. The path equation is \(dX_s=\sqrt2\,dW_s+e_N\,dL_s\); ordinary time spent exactly on the plane is zero almost surely, because \(\mathbb P_x(X_s^N=0)=0\) for \(s>0\) and Tonelli applies. Nonetheless \(L\) can increase. For smooth functions Itô's formula gives

\[df(X_s)=\sqrt2\nabla f(X_s)\cdot dW_s+\Delta f(X_s)\,ds+\partial_N f(X_s)\,dL_s.\tag{10.4}\]

The quadratic variation of the Brownian part contributes \(\Delta f\); the finite-variation regulator has no quadratic variation or mixed quadratic variation. If \(\partial_N f=0\) on the boundary, the last term vanishes: this is the stochastic meaning of the Neumann condition.

Lemma 10.1 · Boundary occupation identity

For any nonnegative Borel function \(k:\partial\mathcal H\times[0,t]\to[0,\infty]\),

\[\mathbb E_x\int_0^t k(X_s,s)\,dL_s=\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,s)k(y,s)\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\tag{10.5}\]

Both sides may be infinite; the argument of \(k\) is on the plane on the support of \(dL\).

Proof. Let \(F_\varepsilon(0)=0\) and \(F_\varepsilon'(r)=\min(r/\varepsilon,1)\) for \(r\ge0\). Its derivative is Lipschitz, and \(F_\varepsilon''=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf1_{(0,\varepsilon)}\) almost everywhere. Smooth approximation of the derivative, or the piecewise-\(C^2\) extension of Itô's formula, applies: the finitely many exceptional heights have zero time occupation by (10.3). Since \(F_\varepsilon'(0)=0\), (10.4) yields

\[L_s^{\varepsilon}:=\frac1\varepsilon\int_0^s\mathbf1_{\{0<X_r^N<\varepsilon\}}\,dr=F_\varepsilon(X_s^N)-F_\varepsilon(x_N)-\sqrt2\int_0^sF_\varepsilon'(X_r^N)\,dW_r^N.\]

Subtract (10.2). The two deterministic errors have absolute value at most \(\varepsilon\) in total. Doob's \(L^2\) inequality and the Itô isometry bound the squared supremum of the stochastic difference on \([0,t]\) by \(8\int_0^t\mathbb P_x(X_r^N<\varepsilon)dr\le C\varepsilon\sqrt t\), using the normal density bound \((\pi r)^{-1/2}\). Thus \(L^\varepsilon\to L\) uniformly in probability, and even in mean square of the supremum.

For bounded continuous \(k\), extend \(k\) to the half-space by evaluating its tangential argument. The pathwise function \(s\mapsto k((X_s',0),s)\) is continuous. Uniform convergence in probability of the increasing integrators implies convergence of the Stieltjes integrals in probability: along any subsequence select an almost surely uniformly convergent subsubsequence; approximate the continuous integrand by step functions and use convergence of total masses. The absolute integrals are bounded by \(\|k\|_\infty L_t^\varepsilon\), uniformly integrable by the mean-square estimate. Expectations converge.

Before taking the limit, (10.3) and Fubini express the expectation as

\[\int_0^t\int_{\mathbb R^{N-1}}\frac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon G(x,(y',r),s)\,dr\ k((y',0),s)\,dy'\,ds.\]

At each fixed \(s>0\) the tangential Gaussian dominates the integrand, and integrating that Gaussian gives a bound \(\|k\|_\infty(\pi s)^{-1/2}\) independent of \(\varepsilon\). Dominated convergence first in space, then time, proves (10.5) for bounded continuous \(k\). The two sides define finite Borel measures when \(k\) is an indicator (total mass finite by the same estimate); equality on bounded continuous functions determines the measures. Simple-function approximation and monotone convergence now establish the assertion for nonnegative Borel k. □

At the boundary start \(x_N=0\), the reflection principle gives \(L_t\overset{d}=|\mathcal N(0,2t)|\), so \(\mathbb E_0L_t=2\sqrt{t/\pi}\). The same value follows from integrating the boundary mass in (2.10). This checks the factor in (10.5).

10.4 Feynman–Kac representation for bounded Robin coefficients

There are two clocks: stochastic elapsed time \(s\) starts at the observation, while the physical time runs backward from \(t\) to \(0\). The moving coefficient is therefore sampled at \(t-s\). The initial datum is evaluated at the path endpoint.

Consider bounded continuous \(V\ge0\) on \(\partial\mathcal H\times[0,T]\) and bounded nonnegative continuous initial datum \(f\) on the closed half-space. The equation is \(u_t=\Delta u\), \(\partial_\nu u=Vu\), \(u(\cdot,0)=f\). For a fixed target point \((x,t)\), follow a reflected path starting from x. Define

\[A_s=\int_0^s V((X_r',0),t-r)\,dL_r,\qquad 0\le s\le t.\tag{10.6}\]

Here \(s\) denotes stochastic elapsed time. The physical time of the PDE is \(t-s\): the path starts at the observation time \(t\), and the terminal factor at \(s=t\) samples the initial datum. The path is not the prescribed boundary trajectory \(\xi\). The argument \(t-r\) cannot be replaced by \(r\) for a general time-dependent V.

Theorem 10.1 · Bounded Robin Feynman–Kac formula

Under the preceding hypotheses the unique bounded continuous integral solution is

\[u(x,t)=\mathbb E_x\left[f(X_t)\exp\left(\int_0^t V((X_s',0),t-s)\,dL_s\right)\right].\tag{10.7}\]

In particular, \(V=0\) recovers the Neumann evolution.

Formal mechanism, made rigorous for bounded classical solutions. For a solution smooth enough for Itô's formula up to the plane and continuous at time zero, apply the product rule to \(M_s=e^{A_s}u(X_s,t-s)\). The result is

\[\begin{aligned}dM_s={}&\sqrt2e^{A_s}\nabla u(X_s,t-s)\cdot dW_s\\&+e^{A_s}(\Delta u-u_t)(X_s,t-s)\,ds\\&+e^{A_s}(\partial_Nu+Vu)(X_s,t-s)\,dL_s.\end{aligned}\]

The PDE cancels the ordinary-time term. Since \(\nu=-e_N\), the Robin condition cancels the local-time term. \(M\) is therefore a local martingale. Stop before leaving compact sets and before PDE time reaches zero, then pass to the endpoints. All stopped values are dominated by \(\|u\|_\infty e^{\|V\|_\infty L_t}\), integrable because \(L_t\le\sqrt2\sup_{r\le t}|W_r^N|\) has a Gaussian tail. Dominated convergence removes localization, giving (10.7). This calculation explains why the exponent is positive for amplification. It does not presume classical regularity for the continuous coefficients used below.

10.5 Derivation for bounded integral solutions

We now prove Theorem 10.1 without a classical-solution assumption. Call the expectation in (10.7) \(F(x,t)\), with \(F(x,0)=f(x)\). The Skorokhod map on a canonical continuous Brownian path is measurable jointly in the starting point, path and time: the running minimum may be evaluated using rational times and the endpoint. On each path the integrand in \(A\) is continuous and \(L\) is increasing, so Riemann–Stieltjes sums on uniform partitions of \([0,t]\) converge; these sums are jointly Borel. Parameter integration (Appendix B.9) makes \(F\) Borel. The Gaussian-tail bound makes \(F\) uniformly bounded for \(t\le T\) and every starting point \(x\).

Proof of the integral identity. The finite-variation chain rule gives \(e^{A_t}=1+\int_0^t e^{A_t-A_s}V((X_s',0),t-s)\,dL_s\). Multiplying by \(f\) at the terminal point and taking expectations, the first term is \(P_tf\). The second involves a random Stieltjes measure; conditioning inside it at deterministic times without justification would be insufficient.

Why the strong Markov argument is valid under \(dL\)

Extend the reflected process for all \(s\ge 0\). For \(\ell\ge0\) define \(\tau_\ell=\inf\{s:L_s>\ell\}\), allowing infinity. This is a stopping time for a right-continuous filtration. For a continuous increasing \(L\) the generalized-inverse change of variables is \(\int_0^t H_s\,dL_s=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{\{\tau_\ell<t\}}H_{\tau_\ell}\,d\ell\) for nonnegative Borel path integrands \(H\), with equality up to level endpoints of zero Lebesgue measure. It follows first for intervals by their \(L\)-masses, then for Borel functions by measure uniqueness. On \(\tau_\ell<t\), the restarted process and regulator increments have the reflected law starting at \(X_{\tau_\ell}\) by the strong Markov property and (10.2). The remaining physical horizon is \(t-\tau_\ell\), measurable at the stopping time. Thus the conditional expectation of \(f(X_t)e^{A_t-A_{\tau_\ell}}\) given the stopped past is \(F(X_{\tau_\ell},t-\tau_\ell)\); the assertion with a random remaining horizon follows from the independent future Brownian path and joint Borel measurability of the functional. Tonelli justifies integrating this identity in \(\ell\). Changing back gives the expectation of \(\int_0^t V((X_s',0),t-s)F(X_s,t-s)\,dL_s\).

Apply (10.5) to this last expression and change the deterministic integration variable from \(s\) to \(r=t-s\). We obtain

\[F(x,t)=P_tf(x)+\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,t-r)V(y,r)F(y,r)\,d\sigma(y)\,dr.\tag{10.8}\]

All terms are finite by boundedness and the integrable boundary mass. Although \(F\) was initially only Borel, its boundary potential is continuous by the recent-time split (bounded density) and dominated convergence in the separated time region; \(P_tf\) is locally continuous including its initial trace by Gaussian concentration. The bounded Volterra argument of Chapter 7 applies without compact support for bounded continuous \(f\): its supremum-norm series estimate uses only \(\|f\|_\infty\) and \(\|V\|_\infty\). Consequently (10.8) identifies \(F\) with the unique bounded integral solution. □

Inverse-regulator stopping times: geometric explanation

For continuous adapted nondecreasing \(L\), \(\{\tau_\ell<s\}=\{L_s>\ell\}\) for the strict inverse (with \(s>0\)); right continuity of the filtration gives the stopping-time property. The pushforward of Lebesgue measure on levels \([0,L_t)\) by this inverse is \(dL\), verified first on time intervals from their regulator increments, then by uniqueness of measures. Flat portions contribute zero level measure. This justifies the change of variables used before strong Markov conditioning.

10.6 An exactly solvable constant-coefficient model

Let \(f\equiv1\), \(V=b\ge0\) constant. This bounded-data example is not a compact-support example of Theorem 3.1. With \(z=x_N\), the reflection principle gives the law \(L_t\overset d=(|Z|-z)_+\), where \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,2t)\). Completing the square in the half-normal integral yields

\[u(z,t)=\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{2\sqrt t}\right)+e^{b^2t-bz}\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{z}{2\sqrt t}-b\sqrt t\right)\quad(t>0).\tag{10.9}\]
Derivation and boundary check

Split the integral at \(|Z|=z\). The first probability is \(\operatorname{erf}(z/(2\sqrt t))\); in the remaining integral multiply \(e^{b(\ell-z)}\) by \((\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-\ell^2/(4t)}\) for \(\ell\)>z. Completing the square gives the second term. At \(z=0\) this is \(2e^{b^2t}\Phi_{\mathrm G}(b\sqrt{2t})\), where \(\Phi_{\mathrm G}\) denotes the standard normal distribution function (distinct from the harmonic profile \(\Phi_\alpha\)). The formula equals one at \(b=0\), and its derivative with respect to \(b\) at zero and \(z=0\) is \(2\sqrt{t/\pi}\). Differentiating with respect to \(z\), the two Gaussian derivative terms cancel because \(b^2t-bz-(z/(2\sqrt t)-b\sqrt t)^2=-z^2/(4t)\), leaving \(u_z(0,t)=-bu(0,t)\), the required Robin sign. At \(t=0\) the continuous initial value is one.

Supplementary proposition 10.1 · Exact sampling variance

Let \(m_b(z,t)=\mathbb E_z e^{bL_t}\), given by (10.9). Gaussian maximum tails make every finite exponential moment finite. For independent copies \(Y_i=e^{bL_t^{(i)}}\), expanding the square and using independence gives

\[\operatorname{Var}(Y_1)=m_{2b}(z,t)-m_b(z,t)^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\right)=\frac{m_{2b}-m_b^2}{n}.\]

Cross-covariances vanish. The theoretical standard error is not a guaranteed error interval. At \(b=0\) it vanishes exactly; at strong amplification a finite sample can miss the dominant tail.

10.7 Moving coefficients: a microscopic laboratory

To explore the time-dependent mechanism, take a bounded regularization in \(N=3\),

\[V_\varepsilon(y,r)=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{(y_1-a(r))^2+y_2^2+\varepsilon^2}},\qquad a(r)=v r.\]

The following experiment estimates a solution value at one observation point \(x=(0,0,z)\) using independent reflected paths and \(f\equiv1\). We display the projection onto the \((x_1,x_N)\) plane, but compute the weight using both tangential coordinates. At a fixed terminal time \(t=1\), the moving coefficient travels along the boundary in reverse physical order as the stochastic path advances. The accumulated weight at intermediate \(s\) is \(e^{A_s}\), not the value \(u(x,s)\). Only the completed expectation corresponds to \(u(x,t)\).

Supplementary proposition 10.2 · Stieltjes quadrature error

Let \(\xi\) be Lipschitz with constant \(v\) and \(\varepsilon >0\). Write \(A_t^\Delta\) for the right-endpoint Stieltjes sum on a partition of mesh at most \(\Delta\), evaluating \(V\) on the boundary plane. Differentiation gives

\[\sup_y|\nabla_y V_\varepsilon|=\lambda\sup_{r\ge0}\frac{r}{(r^2+\varepsilon^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{2\lambda}{3\sqrt3\,\varepsilon^2}.\]

The maximum occurs at \(r=\varepsilon/\sqrt2\). At each time in a partition interval the difference from its endpoint potential is at most this constant times \(\omega_{X'}(\Delta)+v\Delta\), where \(\omega_{X'}(h)=\sup_{|s-r|\le h}|X'_s-X'_r|\) on \([0,t]\). Integrating against positive \(dL\) and summing yields

\[|A_t-A_t^\Delta|\le\frac{2\lambda L_t}{3\sqrt3\,\varepsilon^2}\big(\omega_{X'}(\Delta)+v\Delta\big).\tag{10.11}\]

Continuity gives almost-sure convergence for fixed \(\varepsilon\). For bounded \(f\), \(|f(X_t)e^{A_t^\Delta}|\le\|f\|_\infty e^{(\lambda/\varepsilon)L_t}\); the Gaussian maximum tail supplies an integrable dominator in every finite \(L^p\). The convergence is not uniform in \(\varepsilon\). For continuous \(\xi\) replace \(v\Delta\) by its ordinary continuity modulus.

10.8 Monotone regularization and scope of the interpretation

For the singular model write \(V(y,r)=\lambda/|y-\xi(r)|\) off the graph, with value +∞ on it. For \(m\ge1\), \(V_m=\min(m,V)\) is bounded continuous on the plane-time cylinder, including the pole when assigned the value \(m\). The soft coefficients of Chapter 7 also increase to \(V\). Define the singular path action as the monotone limit of actions for \(V_m\). For nonnegative bounded continuous \(f\), monotone convergence gives

\[\lim_{m\to\infty}u_m(x,t)=\mathbb E_x\left[f(X_t)\exp\left(\int_0^t\frac{\lambda}{|X_s-\xi(t-s)|}\,dL_s\right)\right]\in[0,+\infty].\tag{10.10}\]

On \(\{f(X_t)=0\}\) the product is defined to be zero, by the monotone limit even if the action is infinite. The graph has zero expected regulator occupation: apply (10.5) to the indicator \(y=\xi(t-s)\); each planar singleton has measure zero. Hence the regulator measure assigns zero mass to graph contacts almost surely. This does not imply that the inverse-distance action is finite: unbounded values near a measure-zero set may have a divergent integral.

Identification with the compact-data minimal solution

Under Theorem 3.1's hypotheses, let \(A\) be the full singular positive boundary operator and \(A_m\) its bounded-coefficient version. For every fixed \(k\), \(A_m^k(P_\cdot f)\uparrow A^k(P_\cdot f)\) by induction and monotone convergence, with extended values allowed. The bounded Volterra solution is the sum of these terms. The supremum over \(m\) commutes with the sum of nonnegative terms by monotone convergence on counting measure. Thus every such increasing regularization has limit \(\sum_{k\ge0}A^k(P_\cdot f)\). For the Chapter 7 regularization its limit \(u\) is a finite continuous integral solution off the graph, and the series is bounded above by \(u\) by positivity. Conversely each regularized series is bounded by the full series, so \(u\) is bounded above by that series. They agree, including nonsingular boundary and terminal observations. This proves identification for the compact-data regime \(0<\lambda\le\lambda_N\), with continuous \(\xi\). It uses the analytical existence theorem and does not independently prove it.

The bounded Feynman–Kac theorem uses bounded \(f\); the extension to weighted data requires the separate monotone argument below. The stochastic path \(X\) and the prescribed singularity path \(\xi\) have separate roles. A randomly chosen \(\xi\) would require specifying the random environment and the applicable trajectory hypotheses; simulating Brownian motion for \(X\) does not justify applying condition (M) to \(\xi\). The spectral nonexistence theorem and the Kato inequality remain the analytical results of Chapters 6–8. Neither a large empirical weight nor a finite sample mean decides singular solvability.

Supplementary proposition 10.3 · Finite singular action

Let \(N\ge 3\), \(\lambda \ge 0\), \(\xi\) continuous, and \((x,t)\in D_\xi\) with \(t>0\). Then \(\mathbb E_x A_t<\infty\) and \(A_t<\infty\) almost surely. Here A is the monotone limit of truncated actions in (10.10), zero for \(\lambda =0\).

Proof

By monotone convergence and the occupation identity,

\[\mathbb E_x A_t=\lambda\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}\frac{G(x,y,s)}{|y-\xi(t-s)|}\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\]

Set \(r=|x-\xi(t)|/4>0\). Choose \(s_0\)>0 so that \(|\xi(t-s)-\xi(t)|<r\) for \(s\le s_0\). For \(|y-\xi(t-s)|<r\) the distance to \(x\) is at least \(2r\). Since \(y\) is planar, both Gaussian terms coincide; the contribution is bounded by \(C s^{-N/2}e^{-r^2/s}\int_0^r a^{N-3}da\). The radial integral is finite for \(N\ge 3\). Outside the ball the inverse distance is at most \(1/r\), and boundary kernel mass is at most \((\pi s)^{-1/2}\). Both time bounds are integrable. For \(s\ge s_0\) the same translated split gives a uniform Gaussian prefactor on the near part and the kernel mass on the far part. Thus the expected action is finite; a nonnegative random variable of finite mean is finite almost surely.

Scope. Finite action does not imply finite exponential moment: an exponential random variable of rate one has finite mean but infinite expectation of its exponential. The threshold concerns the datum-weighted exponential expectation. This proposition is not an existence proof.

Supplementary theorem 10.4 · Weighted-data Feynman–Kac identity

Under Chapter 9's weighted solvability hypotheses, including the supplementary full interval \(\alpha_-<\alpha<\alpha_+\), let \(f\) be nonnegative continuous off \(p=\xi(0)\), dominated by \(M\Phi_\alpha(\cdot-p)\). Then at every positive-time nonsingular observation the minimal solution satisfies

\[u(x,t)=\mathbb E_x[f(X_t)e^{A_t}]<\infty.\tag{10.12}\]
Proof by two monotone approximations

For integers \(j\ge 1\) set \(R=|y-p|\), \(\chi_j(y)=\min(1,(jR-1)_+)\min(1,(j-R)_+)\), and \(f_j=\chi_j f\) with value zero at \(p\). These are bounded continuous compactly supported data, vanish near \(p\) and increase to \(f\) off p. At \(t>0\) the volume transition density implies \(X_t=p\) has probability zero. Assign \(f\) value zero there.

For each bounded \(V_m\), bounded-data Feynman–Kac identifies \(\mathbb E_x[f_j(X_t)e^{A_t^{(m)}}]\) with \(\sum_{k\ge0}A_m^k(P_\cdot f_j)(x,t)\), where \(A_m\) denotes the boundary integral operator, distinct from the action \(A_t^{(m)}\). All terms increase in both indices. Monotone convergence in the free term and inductively in every kernel integration gives \(\sup_{j,m}A_m^k(P_\cdot f_j)=A_\xi^k(P_\cdot f)\); the diagonal \(j=m\) is cofinal. Monotone convergence on counting measure interchanges sum and limit, and monotone convergence on path space gives the desired expectation. The full positive series equals the finite weighted minimal solution by §9.7, also at nonsingular boundary and terminal observations. At \(t=0\) the initial value is recovered directly. No unweighted exponential bound or unrestricted uniqueness follows.

Corollary 10.5 · Stationary endpoint Feynman–Kac representation (supplementary)

Under all hypotheses of Theorem 13.8, for every positive-time nonsingular observation, the stationary minimal solution has the finite representation

\[u(x,t)=\mathbb E_x\!\left[f(X_t)\exp\!\left(\int_0^t\frac{\lambda}{|X_s|}\,dL_s\right)\right]\le M\Phi_\alpha(x).\tag{10.13}\]

In particular this includes \(\lambda=C_N(\alpha)\), and hence critical coupling with \(\alpha=\alpha_*\). The pole can be translated to any fixed boundary point.

Proof · Cofinal data and coefficient approximations

Choose the nested compact cutoffs of Theorem 10.4 so that \(f_j\uparrow f\) off the initial pole, with \(f\) assigned zero there. Take the bounded nonnegative continuous coefficients \(V_m\uparrow V\) off the graph, for example the soft inverse-distance regularization; if \(\lambda=0\), take all coefficients zero. For every \(j,m\) the bounded Robin formula identifies the expectation with the positive series. For each finite series term, monotone convergence of the volume integral and then the boundary integrations gives convergence to the corresponding singular term. The diagonal \(j=m\) is cofinal in the two monotone indices, and monotone convergence on counting measure and path space identifies the singular series with the expectation. Theorem 13.8 constructs its minimal solution by the stationary harmonic supersolution, so the series is finite and satisfies the claimed bound. The volume transition density makes the assigned value of \(f\) at the initial pole irrelevant for \(t>0\); the regulator occupation identity removes the null boundary graph. Initial values are those already established by Theorem 13.8. No strict contraction or unweighted exponential moment bound is required. □

Chapter 10 · Takeaways

The generator \(\Delta\) fixes the diffusion variance. Reflection produces a regulator supported on boundary contacts, not a count of hits. Its occupation identity matches the Neumann boundary kernel. The positive exponential weights derive from Robin flux, with reversed physical time. Monotone convergence defines an extended expectation; finiteness and identification with a minimal singular solution require the earlier PDE analysis.

Check your understanding

Why is the increment scale √2 rather than 1? Why is the regulator not the same as time spent on the plane? Why is \(t-s\) used in \(V\)? Why does graph occupation zero not imply finite action? Why is a completed path average different from a density of unweighted endpoints?
