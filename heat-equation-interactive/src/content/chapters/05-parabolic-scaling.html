Part II

5. Parabolic scaling

How fast does the pole move compared with diffusion?

Prerequisites and proof outline

The Gaussian kernel and condition (M).

Identify the \(\sqrt h\) diffusion length → compare normalized displacements → shift Gaussian kernels and rescale time.

5.1 The characteristic diffusion length

The heat equation is invariant under simultaneous spatial and temporal rescaling \(x\mapsto r x\), \(t\mapsto r^2t\). The free heat kernel exhibits this invariance:

\[g_h(z)=h^{-N/2}g_1(z/\sqrt h),\qquad h>0.\tag{5.1}\]

This formula identifies \(\sqrt h\) as the spatial scale over a time interval of length \(h\). To quantify it, observe that the Gaussian factors into independent one-dimensional densities with variance \(2h\). Integration by parts gives

\[\int_{\mathbb R}r^2\frac{e^{-r^2/(4h)}}{\sqrt{4\pi h}}\,dr=2h,\qquad \int_{\mathbb R^N}|z|^2g_h(z)\,dz=2Nh.\tag{5.2}\]

Indeed \(g_h'(r)=-r g_h(r)/(2h)\) in one dimension, so integrating \(-2hr g_h'(r)\) gives \(2h\int g_h=2h\). Summing over coordinates yields the second identity. The root mean square displacement is \(\sqrt{2Nh}\); the Gaussian has nonzero tails at every distance, so this is not a finite propagation radius.

5.2 Comparing the motion with diffusion

Consider two times \(s<t\). In moving coordinates the Gaussian sees the shift \(d=\xi(t)-\xi(s)\) in its argument. The exponent contains a factor \(1/(t-s)\), so the meaningful size of the shift is \(|d|/\sqrt{t-s}\), not \(|d|\) alone.

\[\frac{|\xi(t)-\xi(s)|}{\sqrt{t-s}}\le\omega(h),\qquad 0<t-s\le h.\]

Condition (M) says the largest normalized shift on all sufficiently short intervals tends to zero. A Hölder estimate \(|\xi(t)-\xi(s)|\le L|t-s|^\gamma\) with \(\gamma>1/2\) gives

\[\omega(h)\le Lh^{\gamma-1/2}\longrightarrow0.\tag{5.3}\]

At \(\gamma=1/2\) this argument gives only \(\omega(h)\le L\), not vanishing. For smaller exponents this upper estimate does not imply either vanishing or divergence for every such path. A smoother path can also satisfy a weaker Hölder bound.

Example 5.1 · A sufficient condition, not an equivalence

Near zero take \(\xi(t)=\sqrt{t}/\log(e/t)\,e_1\), extended smoothly for positive times in a sufficiently short interval. This path is not Hölder of any exponent above \(1/2\), because its ratio to \(t^\gamma\) diverges as \(t\downarrow0\). Nevertheless (M) holds: for any \(\epsilon_0>0\), its derivative satisfies \(|\xi'(t)|\le\epsilon_0/(2\sqrt t)\) sufficiently near zero. Integrating on intervals in that region gives increments at most \(\epsilon_0\sqrt{t-s}\). Away from zero the derivative is bounded, giving a normalized increment of order \(\sqrt h\). Splitting intervals that cross the chosen cutoff proves uniform vanishing. This shows why (M) is the natural, weaker hypothesis.

Proposition 5.1 · The regularity hierarchy
\[C^\gamma\ (\gamma>1/2)\ \Longrightarrow\ (M)\ \Longrightarrow\ C^{1/2}\ \Longrightarrow\ C.\tag{5.3a}\]

Here the Hölder spaces refer to trajectories on the entire compact time interval. The first implication follows from the Hölder estimate; continuity follows from the defining Hölder bound. For the middle implication, choose \(h_0\in(0,T]\) with \(\omega(h_0)\le1\). For \(t-s\le h_0\), the normalized increment is at most one. For larger increments it is at most \(2\sup_{r\in[0,T]}|\xi(r)|/\sqrt{h_0}\). Thus the global half-Hölder seminorm is finite.

Why none of the implications reverses

The logarithmically corrected square-root path in Example 5.1 satisfies (M) but belongs to no Hölder class with exponent above one half. The path \(\xi(t)=\sqrt t\,e_1\) is half-Hölder on \([0,1]\) but has \(\omega(h)=1\) for \(0<h\le1\). Finally, \(\xi(t)=t^{1/4}e_1\) is continuous but not half-Hölder: the normalized increments from zero tend to infinity. The revised thesis also considers \(\xi(t)=e_1/\log(e/t)\) for \(t>0\), with \(\xi(0)=0\); it is continuous but fails every positive Hölder order at zero.

5.3 From parabolic displacement to kernel comparison

A moving coordinate frame shifts the tangential Gaussian argument by the difference of the pole positions at the source and observation times. We need estimates comparing that shifted kernel with an unshifted one, not a differential equation involving a derivative of the trajectory.

Write \(U(x,t)=u(x+\xi(t),t)\) and define the translated kernel

\[K_\xi(x,y;t,s)=G(x+\xi(t),y+\xi(s),t-s).\tag{5.4}\]

Set \(f_0(y)=u_0(y+\xi(0))\). Because each translation is parallel to the boundary, both the half-space and surface measure are unchanged. In the boundary integral at time \(s\), substitute the original source point \(Y=y+\xi(s)\); the singular denominator becomes \(|y|\). Applying the same change at time zero gives the complete moving-frame identity:

\[\begin{aligned}U(x,t)={}&\int_{\mathcal H}K_\xi(x,y;t,0)f_0(y)\,dy\\ &+\lambda\int_0^t\int_{\partial\mathcal H}K_\xi(x,y;t,s)\frac{U(y,s)}{|y|}\,d\sigma(y)\,ds.\end{aligned}\tag{5.4a}\]

This is an identity for interior observations in the integral-solution formulation; no differentiability of the trajectory is used. The Fatou inequality from §2.6 transfers to nonsingular boundary observations as well. Compare the original representation in §2.5.

For tangential shifts \(d=\xi(t)-\xi(s)\), the image term is shifted by the same vector. The elementary Young inequalities

\[\begin{aligned}|z+d|^2&\le(1+\delta)|z|^2+(1+\delta^{-1})|d|^2,\\|z+d|^2&\ge(1-\delta)|z|^2-(\delta^{-1}-1)|d|^2\end{aligned}\]

For the upper bound, expand the square and use \(2z\cdot d\le\delta |z|^2+\delta^{-1}|d|^2\) with \(\delta>0\). For the lower bound, use \(2z\cdot d\ge-\delta|z|^2-\delta^{-1}|d|^2\) with \(0<\delta<1\). Both are the adjustable Young inequality applied to the mixed term. Substituting in the Gaussian and accounting for its normalization gives the two-sided estimate below. For \(0<t-s\le\tau\) and \(M=\omega(\tau)<\infty\), set

\[D_\pm=(1\mp\delta)^{-1},\qquad c_\pm=(1\mp\delta)^{-N/2}\exp\left(\pm\frac{(1\mp\delta)M^2}{4\delta}\right).\]
\[c_-G(x,y,D_-(t-s))\le K_\xi(x,y;t,s)\le c_+G(x,y,D_+(t-s)).\tag{5.5}\]

The lower comparison alone allows any \(\delta>0\) (with \(D_-\) and \(c_-\)); both comparisons simultaneously use \(0<\delta<1\). To see the lower estimate, the upper quadratic inequality bounds the exponential from below; \(D_-^{-1}=1+\delta\) and the prefactor ratio is \(D_-^{N/2}\). The upper estimate follows similarly from the lower quadratic inequality. The same bounds apply to the image Gaussian, so adding preserves them.

Derivation of the effective coupling by time substitution

On an initial interval \([0,\tau]\), let \(D>0\) be either diffusivity factor and set \(W_D(y,q)=U(y,q/D)\). With \(q=Ds\) and \(r=Dt\), we have

\[\begin{aligned}&\lambda c\int_0^t\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,D(t-s))\frac{U(y,s)}{|y|}\,d\sigma\,ds\\ &=\frac{\lambda c}{D}\int_0^r\!\int_{\partial\mathcal H}G(x,y,r-q)\frac{W_D(y,q)}{|y|}\,d\sigma\,dq.\end{aligned}\tag{5.5a}\]

Thus in the stationary comparison the new coefficient is \(\mu=\lambda c/D\), not just \(\lambda c\). The free term is bounded separately by \(cP_{Dt}f_0\) in the corresponding direction. On a restarted interval the same substitution uses \(q=D(s-a)\) for initial time \(a\). In the weighted argument choose \(\alpha\) with \(C_N(\alpha)>\lambda\), then make \(\mu_+<C_N(\alpha)\). For the lower comparison choose \(\mu_->\lambda_N\). These are strict inequalities; the full comparison and continuation arguments are deferred to Chapters 8–9.

Time substitution in a boundary integral produces the effective factor \(\lambda c_\pm/D_\pm\). First choose \(\delta\) small enough to keep this factor on the desired side of \(\lambda_N\) when \(M=0\); then use (M) to make \(M\) sufficiently small by choosing \(\tau\). This preserves a strict subcritical or supercritical margin, but does not provide the critical existence argument. Chapters 8 and 9 use these comparisons in detail.

Chapter 5 · Takeaways

Diffusion has spatial scale \(\sqrt h\), with no sharp finite propagation radius. Hölder regularity above one half is sufficient for (M), not necessary. The uniform modulus controls shifts in the moving Neumann kernel and enables comparison while preserving strict coupling margins.

Check your understanding

What is the difference between a finite normalized displacement at a single interval and a finite uniform modulus? Why is (M) unnecessary for the energy method?

The modulus is a supremum over arbitrarily short intervals; the energy estimate uses only a translation at a fixed time.
